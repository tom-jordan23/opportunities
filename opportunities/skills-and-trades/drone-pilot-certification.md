# Drone Pilot Certification (FAA Part 107)

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** FC (Claude Fact Checker)

## Overview

The FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate is your license to fly drones commercially in the United States. The certification itself is straightforward—study for a few weeks, pass a 60-question exam, pay $175. Most people who prepare adequately pass on their first attempt.

Here is the hard truth: Getting certified is the easy part. Building a profitable drone business is genuinely difficult. The market has over 400,000 certified remote pilots as of 2026. Many industries that once outsourced drone work now train their own employees. Entry-level aerial photography—the most obvious gig—has become a race to the bottom on pricing.

For young adults, Part 107 makes sense as a supplemental skill or stepping stone, not necessarily as a standalone career path. Combine it with construction knowledge and you can do site surveys. Pair it with agriculture experience and you can offer crop analysis. The pilots making real money have domain expertise beyond flying—they understand the industries they serve.

The certification costs less than $200 and takes 2-4 weeks of study. Even if the drone business does not work out immediately, the credential does not expire—it just requires free recurrent training every 24 months. Low risk, potential upside.

## What You'll Do

### The Exam Process

You take a 60-question knowledge test at an FAA-approved testing center. You have 2 hours. You need 70% correct (42 questions) to pass.

The test covers:

- **Regulations (22 questions):** Part 107 rules, registration requirements, operating limitations
- **Airspace and Requirements (13 questions):** Airspace classes, reading sectional charts, airport operations
- **Weather (6 questions):** METAR reports, weather effects on flight, density altitude
- **Loading and Performance (4 questions):** Weight and balance, performance calculations
- **Operations (28 questions):** Emergency procedures, crew resource management, maintenance

Reading sectional charts—those complicated aviation maps—trips up the most applicants. You will spend significant study time learning to interpret airspace boundaries, floor and ceiling altitudes, and special use areas.

### As a Commercial Drone Pilot

Daily work varies dramatically depending on your specialization. A few examples:

**Real Estate Photography:** Show up at a property, fly pre-planned routes around the home, capture aerial photos and video. Deliver edited content within 24-48 hours. Jobs last 30 minutes to 2 hours. Pay averages $150-$500 per property.

**Construction Site Mapping:** Fly grid patterns over job sites to create orthomosaic maps and 3D models. Process data through specialized software. Generate progress reports comparing current conditions to design plans. Requires additional training in photogrammetry.

**Infrastructure Inspection:** Capture detailed imagery of roofs, bridges, power lines, cell towers, or solar arrays. Clients use footage to identify damage without putting humans in dangerous positions. Often requires thermal imaging equipment and specialized training.

**Agricultural Services:** Map crop health using multispectral cameras. Identify irrigation problems, pest damage, or nutrient deficiencies across large fields. Requires understanding of agriculture and investment in specialized sensors.

The pattern across all of these: flying is maybe 20% of the job. The rest is client acquisition, scheduling, equipment maintenance, data processing, and delivery.

## Requirements

**Age/Eligibility:**

- Minimum 16 years old
- Able to read, speak, write, and understand English
- Physically and mentally fit to safely fly a drone
- No prior aviation experience needed
- No background check requirement for certification itself—though TSA does a security screening before issuing the certificate

**Skills Needed:**

- Basic understanding of weather and how it affects small aircraft
- Ability to read maps and understand spatial relationships
- Comfort with learning regulations and compliance requirements
- For business purposes: sales ability, customer service, basic marketing

**Physical Requirements:**

- Good vision (correctable is fine) for reading charts and piloting
- Manual dexterity for controller operation
- No medical certificate required—this is a significant difference from manned aviation

**Time Commitment:**

- Study time: 15-40 hours over 2-4 weeks (varies by prior knowledge)
- Exam: 2 hours plus travel to testing center
- Recurrent training: Every 24 months, free online course, takes 2-3 hours

## Training Provided

The FAA does not require formal training. You can self-study using free materials and take the exam whenever you feel ready.

These courses typically include practice exams, video explanations, and pass guarantees. Whether they are worth it depends on your learning style. Many people pass using only free resources.

### What You Will Not Learn

Part 107 covers regulations and airspace—it does not teach you to fly well. You learn:

- Rules for operating commercially
- How to read aviation charts
- Weather interpretation
- Emergency procedures

You do not learn:

- Advanced flight techniques
- Cinematography or photography composition
- Data processing and photogrammetry
- Industry-specific applications
- How to run a business

The actual skills that make money require additional training or experience.

You cannot run a drone business without equipment. Here is what to expect:

**Entry-Level Setup ($2,000-$5,000):**

- DJI Mini 4 Pro or DJI Air 3: $800-$1,100
- Extra batteries (3-4 recommended): $200-$400
- Carrying case: $50-$150
- ND filters: $50-$100
- MicroSD cards: $50-$100
- Editing software (DaVinci Resolve is free, Adobe subscription is $20-$55/month)

**Professional Setup ($10,000-$25,000):**

- DJI Mavic 3 Enterprise or similar: $4,500-$6,000

- Higher insurance limits: $1,000-$2,500/year

Most beginners start with entry-level equipment. Do not buy expensive gear before you have paying clients.

### Insurance Requirements

The FAA does not require insurance. Your clients almost certainly will.

- Most commercial clients require $1-2 million liability coverage
- On-demand/hourly coverage: $5-$15 per flight hour
- Monthly liability: approximately $62/month
- Annual policies: $500-$1,000 for $1 million coverage
- Hull coverage (damage to your drone): 8-12% of drone value annually

### Employment vs. Freelance

**Full-Time Employment:**

- Stable income: $50,000-$90,000 typical for entry to mid-level
- Benefits: health insurance, retirement, paid time off
- Less hustle: someone else finds the clients
- Less flexibility: you work their schedule, their projects
- Fewer openings: most companies hire freelancers, not full-time pilots

**Freelance/Contract:**

- Higher ceiling: no cap on earnings if you can find work
- Higher floor risk: no work means no income
- Full responsibility: marketing, sales, scheduling, invoicing, taxes
- Equipment costs: you buy and maintain everything
- Insurance: you provide your own

**Network Platforms (Middle Ground):**

Companies like FlyGuys, Zeitview (formerly DroneBase), and Dronegenuity connect pilots with clients. You accept missions through their platform.

Pros:

- They handle client acquisition
- Steady stream of potential work
- Good for building experience

Cons:

- Lower rates (they take a cut)
- Less control over pricing
- Competing with thousands of other pilots on the platform

## How to Get Started

### Week 1-2: Study

1. Download the FAA Remote Pilot Study Guide from faa.gov
2. Choose free or paid study resources based on your learning preference
3. Focus extra time on sectional chart reading—this is where most people struggle
4. Take practice tests until you consistently score 80%+ (the buffer matters for test anxiety)

### Week 3: Schedule and Take the Exam

1. Create an IACRA account at iacra.faa.gov (this is your FAA tracking number system)
2. Find a testing center at faa.psiexams.com—most regions have multiple options
3. Schedule your exam—available most weekdays, some weekends
4. Bring government-issued photo ID to the testing center
5. Pass the exam (you will know immediately if you passed)

### Week 4: Complete Certification

1. Return to IACRA and complete FAA Form 8710-13
2. TSA runs a background check (usually clears within 24-48 hours)
3. Print your temporary certificate once approved
4. Permanent plastic certificate arrives by mail in 4-6 weeks

### After Certification: Build Skills

1. Practice flying—a lot. Part 107 does not make you a good pilot.
2. Choose a specialization based on your interests and local market
3. Learn the associated software (video editing, photogrammetry, mapping)
4. Get appropriate insurance before taking any paid work

### Finding Work

1. Join pilot networks: FlyGuys, Zeitview, Dronegenuity, DroneUp
2. Research local demand—what industries in your area need drone services?
3. Build a portfolio—fly free projects if needed to have examples
4. Network with potential clients: real estate agents, contractors, farmers
5. Consider complementary certifications (thermal imaging, mapping)

## The Hard Parts

### The Market is Crowded

Over 400,000 people hold Part 107 certificates. Many never do commercial work, but enough do that competition is fierce—especially in entry-level services like real estate photography. Newcomers undercut established pilots on price. Companies that once hired drone pilots now train their employees in-house.

### "I Have a Drone" is Not a Business

Flying is the smallest part of running a drone services company. You need to:

- Find clients consistently (marketing, networking, cold outreach)
- Price projects profitably (most beginners undercharge significantly)
- Manage client expectations and communication
- Handle scheduling, invoicing, and collections
- Process and deliver content on deadline
- Maintain equipment and stay current on regulations
- Pay self-employment taxes and manage cash flow

Most drone pilots who fail do not fail at flying—they fail at running a business.

### Specialization Requires Investment

The high-paying work—infrastructure inspection, LiDAR mapping, agricultural analysis—requires:

- Expensive equipment ($10,000-$50,000+)
- Additional training and certifications (thermal imaging, photogrammetry)
- Industry knowledge (you need to understand construction, agriculture, or utilities—not just drones)
- Often: established relationships with enterprise clients

You cannot jump straight into this work. It takes years to build the skills and reputation.

### Inconsistent Work

Unless you land a full-time position or large contracts, work comes in waves. Weather cancels shoots. Clients reschedule. Seasonal industries (real estate, agriculture) have busy and slow periods. Many freelancers struggle with income unpredictability.

### Regulatory Complexity

Flying legally requires understanding:

- Airspace restrictions (you cannot fly everywhere)
- LAANC authorization for controlled airspace
- State and local laws (some areas have additional restrictions)
- Property rights and privacy concerns
- Waivers for special operations (night flying, flights over people)

Violating regulations can result in FAA enforcement actions and fines up to $32,666 per incident.

### Equipment Depreciates Fast

Drone technology improves rapidly. The best drone today becomes mid-tier in two years. Clients may expect capabilities your equipment does not have. Budget for regular upgrades.

## Where This Can Lead

### Short-Term (1-2 Years)

- Side income while developing other skills
- Portfolio building for creative work
- Entry point into aviation-related careers
- Additional service offering for existing business (photography, real estate, construction)

### Medium-Term (3-5 Years)

**Specialization Paths:**

- **Mapping and Surveying:** With photogrammetry training, work in construction, mining, or land surveying. Can lead to positions with engineering firms.
- **Inspection Services:** Thermal imaging certification opens doors to roofing, solar, and infrastructure work. Utilities and energy companies hire pilots with this expertise.
- **Agricultural Specialist:** Combine drone skills with agronomy knowledge. Larger farms and ag-tech companies hire for crop analysis.
- **Film and Television:** With cinematography skills, work on productions. Requires networking and a strong demo reel.

### Long-Term

- Operations manager for drone programs at large companies
- Instructor/trainer for new pilots
- Starting a specialized drone services firm
- Transition to manned aviation (some drone pilots pursue traditional pilot certificates)
- Policy and regulatory work as the industry evolves
