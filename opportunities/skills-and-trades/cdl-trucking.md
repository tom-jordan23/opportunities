# CDL / Trucking

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** FC-Claude

## Overview

A Commercial Driver's License (CDL) opens the door to one of the most accessible careers in the United States. Trucking companies are desperate for drivers. Many will pay for your training in exchange for a work commitment. You can go from zero experience to earning $40,000-$60,000 per year within a few months.

For young adults, trucking solves the immediate money problem. Company-sponsored training eliminates upfront costs. Many OTR positions include a truck sleeper cab—your housing is included. The barrier to entry is low: you need a clean driving record, the ability to pass a drug test, and willingness to be away from home for weeks at a time.

The downsides are significant. At 20you can only drive within his state (intrastate) until he turns 21 due to federal regulations. Interstate trucking—the higher-paying, more available work—requires being 21. The lifestyle is hard on your body and your relationships. Loneliness is a real problem. And company-sponsored training comes with contracts that can trap you in bad situations.

This is not a decision to make lightly. But for someone with limited options who wants to start earning immediately, trucking is worth understanding.

## What You'll Do

### Over-the-Road (OTR) Trucking

OTR drivers cover long distances across the country. You might pick up a load in Los Angeles and deliver it to New York, then pick up another load heading to Texas. You live in your truck's sleeper cab for 2-4 weeks at a time before getting home for a few days.

Daily reality:
- Drive 8-11 hours per day (legally limited by Hours of Service regulations)
- Sleep in truck stop parking lots or rest areas
- Pre-trip and post-trip inspections of your vehicle
- Loading and unloading (sometimes—depends on freight type)
- Paperwork and electronic logging
- Finding parking (increasingly difficult—competition for spots is fierce)
- Eating truck stop food or cooking in your cab
- Waiting—at shippers, receivers, terminals, everywhere

### Regional Trucking

Regional drivers stay within a 500-1,000 mile radius of their home base. You cover 4-5 neighboring states and are typically home weekly or on weekends.

The work is similar to OTR but with more predictable routes and better home time. Pay is usually slightly lower than OTR. Regional positions are harder to get as a new driver because companies prefer to give them to experienced drivers.

### Local Trucking

Local drivers are home daily. You pick up and deliver within a metro area or region. The work often involves more loading/unloading and more stops per day.

Local positions almost always require 1-2 years of experience. New CDL holders rarely get local jobs. This is what you work toward, not where you start.

### Specialized Hauling

**Flatbed:** Hauling equipment, lumber, steel—anything that does not fit in an enclosed trailer. Requires securing loads with chains and straps. More physical work. Typically pays 10-15% more than dry van.

**Tanker:** Hauling liquids in tank trailers. Requires a tanker endorsement on your CDL. Some liquids are hazardous materials, requiring additional endorsement.

**Hazmat:** Hauling hazardous materials (fuel, chemicals, explosives). Requires a HazMat endorsement, which involves a TSA background check and fingerprinting. Pays 5-15% more than standard freight.

**Refrigerated (Reefer):** Hauling temperature-controlled freight like food and pharmaceuticals. More monitoring required but similar to dry van otherwise.

## Requirements

**Age:**
- 18 years old minimum for a CDL
- 18-20: Can only drive intrastate (within your state)
- 21+: Can drive interstate (across state lines)
- Most company jobs require interstate driving, so practically speaking, you need to be 21

**Exception:** The FMCSA Safe Driver Apprenticeship Pilot Program allowed some 18-20 year olds to drive interstate under specific conditions. **Note: This program concluded on November 7, 2025.** The American Trucking Associations has applied for an exemption to continue similar arrangements, but as of February 2026, no replacement program exists. Check FMCSA.gov for current status.

**Driving Record:**
- Clean driving record required (no DUIs, no reckless driving, minimal moving violations)
- Some companies have 3-year, 5-year, or 7-year look-back periods
- A serious violation in your past can disqualify you permanently from some companies

**Physical Requirements:**
- Must pass a DOT physical exam ($50-$150, typically every 2 years)
- Vision: 20/40 in each eye (with or without correction)
- Hearing: Ability to hear a forced whisper at 5 feet
- Blood pressure must be within acceptable range
- No disqualifying medical conditions (insulin-dependent diabetes, epilepsy, and certain heart conditions require exemptions)
- Drug test: Marijuana is disqualifying, even in legal states

**Criminal Background:**
- Background check required
- Felony convictions may disqualify you, especially for theft, assault, or drug-related offenses
- HazMat endorsement requires TSA security check—stricter standards

**Time Commitment:**
- CDL training: 3-8 weeks depending on program
- Company training (with a trainer): 4-8 weeks after getting your CDL
- Work schedule: Irregular hours, nights, weekends, holidays
- OTR drivers are away 2-4 weeks at a time

## Training Provided

### Entry-Level Driver Training (ELDT) Requirement

Since February 2022, the FMCSA requires all new CDL applicants to complete training from a provider listed on the Training Provider Registry. This is a federal requirement—you cannot skip it.

ELDT includes:
- Theory/classroom instruction
- Behind-the-wheel training on a range
- Behind-the-wheel training on public roads

There is no federally mandated minimum number of training hours. Completion is based on demonstrating proficiency, not hours logged.

### CDL Classes

**Class A:** Required for driving tractor-trailers (semi-trucks). This is what most trucking jobs require. Allows you to drive vehicles over 26,001 pounds towing trailers over 10,000 pounds.

**Class B:** For straight trucks, buses, and other large single vehicles. Fewer job opportunities than Class A but useful for local delivery, waste management, and bus driving.

Most people pursuing trucking as a career get a Class A CDL.

### Training Options

**Community College Programs**
- Cost: $1,000-$5,000
- Duration: 8 weeks to 6 months
- Pros: Cheaper, more thorough training (often 300+ hours), no employment obligation, better hired by selective companies
- Cons: Takes longer, you pay upfront, may qualify for financial aid

**Private Truck Driving Schools**
- Cost: $3,000-$10,000
- Duration: 3-6 weeks
- Pros: Faster, focused on getting you licensed quickly
- Cons: More expensive, quality varies widely, some are diploma mills

**Company-Sponsored Training**
- Cost: $0 upfront (you sign a contract instead)
- Duration: 2-4 weeks for CDL, then 4-8 weeks with a company trainer
- Pros: No money needed to start, guaranteed job after training
- Cons: You are locked into a contract—leaving early triggers repayment

### Major Companies Offering Sponsored Training

**CRST Expedited**
- Training locations: Cedar Rapids, IA (company school) plus partner schools in Dallas, Riverside CA, Jacksonville FL
- Contract: 10-month commitment minimum
- Training: 2-4 weeks CDL training, 4 days orientation, minimum 28 days/14,000 miles with company trainer
- Penalty for leaving early: Up to $6,500

**Werner Enterprises**
- Partners with Roadmaster Drivers School
- Contract: 1-year employment commitment
- Werner covers tuition in exchange for the commitment

**Swift Transportation**
- Swift Academy provides training
- Contract: Set period of employment required (varies)
- Also offers tuition reimbursement if you trained elsewhere

**Schneider**
- Paid CDL training with guarantee pay program
- Known for better treatment of new drivers compared to some competitors

**Roehl Transport**
- Three-phase paid training program
- $400 hire bonus during onboarding
- Reputation for better training quality

## The Money Reality

### Pay

**First-Year OTR Drivers:**
- Typical range: $40,000-$55,000 annually
- Per-mile pay: $0.30-$0.45 per mile for new drivers
- Some companies offer cents-per-mile, others weekly guarantees or hourly pay

**After 1-2 Years:**
- $55,000-$70,000 annually is common
- Better companies and specialized freight pay more

**Experienced Drivers (3+ years):**
- $65,000-$90,000+ depending on freight type and company
- Owner-operators can earn more but have significant expenses

**Specialized Hauling Premium:**
- Flatbed: 10-15% more than dry van
- Tanker: 5-15% more
- HazMat Tanker: Average $66,555/year vs. $46,673 for no-endorsement drivers

**How Pay Works:**
Most OTR drivers are paid per mile. This means you do not get paid for:
- Waiting at shippers/receivers (some companies pay detention time after a set period)
- Pre-trip and post-trip inspections
- Fueling
- Traffic delays
- Finding parking

Your "payable miles" are always less than the actual miles you drive.

### Housing

**OTR:** Your truck's sleeper cab is your home. This solves the housing cost problem but creates lifestyle challenges. Sleeper cabs have a bed, small fridge, sometimes a microwave, and limited storage.

**Regional/Local:** You need your own housing. These positions do not provide accommodations.

### Startup Costs

**If paying for your own CDL training:**
- CDL school tuition: $1,000-$10,000
- DOT physical exam: $50-$150
- CDL knowledge tests (state fees): $10-$50
- CDL skills test (state fees): $50-$100
- Drug test: Often covered by employer or school
- Total: $1,200-$10,500

**If using company-sponsored training:**
- Nothing upfront
- DOT physical sometimes covered
- You commit your time instead of your money

### Benefits

Benefits vary significantly by company. Larger carriers typically offer:
- Health insurance (often after 30-90 days)
- 401(k) retirement plans
- Paid time off (limited for new drivers)
- Life insurance

Smaller carriers may offer limited or no benefits.

### Hidden Costs

- Food on the road: Truck stop meals are expensive ($10-15 per meal adds up)
- Showers: $12-15 at truck stops (free with fuel loyalty programs)
- Laundry: $3-5 per load at truck stops
- Communication: Cell phone plan for staying connected
- Personal items for the truck: Bedding, cookware, cooler
- Parking tickets and scale violations: Your responsibility

## How to Get Started

### Step 1: Check your eligibility

Before investing time in this path, verify you meet requirements:
- Are you at least 18? (21 for most realistic job options)
- Is your driving record clean for the past 3-5 years?
- Can you pass a drug test? (Stop using marijuana now if applicable—it stays in your system 30+ days)
- Can you pass a DOT physical?

### Step 2: Decide on a training path

**If you are under 21:**
Your options are limited to intrastate work. Consider waiting until 21, or look into intrastate opportunities like local dump truck driving, concrete mixer driving, or bus driving (Class B).

**If you are 21+ and have money saved ($2,000-$5,000):**
Consider community college CDL programs. You will get better training and have no contractual obligation to any company. This gives you more bargaining power when job hunting.

Search for programs at [FMCSA Training Provider Registry](https://tpr.fmcsa.dot.gov).

**If you are 21+ and need to start with zero money:**
Company-sponsored training is your path. Research companies carefully before committing.

### Step 3: Research companies thoroughly (for sponsored training)

Before signing anything:
1. Search "[company name] + reddit" and read r/Truckers discussions
2. Search "[company name] + reviews" on Indeed and Glassdoor
3. Ask: What is the contract length? What are the penalties for leaving early?
4. Ask: What is the starting pay per mile? What is the average weekly mileage?
5. Ask: How long will you be with a trainer? What if you do not get along with your trainer?

### Step 4: Get your Commercial Learner's Permit (CLP)

Before starting CDL school, you need a CLP from your state DMV.

Requirements:
- Pass the CDL knowledge tests (General Knowledge + any endorsements)
- Provide required documents (ID, Social Security card, proof of residence)
- Pay the fee ($3-$50 depending on state)

Study resources:
- [Driving-Tests.org](https://driving-tests.org/cdl-practice-test/) - Free CDL practice tests
- Your state's CDL manual (free download from DMV website)

### Step 5: Complete ELDT-approved training

Enroll in and complete training from a provider on the [FMCSA Training Provider Registry](https://tpr.fmcsa.dot.gov).

### Step 6: Pass the CDL skills test

The test includes:
- Pre-trip inspection (demonstrate you can inspect your vehicle)
- Basic controls (backing, parking, maneuvering)
- Road test (driving on public roads)

### Step 7: Start working

**Company-sponsored route:** You will transition directly into their finishing program, driving with a trainer before going solo.

**Self-paid training route:** Apply to companies. Your options are better without a training contract.

## The Hard Parts

### The age problem

At 20, you cannot legally drive interstate. Most trucking jobs are interstate. This means either:
- Waiting until 21 (most practical option)
- Finding intrastate-only work (limited options, often lower pay)

Note: The FMCSA Safe Driver Apprenticeship Pilot Program ended November 7, 2025. There is currently no active federal program allowing under-21 interstate driving.

### Loneliness is real

Nearly 28% of truckers report struggling with loneliness. Between 22% and 27% report depression. You are alone in a truck cab for 10+ hours a day, weeks at a time. Your social life happens through phone calls and video chats. Relationships strain under this pressure.

This is not an exaggeration or scare tactic. It is the documented reality of the profession. Truck drivers rank 5th among occupations for work-related suicide risk.

### Health deteriorates

More than half of long-haul drivers have two or more major health problems. 80% have at least one serious health condition. Common issues:
- Back pain (50-73% of drivers report low back pain, depending on study)
- Obesity (69% of long-haul truckers are obese vs. 33% of general working adults—per NIOSH)
- Sleep problems (research shows an average of 4.78 hours of sleep per 24-hour period)
- Cardiovascular disease (87% have hypertension or pre-hypertension)
- Diabetes (50% higher prevalence than general population)

The job fights against your health. Staying healthy requires active effort: exercising at truck stops, cooking your own food in the cab, prioritizing sleep. Truck drivers have an average life expectancy 17 years lower than the general population.

### The training contract trap

Company-sponsored training is not free. You pay with a contract—typically 6-24 months.

If you leave before the contract ends:
- You owe the training costs (often $3,500-$9,000)
- Companies send the debt to collections
- Some companies have non-compete clauses—they can sue any company that hires you
- Your credit takes the hit if you cannot pay

**Real example:** One company requires a 10-month commitment for 2 weeks of training. Leave before 10 months? You owe $6,500.

**California update:** As of January 1, 2026, California bans most "stay-or-pay" training contracts. This does not help drivers in other states.

### Home time is limited

OTR drivers typically get:
- 2-4 days home for every 2-3 weeks on the road
- Home time that does not align with weekends or holidays
- Unpaid time off (you only earn when you drive)

Holidays, birthdays, family events—you will miss many of them.

### Pay is not as good as advertised

Advertisements show gross annual earnings. Reality is different:
- Per-mile pay does not include unpaid waiting, inspections, and delays
- "Average" includes experienced drivers; new drivers earn less
- First year earnings are often 20-30% below advertised "potential"

### Parking is a crisis

There are not enough truck parking spots. Every night, thousands of drivers circle truck stops looking for places to sleep legally. Running out of driving hours without finding parking means either:
- Parking illegally and risking tickets
- Continuing to drive illegally and risking your CDL
- Neither option is acceptable, but drivers face this regularly

## Scam Detection: Trucking Industry Warnings

### Lease-Purchase Programs: Avoid Them

The FMCSA Truck Leasing Task Force has called lease-purchase programs "irredeemable tools of fraud." More than 90% of lease-purchase arrangements fail, leaving drivers financially devastated.

**How lease-purchase scams work:**
- Company offers you a truck through a "lease-to-own" program
- You make payments but build no equity
- The company controls your loads, your pay, and your expenses
- Less than 1 in 100 drivers actually end up owning the truck (possibly as low as 1 in 1,000)
- You are responsible for repairs, fuel, insurance—often at inflated prices through the company

**Red flags:**
- Payments based on miles driven (not fixed payments)
- No fixed term, interest rate, or final purchase price
- Being put into a lease-purchase as a brand new driver
- "Become an owner-operator with no money down!"

The Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association (OOIDA) supports legislation to ban these predatory arrangements. Until that passes, protect yourself by avoiding them entirely.

### Predatory Training Schools

Some private CDL schools are barely functional diploma mills.

**Warning signs:**
- Extremely short programs (1-2 weeks is not enough time to learn)
- No hands-on training or minimal behind-the-wheel time
- Guarantees of job placement with high pay
- Pressure to sign up immediately
- Not listed on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry

**Protection:** Only use schools listed on the [Training Provider Registry](https://tpr.fmcsa.dot.gov). Check reviews on TruckersReport.com and Reddit before enrolling.

### Contract Fine Print

Before signing any company training contract:
- Get the full contract in writing before making any commitment
- Look for: contract length, early termination penalty amount, non-compete clauses
- Ask: Does the penalty decrease over time, or is it the full amount regardless of when you leave?
- Ask: What happens if I am fired? Do I still owe the training costs?
- Ask: What if I cannot pass the CDL test?

Companies that refuse to give you the contract to review before committing are not trustworthy.

## Where This Can Lead

### Career Progression

**Years 1-2: Company Driver**
- Build experience and a clean safety record
- Learn the industry, different freight types, and regional differences
- Earn $40,000-$55,000

**Years 2-5: Experienced Driver**
- Qualify for better companies and better freight
- Move to regional or local positions if desired
- Earn $55,000-$80,000
- Can become a company trainer (training new drivers while earning extra)

**Years 5+: Multiple Paths**

*Specialized Hauling:* Tanker, HazMat, oversized loads. Requires additional endorsements and experience. Higher pay for specialized skills.

*Owner-Operator:* Buy or lease your own truck, contract with carriers or find your own freight. Higher earning potential but significant business risk. Requires understanding of business operations, maintenance costs, and insurance. Not recommended without 5+ years of experience and substantial savings.

*Trainer/Instructor:* Train new drivers for companies or CDL schools. Requires clean safety record and teaching ability. Can earn extra while still driving or transition to classroom instruction.

*Fleet Management:* Move into dispatch, safety management, or fleet operations. Requires understanding the business side and often some education or certification.

*Local/Regional:* After proving yourself OTR, you have better options for positions with home time. Local P&D (pickup and delivery), regional runs, dedicated routes.

### Transferable Skills

- CDL is a portable, nationally recognized credential
- Endorsements (HazMat, Tanker, Doubles/Triples) add value
- Logistics and supply chain understanding
- Time management and self-discipline
- Vehicle inspection and basic maintenance knowledge

### Exit Options

If trucking does not work out, your CDL opens doors to:
- Bus driving (transit, school, charter)
- Delivery driving (smaller vehicles)
- Equipment operation (overlaps with Operating Engineers work)
- Logistics and warehouse management
- Sales for trucking-related companies
