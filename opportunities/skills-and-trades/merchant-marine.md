# Merchant Marine - Entry Level

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** Claude Code (Haiku)

## Overview

The merchant marine offers one of the least-known paths to a stable, well-paying career without college. You work on commercial ships—container vessels, tankers, tugboats, ferries—moving cargo and passengers. Entry-level positions start at $40,000-$70,000 annually. Experienced officers can earn $140,000-$200,000 or more. Housing and meals are included while aboard ship.

For young adults, the merchant marine has a remarkable advantage: the Seafarers International Union (SIU) operates the Paul Hall Center in Piney Point, Maryland, which provides free entry-level training with room and board included. You pay approximately $1,500 for credentials and documents. In exchange, you get a guaranteed first job aboard an SIU-contracted vessel.

The Jones Act protects American mariners. Federal law requires that ships moving cargo between U.S. ports must be crewed by American citizens. This creates ongoing demand for qualified U.S. merchant mariners that foreign workers cannot fill.

The downsides are significant and specific to this career. You will be away from home for months at a time. A typical rotation might be 60-120 days at sea followed by an equivalent period ashore. You live in confined spaces with the same small group of people. The hierarchy is strict—this is not a casual workplace. Drug testing is regular and rigorous. A single failed test ends your career.

This is a path that rewards patience and discipline. Entry-level work is unglamorous—cleaning, painting, standing watch, following orders. But the progression is clear: ordinary seaman to able-bodied seaman to bosun, or through the engine or steward departments. With enough sea time, you can test for officer licenses and eventually command your own vessel.

## What You'll Do

The merchant marine has three departments: Deck, Engine, and Steward. Your choice determines your career path.

### Deck Department - Entry Level

**Ordinary Seaman (OS):**

You are the junior member of the deck crew. Your work involves:

- Standing watch on the bridge under supervision (looking for hazards, monitoring instruments)
- Deck maintenance: chipping rust, painting, cleaning
- Cargo handling assistance when loading and unloading
- Line handling when docking and undocking
- Basic seamanship tasks assigned by able-bodied seamen and officers
- Safety drills and emergency station duties

The work is physical. You chip paint in the sun. You stand 4-hour watches at all hours. You take orders from everyone above you. There is no glamour here—this is learning the trade from the bottom.

### Engine Department - Entry Level

**Wiper:**

You are the entry point to the engineering side. Your work involves:

- Cleaning machinery spaces (engine room, pump rooms)
- Wiping down equipment and bilges
- Assisting engineers with maintenance tasks
- Learning to operate and monitor machinery
- Standing engine room watches under supervision
- Basic maintenance: greasing, oiling, filter changes

Engine work is hot, noisy, and confined. You work in spaces with running machinery at all hours. The pay tends to be slightly higher than deck positions at the same experience level.

### Steward Department - Entry Level

**Steward's Assistant / Galley Utility:**

You support the ship's food service operations. Your work involves:

- Cleaning galley, mess areas, and living quarters
- Assisting cooks with food preparation
- Setting tables and serving meals
- Dishwashing and kitchen sanitation
- Inventory and food storage
- Laundry operations

This is the smallest department but offers a different lifestyle. You work regular hours in climate-controlled spaces. The progression leads to ship's cook and chief steward positions.

### Life Aboard Ship

**The Daily Schedule:**

Ships run 24 hours. Watchstanders work in shifts—typically 4 hours on, 8 hours off. Day workers (maintenance crews, galley staff) work more conventional hours. You eat in the crew mess. Your cabin is small but private on most modern vessels. Larger ships have recreation areas, gyms, and internet access. Smaller vessels have fewer amenities.

**The Rotation:**

Time aboard varies by vessel type and company:

- Container ships and tankers: 60-120 days on, 60-120 days off is common
- Military Sealift Command (MSC): Initial assignments are 4 months, with up to 6 months authorized per year
- Offshore supply vessels: 28-42 days on, 14-21 days off
- Ferries and coastal vessels: Often daily schedules, home every night
- Tugboats: Varies widely—some are 2 weeks on/2 weeks off

**The Reality:**

When you are aboard, the ship is your entire world. You see the same dozen to thirty people every day. Personal conflicts become difficult to escape. Internet access has improved but is often slow and limited. You miss weddings, birthdays, holidays, and emergencies at home. Some people find this liberating. Others find it unbearable.

## Requirements

**Age and Citizenship:**

- Must be 18 years old minimum
- Must be a U.S. citizen or permanent resident for most positions
- Jones Act vessels require the master, all officers, and 75% of crew to be U.S. citizens

**Required Credentials (Before You Can Work):**

Three documents are required for any commercial maritime work:

1. **Transportation Worker Identification Credential (TWIC):**
   - Cost: $124 (as of January 2025)
   - Issued by TSA
   - Valid for 5 years
   - Requires background check and fingerprinting
   - Apply at a TSA enrollment center

2. **Merchant Mariner Credential (MMC):**
   - Cost: $150 for initial issuance
   - Issued by U.S. Coast Guard National Maritime Center
   - Valid for 5 years
   - Requires drug test, physical exam, and background check
   - Contains your endorsements (the specific jobs you are qualified to do)

3. **STCW Basic Safety Training (BST):**
   - Cost: $695-$1,000 if paid separately
   - Required for all working seafarers
   - Covers personal survival, firefighting, first aid, and security awareness
   - Must be refreshed every 5 years

**Physical Requirements:**

You must pass a Coast Guard medical examination. Requirements include:

- Vision: 20/800 minimum in each eye, correctable to 20/40 (deck) or 20/50 (engine)
- Hearing: Ability to hear a forced whisper at 5 feet
- Blood pressure within acceptable limits
- No disqualifying medical conditions (waivers available for some conditions)
- Physical ability to perform duties—climbing ladders, lifting, working in confined spaces

**Drug Testing:**

This is absolute. The maritime industry follows DOT drug testing requirements.

- Pre-employment drug test required
- Random testing throughout your career
- Post-accident testing after any incident
- The 5-panel test screens for marijuana, cocaine, amphetamines, opioids, and PCP
- Marijuana use disqualifies you, regardless of state legalization
- A failed test is reported to the Coast Guard and results in credential suspension or revocation
- You cannot hide it, work around it, or get a second chance easily

**Background Check:**

- Criminal history check for MMC
- TSA security threat assessment for TWIC
- Some convictions are disqualifying; others may require explanation or waiver
- Cannot be on probation or parole for most training programs

## Training Provided

### Path 1: SIU Paul Hall Center (Free Training)

The Seafarers International Union operates the Paul Hall Center for Maritime Training and Education in Piney Point, Maryland. This is the largest training facility for merchant mariners in the United States.

**Unlicensed Apprentice Program:**

- Duration: Approximately one year (combined classroom and shipboard phases)
- Cost: $0 for tuition, room, and board
- You pay: Approximately $1,500 for credentials, uniforms, physical exam, drug test, and documents

**Program Structure:**

- **Phase 1:** 16 weeks of entry-level training at the school
- **Phase 2:** 60+ days of shipboard training as an apprentice
- **Phase 3:** 120 days at sea in an entry-level paid position
- **Phase 4:** Return to school for department-specific advanced training

**What You Get:**

- STCW Basic Safety Training
- Merchant Mariner Credential with entry-level endorsements
- Guaranteed first job aboard an SIU-contracted vessel
- Meals and housing during training
- Healthcare benefits through the Seafarers Health and Benefits Plan

**Requirements for Admission:**

- High school diploma or GED
- Able to obtain USCG medical certificate
- Able to obtain TWIC
- Not on probation or parole
- Pass physical exam, drug test, and selection interview
- Demonstrate discipline and fitness for a maritime career

**How to Apply:**

1. Contact an SIU hiring hall or Student Services at 301-994-0010
2. Schedule an interview and take reading and math tests at an SIU hall
3. Submit application to selection committee
4. If accepted, receive report date for training

**The Catch:**

There is not really a catch—this is a union training program designed to produce union members who will work aboard union-contracted vessels. You are expected to work SIU jobs after graduation. The union benefits from having trained workers; you benefit from free training and guaranteed employment. It is a mutually beneficial arrangement.

### Path 2: Military Sealift Command (Federal Employment)

Military Sealift Command (MSC) operates the civilian-crewed ships of the U.S. Navy. As a Civil Service Mariner (CIVMAR), you are a federal employee.

**Entry-Level Positions:**

- Supply Utility
- Wiper
- Ordinary Seaman
- Steward Department positions

**Pay and Benefits:**

- Entry-level: $50,000-$70,000+ annually
- Federal employee benefits: health insurance, retirement, paid leave
- Room and meals provided aboard ship
- No union dues

**Requirements:**

- U.S. citizenship required
- Must obtain security clearance
- Same credential requirements as commercial maritime (TWIC, MMC, STCW)
- Must meet MSC medical standards

**Application Process:**

Contact the CIVMAR Support Center at 866-JOBSMSC (562-7672) or visit sealiftcommand.com.

### Path 3: Other Maritime Unions

**Sailors' Union of the Pacific (SUP):**

- West Coast focus
- Unlicensed deck, engine, and steward positions
- Hiring hall system in San Francisco, Wilmington, Seattle, Honolulu
- Affiliated with SIU

**Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association (MEBA):**

- Represents licensed marine engineers
- Requires engineer officer license
- Not an entry-level path

**American Maritime Officers (AMO):**

- Represents licensed deck officers
- Requires officer license
- Not an entry-level path

**Masters, Mates & Pilots (MM&P):**

- Represents licensed deck officers
- Requires officer license
- Not an entry-level path

For entry-level, the SIU Paul Hall Center is your best option. Other unions primarily serve licensed officers or require existing credentials.

### Path 4: Pay-Your-Own Training

If you want to enter the industry without committing to a union, you can pay for training yourself.

**STCW Basic Safety Training Costs:**

- $695-$1,000 at private maritime schools
- Available at schools in coastal cities (Fort Lauderdale, San Diego, Seattle)
- 5-day course covering survival, firefighting, first aid, and security

**Entry-Level MMC Endorsements:**

After completing STCW-BST, you can apply for entry-level endorsements:
- Ordinary Seaman
- Wiper
- Steward's Assistant

You then find work through maritime job boards, company applications, or union hiring halls (if you join later).

### Housing

When aboard ship, housing and meals are provided at no cost. This is a significant financial advantage—you have no rent, no grocery bills, no utility costs while working.

When ashore, you need your own housing. This is your responsibility and expense. Some mariners maintain apartments or homes. Others stay with family or minimize expenses during time off. Your ashore time is unpaid unless you have vacation benefits.

## How to Get Started

### Step 1: Verify Eligibility

Before pursuing this path, confirm you meet basic requirements:

- Are you 18 or older?
- Are you a U.S. citizen or permanent resident?
- Do you have a high school diploma or GED?
- Can you pass a drug test? (If you use marijuana, stop now—it can take 30+ days to clear your system)
- Is your criminal record clear of disqualifying offenses?
- Are you willing to be away from home for months at a time?
- Can you work in confined spaces and handle physical labor?

### Step 2: Get Your TWIC Card

Apply before anything else. The TWIC is required to even enter most maritime facilities.

1. Go to [tsa.gov/twic](https://www.tsa.gov/twic) and find an enrollment center
2. Schedule an appointment
3. Bring required documents (proof of identity, citizenship/immigration status)
4. Pay the $124 fee
5. Get fingerprinted and photographed
6. Card arrives by mail in 7-10 business days (can take longer)

### Step 3: Choose Your Training Path

**If you have minimal funds (under $2,000):**

The SIU Paul Hall Center is your best option. You need approximately $1,500 for credentials and documents, but no tuition.

1. Find your nearest SIU hiring hall at [seafarers.org](https://www.seafarers.org)
2. Contact them or call Student Services at 301-994-0010
3. Schedule an interview and testing appointment
4. Complete the application process
5. If accepted, you will receive a report date

**If you can afford to pay for training:**

1. Complete STCW-BST at a maritime training center
2. Apply for your MMC with entry-level endorsements
3. Look for work through job boards, company websites, or union halls

### Step 4: Complete Required Medical and Documentation

For the MMC application, you need:

- Completed CG-719K medical exam form from an approved medical provider
- Proof of valid drug test (within 6 months)
- Evidence of citizenship or permanent residency
- Passport-style photographs

Submit your application to the National Maritime Center. Processing takes 4-6 weeks typically.

### Step 5: Complete Training

Whether at Paul Hall Center or elsewhere, complete your required training. For Paul Hall Center students, this includes phases of classroom and shipboard training.

### Step 6: Find Your First Job

**SIU graduates:** Your first job is guaranteed aboard an SIU-contracted vessel.

**Others:** Use these resources:
- Maritime job boards (maritimejobs.com, indeed.com)
- Company websites directly
- Union hiring halls if you join a union
- Military Sealift Command if pursuing federal employment

## The Hard Parts

### Months Away from Home

This is not like trucking where you might be home every few weeks. Deep-sea voyages can keep you aboard for 90-120 days straight. You miss birthdays, holidays, and emergencies. Relationships strain under this pressure. Many mariners are divorced or struggle to maintain partnerships. This lifestyle works for some people. It breaks others.

### Confined Spaces and Small Crews

Your cabin is small. The ship is your entire world. You see the same 15-30 people every single day for months. If you do not get along with someone, you cannot escape them. Personality conflicts become serious problems. The work environment tolerates less individual expression than most jobs—the hierarchy matters, and you follow orders.

### The Hierarchy Is Real

Merchant ships operate on strict chains of command. As an entry-level rating, you are at the bottom. You take orders. You do not question them in the moment. The captain's authority is nearly absolute at sea. Some people thrive in this structure. Others find it oppressive. Know which type you are before committing.

### Physical and Mental Health Challenges

Seafarers face elevated risks:

- Isolation and loneliness are documented problems
- Limited access to healthcare while at sea
- In emergencies, you may be days from a hospital
- Sleep can be disrupted by watch schedules
- Exercise and healthy eating require active effort

### Drug Testing Is Absolute

There is no tolerance. A single failed drug test goes on your record with the Coast Guard. Your credential gets suspended or revoked. Your career may be over. Marijuana is tested regardless of state legalization. If you cannot commit to complete abstinence from drugs, this career is not for you.

### Getting Started Takes Time

Even with Paul Hall Center's free training, the process takes time:

- TWIC application and processing: 2-4 weeks
- Training program: Several months to a year
- Building sea time for advancement: Years

This is not a quick fix for immediate income needs. The payoff comes later.

### The Industry Can Be Unstable

Shipping is cyclical. Economic downturns reduce cargo volumes and shipping demand. Being a junior member of a union hiring hall means you ship last when work is scarce. The Jones Act protects American mariners, but the U.S. flag fleet is much smaller than it once was.

## Where This Can Lead

### Career Progression - Deck Department

The traditional path for deck ratings:

1. **Ordinary Seaman (OS):** Entry level. Learn the basics of seamanship.
2. **Able Bodied Seaman (AB):** After 180-360 days of sea time (depending on endorsement level). More responsibility, higher pay.
3. **Bosun (Boatswain):** Senior unlicensed deck position. Supervises deck crew, reports to Chief Mate.

### The Hawsepipe: Rating to Officer

You can become a licensed deck officer without attending a maritime academy. This is called "coming up through the hawsepipe."

**Requirements for Third Mate License:**

- 1,080 days of sea time as a rating
- Complete required training courses
- Pass Coast Guard license examination

This takes years but creates officers with deep practical experience. The license examination is challenging—many people take prep courses.

### Career Progression - Engine Department

1. **Wiper:** Entry level. Cleaning and assisting.
2. **QMED (Qualified Member of Engine Department):** After training and sea time. Multiple specialty ratings (fireman/watertender, oiler, electrician, pumpman, etc.).
3. **Junior Engineer/Designated Duty Engineer:** Senior unlicensed or entry licensed positions.

Engine officers can also come up through the hawsepipe with appropriate sea time and examination.

### Types of Vessels and Lifestyle Differences

**Container Ships:**
- Largest modern cargo vessels
- Long voyages (60-120 days)
- Small crews (20-25)
- Highly automated
- Best pay typically

**Tankers:**
- Carry oil, chemicals, or liquefied gas
- Additional certifications required for some
- Higher pay due to hazardous cargo
- Strict safety protocols

**Tugboats:**
- Harbor and coastal work
- Shorter rotations (often 2 weeks on/2 weeks off)
- Smaller crews (3-8)
- More home time than deep-sea

**Ferries:**
- Often home daily
- Passenger service orientation
- Lower pay than deep-sea typically
- Better work-life balance

**Offshore Supply Vessels:**
- Support oil platforms and offshore operations
- 28-42 days on, 14-21 days off
- Gulf of Mexico is primary U.S. location
- Boom and bust with oil industry

**Military Sealift Command:**
- Government vessels supporting Navy operations
- Federal employment with benefits
- May deploy to conflict areas
- 4-6 month assignments

### The Jones Act and Job Security

The Merchant Marine Act of 1920 (Jones Act) requires that cargo shipped between U.S. ports must travel on ships that are:

- U.S.-built
- U.S.-owned (75% citizen ownership)
- U.S.-flagged
- Crewed by U.S. citizens (75% of crew, 100% of officers)

This protects American mariners from foreign competition for domestic shipping jobs. It is why U.S. merchant mariners can earn significantly more than foreign mariners doing similar work. The Jones Act has critics who argue it raises shipping costs, but it has survived repeated attempts at repeal. For now, it provides meaningful job protection.
