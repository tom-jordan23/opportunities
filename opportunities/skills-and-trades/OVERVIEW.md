# Skills and Trades: Category Overview

This document covers common elements across skills and trades training paths. Individual opportunity documents in this category will reference this overview for shared context.

---

## Why Trades and Skills Training

### Alternative to College Debt

The average four-year degree costs $100,000+ and takes four years of not earning. Trade training flips this model. You earn while you learn, often with zero upfront cost if you go the apprenticeship route.

By age 24, a union electrician apprentice who started at 20 has four years of paid experience and a journeyman license. A college graduate has four years of debt payments ahead and is just starting their first job.

This math works especially well for young adults. He has limited funds and wants to start now. Trades let him do both.

### Earning While Learning Model

Most formal trade training pays you from day one. Apprenticeship programs are jobs with built-in education, not schools with optional work. You clock in, learn on the jobsite, attend classes, and collect a paycheck.

This matters for someone without a financial safety net. You build skills without borrowing money. You gain work experience that counts on a resume. You contribute to real projects while learning.

### Long-Term Earning Potential

Journeyman electricians earn $55,000-$75,000 annually. Journeyman plumbers average around $75,700 per year. HVAC technicians earn $54,000-$58,000 at the journeyman level. These figures are for employees, not business owners.

Master tradespeople who start their own businesses can earn significantly more. A licensed contractor with a few employees and a solid reputation can clear six figures. The ceiling is high for people willing to build their own operation.

These numbers compare favorably to many four-year degree holders, especially when you factor in zero student debt and four years of earning instead of paying.

## Types of Training Paths

### Union Apprenticeships

Union apprenticeships are the gold standard for trades training. The union, employers, and often the Department of Labor oversee program quality. You get structured training, guaranteed wage progression, and a nationally recognized credential at the end.

**How they work:**
- Apply directly to a local union or joint apprenticeship training committee (JATC)
- If accepted, you become an employee of a union contractor from day one
- Training lasts 4-5 years depending on the trade
- You split time between jobsite work and classroom instruction
- Pay increases on a set schedule as your skills develop

**Pay structure:**
Starting pay is typically 40-60% of the journeyman wage. Every six months to a year, your percentage increases. By the end of apprenticeship, you are earning 80-90% of journeyman rate. After passing your journeyman exam, you earn full scale.

Example: If journeyman electricians in your area earn $45/hour, you might start at $18-$22/hour and work up to $40/hour by your fourth year. After passing the journeyman exam, you hit full scale.

**Benefits:**
Union apprentices typically get health insurance, pension contributions, and other benefits from day one. This is unusual in entry-level positions.

**Downsides:**
Programs are competitive. Some have waiting lists. You go where the work is, which may mean different jobsites every few months. The union controls job assignments during apprenticeship.

### Employer-Sponsored Training

Many non-union employers offer their own training programs. Large companies in HVAC, electrical, plumbing, and other trades hire entry-level workers and train them on the job.

**How they work:**
- Apply for an entry-level position at a trade company
- Start as a helper or trainee
- Learn from experienced technicians on real jobs
- May include structured classroom training or may be purely on-the-job
- Company may pay for certifications and licensing exams

**Advantages:**
Often easier to get into than union programs. Less formal application process. You can start immediately if hired.

**Disadvantages:**
Quality varies wildly. Some companies provide excellent training. Others use you as cheap labor and teach you nothing. Pay progression is not guaranteed. Benefits depend entirely on the employer.

**How to evaluate:**
Ask current employees about the training quality. Look at Glassdoor reviews. Ask specifically: "What certifications will I earn? Who pays for licensing exams? What does the training timeline look like?"

### Vocational Schools and Certifications

Trade schools, community colleges, and technical institutes offer programs in most trades. These range from a few months to two years.

**What they provide:**
- Classroom instruction and hands-on practice
- Industry certifications
- Some job placement assistance
- A credential to put on a resume

**Cost reality:**
Programs range from $1,000 for a basic certificate to $15,000+ for comprehensive programs. Community colleges are usually cheapest. Private trade schools cost more and are not always better.

**When they make sense:**
- You want to get basic skills before applying to apprenticeships
- Your local job market values certificates for entry-level positions
- The program leads to a specific, valuable certification (HVAC EPA certification, welding AWS certification, etc.)

**When they don't:**
- You can get into a paid apprenticeship instead
- The program costs more than a year of apprentice wages
- The certificate is not recognized by employers in your area

Research carefully. Some vocational programs are excellent. Others are expensive pipelines to mediocre jobs. See the scam detection section below.

### Self-Taught Paths

Some fields, especially in technology, allow people to learn on their own and build portfolios that demonstrate competence.

**Where this works:**
- Software development and coding
- Web design and development
- IT support and system administration
- Video editing and content creation
- Some aspects of renewable energy installation

**Where this doesn't work:**
- Licensed trades (electrical, plumbing, HVAC) require formal apprenticeship or schooling in most states
- Safety-critical fields require certified training
- Anywhere licensing laws mandate specific credentials

**Reality check:**
Self-taught paths require unusual self-discipline. Most people who start learning to code on their own do not finish. Having skin in the game, whether through a paid program or a job that demands results, helps most people follow through.

## Financial Realities

### Apprenticeship Pay Progression

Union apprenticeships follow a predictable pay scale. You start at a percentage of journeyman wages and increase over time.

**Typical progression:**

| Year | Percentage of Journeyman Wage |
|------|-------------------------------|
| 1st  | 40-50%                        |
| 2nd  | 50-60%                        |
| 3rd  | 60-70%                        |
| 4th  | 70-80%                        |
| 5th  | 80-90%                        |

After completing your apprenticeship and passing the journeyman exam, you earn full journeyman scale.

**Concrete example:**
In Chicago, laborers' apprentices earn 60% of journey worker rate in their first period, progressing to 70%, 80%, and 90% as they advance. In New York City construction, apprentices progress through 50%, 60%, 70%, and 80% of journeyperson wages.

Non-union employer training programs vary. Some mirror the union model. Others start you at minimum wage and give raises based on their own judgment.

### Certification Costs by Category

**Electrician training:**
- Trade school programs: $1,000-$11,000 for certificates, $11,000+ for associate degrees
- Tools and books: $500-$2,500
- Licensing exam fees: $50-$500 depending on state
- Union apprenticeship: Free (you are paid to train)

**HVAC training:**
- EPA 608 certification: $150-$200 for exam
- Trade school programs: $1,500-$15,000
- Tools: $300-$1,000 starting kit
- Union apprenticeship: Free

**Plumbing:**
- Trade school programs: $1,500-$15,000
- Tools: $500-$1,500 starting kit
- Licensing exam fees: $50-$300
- Union apprenticeship: Free

**Welding:**
- Trade school programs: $5,000-$20,000
- Certifications (AWS): $50-$300 per certification
- Equipment for practice: Varies widely

**CDL (Commercial Driver's License):**
- CDL school: $3,000-$7,000 at independent schools
- Company-sponsored training: Free, but read the contract carefully
- Testing fees: $100-$300

### Programs That Pay You vs. You Pay Them

**They pay you:**
- Registered apprenticeship programs
- Most union training
- Employer on-the-job training
- Some pre-apprenticeship programs (often include a training stipend)

**You pay them:**
- Trade schools and vocational programs
- Private certification courses
- Most coding bootcamps
- Independent CDL schools

**It's complicated:**
- Company-sponsored CDL training is "free" but locks you into a contract
- Some bootcamps have income share agreements (you pay a percentage of future earnings)
- Pre-apprenticeship programs are sometimes free, sometimes not

### Return on Investment Timelines

**Fastest payback:**
- Pre-apprenticeship programs (free training, direct placement into paid apprenticeship): Immediate
- Registered apprenticeships: Immediate positive cash flow, journeyman wages in 4-5 years

**Medium timeline:**
- Trade school + apprenticeship: Recover tuition costs in 1-3 years
- CDL training: 6-12 months to recover tuition if you paid out of pocket

**Longest payback:**
- Private trade schools charging $15,000+: May take 2-4 years to recover costs versus going straight into an apprenticeship
- Coding bootcamps: 1-3 years to recover costs if you land a job at median salary

## Common Requirements

### Physical Demands

Trades work is physical work. Be honest with yourself about this.

**What to expect:**
- Standing and walking 8-12 hours per day
- Lifting 50+ pounds regularly, sometimes 100+ with assistance
- Working on ladders, scaffolding, rooftops, and in confined spaces
- Kneeling, crouching, and crawling
- Working outdoors in heat, cold, rain, and wind
- Exposure to dust, noise, and fumes (with proper protective equipment)

**Trades with heaviest physical demands:**
- Ironwork
- Roofing
- Concrete work
- Demolition
- Plumbing (lots of working in awkward positions)

**Trades with lighter physical demands:**
- Electrical (still physical, but less heavy lifting)
- HVAC (moderate)
- Instrumentation and controls

If you have physical limitations, some trades are more accessible than others. Be upfront during the application process. Some apprenticeship programs have physical agility tests.

### Driver's License Requirements

Most trades require a valid driver's license. You need to get to jobsites, which change frequently. Employers and unions expect you to have reliable transportation.

Some trades require a commercial driver's license (CDL) for specific work. Operating certain equipment or driving work trucks over a weight limit requires a CDL.

If you do not have a driver's license, get one before applying. This is not negotiable for most programs.

### Drug Testing Norms

Drug testing is standard in the trades. Construction sites often require it as a condition of access.

**What to expect:**
- Pre-employment drug test during application process
- Random drug testing throughout employment
- Post-accident drug testing
- Testing if impairment is suspected

**Important:** Marijuana remains federally illegal and banned in most construction environments, even in states where it is legal recreationally. Testing positive for marijuana will disqualify you from most apprenticeship programs and jobsites.

If you use marijuana, stop well before applying. Detection windows vary, but 30+ days is common for regular users.

### Background Check Considerations

Background checks are common but not automatic disqualifiers for all programs.

**What programs check:**
- Criminal history
- Driving record
- Employment history verification
- Sometimes credit history for positions involving financial responsibility

**How criminal history affects you:**
Different programs have different policies. Some cannot hire people with certain convictions. Others evaluate on a case-by-case basis. Violent crimes and theft are usually the most problematic. The further in the past the conviction, the better your chances.

**What to do if you have a record:**
- Be honest on applications
- Ask directly about the program's policy before investing time in applying
- Look for programs specifically designed for people with records
- Pre-apprenticeship programs sometimes help connect people with flexible employers

### Math and Technical Aptitude Expectations

Trades require more math than people expect. You will use basic algebra, geometry, and measurement constantly.

**Math you need:**
- Fractions and decimals
- Basic algebra (solving for unknowns)
- Geometry (area, volume, angles)
- Reading and using formulas
- Unit conversions
- Reading technical drawings and blueprints

**Aptitude testing:**
Many apprenticeship programs require aptitude tests during application. These typically cover math, reading comprehension, and mechanical reasoning.

**If your math is weak:**
Fix this before applying. Free resources exist. Khan Academy covers all the math you need. Many pre-apprenticeship programs include math refreshers.

Do not skip this. Failing the aptitude test ends your application. Passing the aptitude test but struggling with math on the job makes everything harder.

## How to Get Started Without Connections

### Finding Apprenticeship Programs

You do not need to know someone. Registered apprenticeship programs have formal application processes open to anyone who meets the requirements.

**Step 1: Use Apprenticeship.gov**
Go to [Apprenticeship.gov](https://www.apprenticeship.gov/apprenticeship-job-finder) and use the Apprenticeship Job Finder. Filter by occupation and location. This shows registered programs near you.

**Step 2: Check state labor department websites**
Each state has an apprenticeship agency or office. They list approved programs and often have additional resources. Search "[your state] apprenticeship programs" to find yours.

**Step 3: Contact local unions directly**
For union apprenticeships, find your local union's website. Search for "[trade] union [your city]" or "[trade] local [your city]." The website will list application windows and requirements.

**Step 4: Apply to multiple programs**
Programs have limited spots and specific application windows. Applying to one program is not enough. Apply to every relevant program you qualify for.

### Union Hall Processes

Union apprenticeship applications typically work like this:

1. **Application window opens.** Many programs accept applications only during specific periods, sometimes just a few days per year. Check websites frequently or call to ask when applications open.

2. **Submit application.** Usually online or in person. You provide basic information, proof of age, proof of education (high school diploma or GED), and sometimes letters of recommendation.

3. **Aptitude testing.** If your application meets basic requirements, you are scheduled for aptitude tests. These cover math, reading, and mechanical reasoning.

4. **Interview.** Scored interview with a committee. They ask about your interest in the trade, work ethic, and reliability. Dress professionally. Show up early.

5. **Ranking and selection.** You receive a score based on your test results and interview. Programs select from the top of the ranked list as positions open.

6. **Waiting.** Even if you rank well, you may wait months before being called. Stay in contact. Keep your phone number updated with the program.

### Pre-Apprenticeship Programs

Pre-apprenticeship programs prepare you for apprenticeship applications. They teach basic skills, help with math and aptitude test prep, and often have direct connections to apprenticeship programs.

**What they offer:**
- Introduction to tools and safety
- Math and blueprint reading basics
- Physical conditioning
- Soft skills training
- Direct referrals or preferred entry to apprenticeship programs
- Sometimes a training stipend

**Where to find them:**
- Community colleges
- Workforce development boards
- Nonprofit organizations focused on trades training
- Union training centers

**Notable free programs:**
- Building Pathways (Massachusetts)
- ANEW (Seattle area)
- The Master's Apprentice (Colorado)
- Long Beach City College Construction Pre-Apprenticeship (California)
- Many local workforce development programs

Pre-apprenticeship is especially valuable if you are not sure which trade you want, need to brush up on math, or want preferred entry into competitive programs.

### Entry-Level Positions That Lead to Training

If you cannot get into an apprenticeship immediately, entry-level positions in the trades can be a stepping stone.

**Positions to look for:**
- Helper or laborer for trade companies
- Material handler at supply houses
- Warehouse work for electrical, plumbing, or HVAC distributors
- Maintenance assistant for property management companies

**How this helps:**
- Exposure to the trade
- Learning basic terminology and tools
- Connections with tradespeople who can recommend you
- Something to put on your apprenticeship application

**Warning:**
Some "helper" positions are dead ends. Ask directly: "Is there a path to apprenticeship or training here?" If the answer is vague, the position probably will not lead anywhere.

## Scam Patterns in Trades Training

Young people entering trades training are targets for scams. Know the patterns.

### Predatory Trucking Company Contracts

Trucking is notorious for this. Companies offer "free" CDL training, then lock you into contracts with punishing terms.

**How it works:**
- Company offers free CDL school
- You sign a contract requiring you to work for them for 1-2 years
- If you leave early, you owe the full cost of training ($3,000-$7,500 or more)
- Working conditions are poor: low pay per mile, long routes, minimal home time
- The contract makes it financially impossible to leave

**Red flags:**
- Training "cost" is inflated far above market rate
- Contract requires you to pay back training if you leave for any reason
- Company has high turnover and constant recruiting
- Online reviews mention driver exploitation

**Better alternatives:**
- Pay for independent CDL school if you can ($3,000-$5,000)
- Look for union trucking apprenticeships
- Research community college CDL programs
- If you must use company training, compare contracts carefully

### Worthless Certification Mills

Some schools sell certificates that mean nothing to employers.

**How to identify them:**
- No accreditation from recognized bodies
- Promises of high-paying jobs without evidence
- Extremely short programs for complex trades
- Pressure to enroll immediately
- Recruiting via social media ads targeting desperate job seekers

**How to verify a program:**
- Check accreditation with the U.S. Department of Education database
- Ask local employers if they recognize graduates from this program
- Search "[school name] scam" and "[school name] reddit"
- Compare program length to apprenticeship requirements in your state

### "Guaranteed Job Placement" Lies

No training program can guarantee you a job. Anyone who says otherwise is lying.

**What legitimate programs say:**
- "X% of our graduates find employment within 6 months"
- "We have connections with local employers"
- "We provide job placement assistance"

**What scams say:**
- "Guaranteed job placement"
- "You will definitely earn $X after graduating"
- "Every graduate gets a job"

Ask for evidence. Legitimate programs can show you outcomes data. Scams get evasive when you ask for specifics.

### How to Verify Legitimate Programs

**Check for registration:**
Legitimate apprenticeship programs are registered with the Department of Labor or your state apprenticeship agency. You can verify this on Apprenticeship.gov.

**Check accreditation:**
Trade schools should be accredited by recognized accrediting bodies. Verify claims directly with the accreditor.

**Search for complaints:**
- "[Program name] scam"
- "[Program name] reddit"
- "[Program name] complaints"
- Better Business Bureau (with appropriate skepticism)

**Talk to graduates:**
Ask the program for contact information for recent graduates. If they refuse or cannot provide it, that is a problem.

**Read the contract:**
Before signing anything, read every word. Look for:
- Hidden fees
- Repayment requirements if you leave
- Non-compete clauses
- Binding arbitration clauses

## Career Progression

### Apprentice to Journeyman to Master

Most trades follow a three-tier progression:

**Apprentice (4-5 years):**
- Learning on the job under supervision
- Attending classroom instruction
- Cannot work unsupervised
- Pay increases on a schedule

**Journeyman (indefinitely, typically 2-4+ years before master eligibility):**
- Licensed to work independently
- Can supervise apprentices
- Full trade wages
- Portable credential that works anywhere

**Master (after meeting experience requirements and passing exam):**
- Can own and operate a trade business
- Can pull permits for work
- Can supervise journeymen and apprentices
- Required for running your own contracting company in most states

**Timeline:**
Apprentice to journeyman: 4-5 years
Journeyman to master: Additional 2-4 years of experience, then licensing exam
Total: 7-10 years from starting as an apprentice to master licensure

### Specialization Opportunities

Within each trade, specialists earn more than generalists.

**Electrical specializations:**
- High voltage work
- Industrial controls
- Fire alarm systems
- Renewable energy and solar
- Data center infrastructure

**Plumbing specializations:**
- Medical gas systems
- Fire sprinkler installation
- Industrial piping
- Hydronic heating

**HVAC specializations:**
- Commercial refrigeration
- Building automation systems
- Clean room environments
- Industrial HVAC

Specialization usually requires additional training and certifications. But specialists command higher wages and have more job security.

### Business Ownership Potential

The ultimate ceiling in trades is running your own company. Master licensure opens this door.

**What you need:**
- Master license in your trade (requirements vary by state)
- Contractor's license (if separate from trade license)
- Business registration
- Liability insurance
- Workers' compensation insurance (if you have employees)
- Surety bond (in some states)

**Reality check:**
Running a business is different from doing the work. You need skills in:
- Bidding and estimating
- Managing employees
- Bookkeeping and taxes
- Customer service
- Marketing

Many excellent tradespeople fail as business owners because they do not develop these skills. Others thrive and build substantial wealth.

If business ownership interests you, start learning about it during your apprenticeship. Pay attention to how successful contractors run their operations.

## Summary for young adults

Trades training offers a path to stable, well-paying work without college debt. You earn from day one with most apprenticeship programs. The ceiling is high if you develop both technical skills and business sense.

The catch: physical demands are real, drug testing is universal, and you need reliable transportation. Some programs are competitive and require waiting. Not every training program is legitimate.

Your best bet is a registered apprenticeship through a union or employer. These cost you nothing, pay you to learn, and result in portable credentials. Pre-apprenticeship programs can help you get there if you need preparation.

Research any program before committing. Use the scam detection patterns above. Verify registration with Apprenticeship.gov or your state apprenticeship agency.

The trades need workers. The barriers to entry are lower than most people think. But you have to show up, work hard, and stay clean.
