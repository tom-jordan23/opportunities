## Overview

Welding is one of the most portable skilled trades. You learn to join metal, and that skill travels with you. Construction sites in Texas, shipyards in Virginia, oil platforms in the Gulf, manufacturing plants in the Midwest—they all need welders. The work is physically demanding and the conditions can be harsh, but the barrier to entry is low compared to the earning potential.

For someone in the reader's position—limited funds, ready to start now—welding offers a realistic path. You can complete basic training in 6-12 months for under $10,000, often with financial aid covering most of it. Many programs provide certifications that employers recognize immediately. Once certified, you start earning. The more certifications you stack, the more doors open.

The question is not whether welding pays. It does. The question is whether you can handle the physical reality of the work—heat, fumes, awkward positions, outdoor conditions. If the answer is yes, this path can lead to $50,000-80,000 within a few years, with specialty tracks paying over $100,000.

## What You'll Do

Welding fuses metal parts together using heat. The specific work depends on the setting.

**Shop Work (Manufacturing)**
- Fabricating metal components in a controlled environment
- Repetitive work on production lines or custom jobs
- Climate-controlled building, predictable hours
- Often entry-level friendly—companies train their own welders

**Field Work (Construction)**
- Structural steel on buildings, bridges, highways
- Working outdoors in all weather conditions
- Significant physical demands—climbing, lifting, working at heights
- Often requires travel or relocation

**Specialty Work**
- Pipeline welding—travel-heavy, remote locations, high pay
- Shipyard welding—large-scale vessel construction and repair
- Aerospace welding—precision work on aircraft components
- Underwater welding—requires commercial diving certification, extreme conditions

A typical day involves reading blueprints, setting up equipment, positioning metal pieces, making welds, and inspecting your work. You spend hours under a welding hood with limited visibility. The arc is blindingly bright. The metal is hot enough to burn through gloves. When you finish a good weld, you can see it—clean, consistent, strong.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** Must be 18 to work as a welder (some training programs accept 16+). No citizenship requirements for most jobs, though government contractor work may require US citizenship.
- **Skills Needed:** Basic math, ability to read technical drawings, steady hands, good hand-eye coordination. No prior experience required for training programs.
- **Physical Requirements:** Standing for long periods, working in confined spaces, lifting 50+ pounds, tolerance for heat and uncomfortable positions. Good color vision helps distinguish metal temperatures.
- **Time Commitment:** Training programs range from 7 months to 2 years. Full-time work is typically 40-60 hours per week, with overtime common in construction and pipeline work.

## Training Provided

### Welding Processes You Will Learn

**MIG (GMAW) - Gas Metal Arc Welding**
- Easiest to learn—most beginners start here
- Uses a wire-fed electrode with shielding gas
- Common in manufacturing and auto repair
- Good for thin to medium-thickness metals

**Stick (SMAW) - Shielded Metal Arc Welding**
- The oldest and most versatile process
- Works outdoors and in windy conditions
- No external shielding gas required
- Common in construction and repair work

**TIG (GTAW) - Gas Tungsten Arc Welding**
- Most difficult to master—requires both hands plus foot pedal
- Produces the cleanest, most precise welds
- Used for thin metals, aluminum, stainless steel
- Required for aerospace and high-end fabrication

**Flux-Core (FCAW) - Flux-Cored Arc Welding**
- Similar to MIG but with self-shielding wire
- Excellent for outdoor construction work—wind resistant
- High deposition rate—fast welding
- Common in structural steel and shipbuilding

### Training Paths

**Community College (6-24 months)**
- Cost: $5,000-15,000 total
- Leads to certificate or associate degree
- Broader education—metallurgy, blueprint reading, safety
- Often includes AWS certification testing
- Financial aid eligible (Pell Grants, WIOA)

**Trade School (6-12 months)**
- Cost: $5,000-20,000 total
- Focused on hands-on welding only
- Faster completion
- Some are for-profit—research carefully
- Quality varies significantly

**Union Apprenticeship (3-5 years)**
- Cost: Free—you earn while you learn
- Combines classroom and on-the-job training
- Paid from day one, starting around $18-22/hour
- Leads to journeyman status with full wages
- Competitive entry—often requires aptitude tests

**Employer Training**
- Cost: Free
- Some manufacturers and shipyards hire trainees
- You work while learning
- Company-specific skills, may not transfer
- Lower starting wages during training

### Certifications That Matter

**AWS Certified Welder (CW)**
- Industry-standard certification from American Welding Society
- Performance-based test—no written exam required
- Test costs $300-450 depending on positions tested
- Valid for 6 months; maintained by employer verification
- Positions: 3G (vertical) + 4G (overhead) qualifies you for all plate positions

**AWS Certified Welding Inspector (CWI)**
- Requires 3-5 years of welding experience
- Written exam covering codes, inspection, metallurgy
- Current cost approximately $1,000 for exam and prep
- Leads to inspector roles paying $70,000-95,000+
- Major career advancement step

## The Money Reality

### Pay by Experience Level

| Experience | Hourly Rate | Annual (40 hrs) |
|------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Entry-level | $18-22 | $37,000-46,000 |
| 2-4 years certified | $22-28 | $46,000-58,000 |
| Journeyman/Specialist | $28-40 | $58,000-83,000 |
| Pipeline/Underwater | $40-75+ | $83,000-150,000+ |

**Median wage (May 2024):** $51,000/year or approximately $24.50/hour according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

### Pay by Location

Wages vary significantly by region. Highest-paying states include:
- Washington D.C.: $71,230 average
- Alaska: $70,490 average
- Hawaii: $66,020 average

States with strong oil/gas or shipbuilding industries (Texas, Louisiana, Virginia) often have higher demand and competitive wages.

### Pay Structures for Travel Work

Pipeline welders often receive compensation in three parts:
- **Arm pay:** Taxable hourly wage for welding work
- **Truck pay:** Compensation for your equipment/vehicle
- **Per diem:** Tax-free daily allowance for expenses

A 60-hour week for a pipeline welder can total around $5,000 when combining these components.

### Financial Aid Options

**Pell Grant**
- Maximum $7,395 for 2025-2026 academic year
- Does not need to be repaid
- Based on financial need
- Covers most community college welding programs

**WIOA (Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act)**
- Grant funding for job training
- Available if household income below 250% of poverty level
- Or if you lost a job through no fault of your own
- Contact your local American Job Center to apply

**Union Apprenticeship**
- No tuition—you earn from day one
- Starting wages typically $18-22/hour
- Wages increase as you progress

### Housing

- **Not typically provided** for shop or construction work
- **Sometimes provided** for remote pipeline, offshore, or shutdown work
- **Shipyard programs** occasionally include housing for apprentices
- Budget for housing costs in your area

### Hidden Costs

- Personal protective equipment wears out and needs replacement
- Certification renewal or re-testing if employer changes
- Tools—employers provide major equipment, but you buy personal tools
- Steel-toed boots ($100-200) wear out faster than regular shoes
- Work clothes get destroyed—plan on replacing them regularly

## How to Get Started

1. **Find training programs in your area.** Go to careeronestop.org and search for WIOA-eligible welding programs. Filter by your state. This shows programs that qualify for government funding.

2. **Visit your local American Job Center.** Search "American Job Center [your city]" to find the nearest location. Ask about WIOA eligibility and funding for welding training. They can assess your eligibility on the spot.

3. **Research union apprenticeships.** Visit these union websites and find your local hall:
   - Boilermakers: boilermakers.org
   - Ironworkers: ironworkers.org
   - United Association (Pipefitters): ua.org
   - Call the local hall and ask when the next apprenticeship application period opens

4. **Apply for FAFSA.** Go to studentaid.gov and complete the Free Application for Federal Student Aid. This determines your Pell Grant eligibility. Required for community college programs.

5. **Visit community colleges.** Meet with welding program advisors. Ask specific questions: What certifications are included? What is the job placement rate? Do they have industry partnerships?

6. **Explore employer-sponsored training.** Search Indeed or ZipRecruiter for "welding trainee" or "welding apprentice" in your area. Some manufacturers train entry-level workers.

## The Hard Parts

**The physical toll is real.** Welding demands working in awkward positions for hours. You will crouch, kneel, reach overhead, and hold steady while your muscles burn. Back, neck, and shoulder problems are common among career welders. Stretch. Take breaks when you can. This work wears on your body.

**Fumes are a serious health risk.** Welding fume contains metal particles small enough to reach deep into your lungs. Long-term exposure is linked to lung disease, including cancer. OSHA requires employers to provide ventilation and respiratory protection. Use it. Every time. This is not optional—it is the difference between a 30-year career and serious illness.

**The heat is oppressive.** You work inches from molten metal. In summer, add that to already hot environments. Welders wear heavy protective clothing despite the heat. Staying hydrated is not a suggestion—it is survival.

**The work is not always steady.** Construction and pipeline work follows projects. When one ends, you find the next. This means relocation, travel, or periods between jobs. Shop work is more stable but often pays less.

**Entry-level jobs can be hard to find.** Employers want certified welders. Training programs help, but you may need to take lower-paying fabrication or helper positions first. Breaking in takes persistence.

**The conditions vary wildly.** One job might be a climate-controlled aerospace facility. The next might be an outdoor construction site in January. Field welders go where the work is, and that place is not always comfortable.

**Advancement requires continuous certification.** Different jobs require different certifications. You may pass a 3G/4G plate test, then need pipe certifications for the next job. Testing costs money and time.

## Where This Can Lead

### Short-Term (1-3 Years)

- **Shop welder:** Stable manufacturing work, $40,000-55,000
- **Construction welder:** Field work with better pay, $45,000-65,000
- **Shipyard welder:** Large-scale fabrication, often entry-level friendly, $40,000-60,000

### Mid-Career (3-7 Years)

- **Pipe welder:** Specialized certification, in demand, $60,000-85,000
- **Combo welder:** Multiple processes, more versatile, higher pay
- **Traveling welder:** Shutdown and turnaround work, $70,000-100,000+
- **Foreman/Lead:** Supervising crews, less welding, more coordination

### Long-Term Specialization

**Certified Welding Inspector (CWI)**
- 5+ years experience required
- Exam and certification process
- Salary: $70,000-95,000+
- Less physical, more analysis and documentation
- Major career shift from production to quality

**Pipeline Welding**
- Highest-paying production welding
- Extensive travel—weeks away from home
- Demanding conditions, remote locations
- Salary: $80,000-120,000+ with overtime

**Underwater Welding**
- Requires separate commercial diving certification ($15,000-25,000, 5-7 months)
- Plus welding certification
- Physically demanding, hazardous work
- Salary: $54,000-150,000+ depending on depth and location
- Offshore saturation divers earn the most

**Aerospace Welding**
- Precision TIG work on aircraft and spacecraft
- Requires specific certifications (AWS D17.1, NADCAP)
- Clean facility environments
- Salary: $55,000-85,000
- Less physically demanding than construction

**Welding Engineer**
- Requires additional education (degree or extensive experience)
- Designs welding procedures and specifications
- Salary: $75,000-110,000+
- Office-based, minimal production work

### Skills That Transfer

- Blueprint reading—applies to any construction trade
- Fabrication and metal work—leads to machinist, millwright roles
- Equipment operation—many welders become equipment operators
- Safety culture—valued in any industrial setting
- Problem-solving under pressure—universal skill
