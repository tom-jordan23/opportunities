# Wind Turbine Technician

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** FC-001

## Overview

Wind turbine technician is the fastest-growing occupation in the United States. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 50% job growth from 2024 to 2034. That growth rate exceeds every other occupation tracked by the BLS.

This path offers strong earning potential for someone in a young person's situation. Entry-level technicians earn $45,000-$55,000 per year. Experienced technicians earn $62,000-$80,000. The median wage was $62,580 in May 2024.

Training programs run 12-24 months at community colleges and cost $9,000-$15,000. Some employers hire candidates with mechanical or electrical backgrounds and provide on-the-job training. Either path leads to the same career.

The work requires climbing 200-300 foot towers every day. You work inside the nacelle—the housing at the top of the turbine that contains the generator and mechanical systems. If you have a fear of heights or claustrophobia, this career is not for you. This is not something you can work through. Technicians who cannot handle heights do not last in this field.

The job also requires extensive travel. Many positions have you on the road for weeks at a time, moving between wind farms across multiple states. This lifestyle works well for someone who wants to travel. It does not work well for someone who needs to be home regularly.

## What You'll Do

Wind turbine technicians maintain and repair the mechanical, electrical, and hydraulic systems inside wind turbines. Most of your work happens in the nacelle—a space about the size of a small bus, mounted on a tower 200-300 feet in the air.

**A typical day includes:**
- Driving to the wind farm and checking in with site operations
- Climbing the tower using internal ladders while wearing fall protection harness and carrying tools
- Inspecting components: gearboxes, generators, bearings, brakes, pitch systems
- Lubricating moving parts according to maintenance schedules
- Diagnosing electrical and mechanical problems using diagnostic tools
- Replacing worn or malfunctioning components
- Performing routine preventive maintenance on schedule
- Documenting all work performed and parts used
- Rappelling from the nacelle to inspect or repair blades when needed
- Descending the tower and moving to the next turbine

Modern turbines have climb assist systems—powered ascenders that reduce the physical strain of climbing. Older turbines require manual climbing. Either way, you climb multiple towers per day.

Maintenance schedules vary by manufacturer and operating hours. Turbines running year-round may need routine maintenance one to three times per year. Unplanned repairs happen when components fail. Some repairs require multi-day efforts with specialized equipment.

You work in crews of two or more for safety. Solo work is rare and dangerous. One technician might work inside the nacelle while another provides ground support and rescue capability.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** Must be 18 or older. Valid driver's license required. Clean driving record. Work authorization required. No specific citizenship requirements for most employers.

- **Skills Needed:** Mechanical aptitude is essential. Electrical troubleshooting skills are valuable. Prior experience in HVAC, automotive repair, electrical work, or industrial maintenance helps but is not required. Strong reading comprehension for technical manuals. Basic math and measurement skills.

- **Physical Requirements:** This work demands physical fitness and specific tolerances:
  - **Heights:** You must be completely comfortable working 200-300 feet in the air. This is non-negotiable.
  - **Confined spaces:** The nacelle is cramped. You work in tight positions for hours.
  - **Climbing:** Even with climb assist, you spend significant time on ladders.
  - **Lifting:** Carry tools and equipment up to 50 pounds while climbing.
  - **Weather exposure:** You work outside in heat, cold, wind, and humidity.
  - **Physical fitness:** Annual medical evaluations are standard. Many employers require fitness certifications.

- **Time Commitment:** Full-time work. Many positions require 10-12 hour shifts. Overtime is common during busy periods. Travel positions may work 6 days per week for extended stretches, then have time off. Site-based positions may have more regular schedules.

## Training Provided

You can enter this field through two main paths: formal education programs or employer-sponsored training.

**Community College Programs (12-24 months):**

These programs provide comprehensive technical training and industry certifications.

- **Texas State Technical College (TSTC):** 12-month certificate for approximately $9,000. Associate degree in 20 months for approximately $13,000. Programs cover AC circuits, industrial automation, and wind turbine fundamentals.

- **Iowa Lakes Community College:** Offers both diploma (2 terms) and associate degree programs. Students train on a full-scale 1.65-megawatt operational turbine standing nearly 300 feet tall. The campus is located in prime wind country.

- **LaGuardia Community College (New York):** Includes OSHA 10-hour certification and NCCER curriculum. Students earn Global Wind Organisation (GWO) Basic Safety Training and Basic Technical Training certifications.

- **Kalamazoo Valley Community College (Michigan):** Wind Turbine Technician Academy with hands-on training.

**GWO Certifications:**

The Global Wind Organisation (GWO) sets the international training standard for wind technicians. GWO Basic Safety Training (BST) is mandatory for most wind industry positions.

GWO BST includes five modules:
- Working at Height (WAH)
- Fire Awareness (FAW)
- First Aid (FA)
- Manual Handling (MH)
- Sea Survival (for offshore work)

Standalone GWO BST training costs approximately $1,900-$2,500 at certified providers. Many community college programs include GWO certification in their curriculum.

**Employer Training:**

Major manufacturers and service companies hire candidates with mechanical or electrical backgrounds and provide company-specific training. This path works if you already have:
- Military technical training (aviation, electrical, mechanical)
- HVAC, automotive, or industrial maintenance experience
- Electrician or mechanical trade background

Employer training is specific to their turbine models and procedures. Companies like Vestas offer apprenticeship and traineeship programs with paid on-the-job training from day one.

## The Money Reality

**Entry-level pay (0-2 years):**
- $22-$27 per hour ($45,000-$56,000 annually)
- Entry-level positions start around $43,680-$45,600 per year

**Mid-career pay (3-5 years):**
- $27-$35 per hour ($56,000-$73,000 annually)

**Experienced technicians (5+ years):**
- $35-$42 per hour ($73,000-$88,000 annually)
- Top 10% earn over $80,000

**Lead technicians and supervisors:**
- $75,000-$95,000+ annually

**Offshore wind technicians:**
- $83,000+ average annual salary
- Higher pay reflects increased risk, specialized training, and logistics complexity
- Per diem and overtime add significantly to base salary

**Overtime:** Common in this field. Wind farms operate 24/7 and need coverage. Many technicians earn substantial additional income through overtime hours.

**Per diem:** Traveling technicians often receive per diem payments for lodging and meals. This can add $100-$150+ per day when away from home, adding tens of thousands to annual income.

**Housing:** Not provided for most positions. Site-based technicians find their own housing near the wind farm. Traveling technicians stay in company-paid hotels.

**Startup Costs:**
- Community college program: $9,000-$15,000
- GWO BST certification (if standalone): $1,900-$2,500
- Work boots: $150-$250
- Basic hand tools: $200-$500 (many employers provide)
- Driver's license: Required
- Relocation costs: Varies (many jobs are in rural areas)

**Benefits:** Large employers like Vestas, GE, and Siemens Gamesa offer full benefits packages: health insurance, dental, vision, 401(k) matching, paid time off, and training advancement opportunities.

## How to Get Started

**Step 1: Assess your comfort with heights honestly**

This is the critical first filter. Research tower climbing videos online. If watching makes you anxious, this career is likely not for you. Some training programs include tower climbing assessments early so you do not waste time and money discovering you cannot do this work.

**Step 2: Choose your training path**

*If you have funds for education:*
Research accredited wind technician programs. Look for programs that include:
- GWO certifications
- OSHA 10 or 30 certification
- Hands-on turbine access
- Industry connections for job placement

Start here:
- [WINDExchange Training Directory](https://windexchange.energy.gov/training) - DOE directory of training providers
- [Texas State Technical College](https://www.tstc.edu/programs/windenergytechnology/)
- [Iowa Lakes Community College](https://iowalakes.edu/program/wind-energy-turbine-technology-diploma/)

*If you have relevant experience (military, HVAC, electrical, mechanical):*
Apply directly to employers who offer on-the-job training. Emphasize your transferable skills.

**Step 3: Get basic certifications**

Complete OSHA 10-Hour Construction Safety certification online ($25-$50). This is a baseline expectation across the industry.

**Step 4: Apply to employers**

Major companies that hire and train entry-level technicians:
- **Vestas:** World's largest wind turbine manufacturer. Offers apprenticeship programs.
- **GE Renewable Energy:** Major manufacturer with training programs.
- **Siemens Gamesa:** Offers basic technician training and certification.
- **Nordex:** European manufacturer with U.S. operations.
- **NextEra Energy:** Largest U.S. wind operator.
- **Independent service companies:** Global Wind Service, Sky Climber Renewables, and others.

Search for jobs at:
- Company career websites directly
- Indeed.com (search "wind turbine technician entry level")
- ZipRecruiter.com
- Glassdoor.com
- SkillPointe.com

**Step 5: Be willing to relocate**

Wind technician jobs are concentrated in specific states. If you are not near a wind farm, you will need to relocate or take a traveling position. The top states for employment are:
- Texas (by far the largest)
- Iowa
- Oklahoma
- Kansas
- California
- Illinois
- North Dakota
- Minnesota
- Colorado
- Nebraska

**Step 6: Consider starting as traveling technician**

Many new technicians start in traveling roles, moving between sites. This provides diverse experience and higher income through per diem. After building experience, you can transition to a site-based position if you prefer stability.

## The Hard Parts

**Heights are unavoidable and intense.** You climb 200-300 foot towers every working day. There is no alternative. Some people think they can adjust to heights. Most who have a genuine fear of heights cannot. The height assessment during training is real—people wash out at this stage. If you have any doubt, volunteer to climb a cell tower or attend a training center open house before investing in education.

**The nacelle is cramped.** The equipment housing at the top of the turbine is not spacious. You work in tight, awkward positions around heavy machinery. Claustrophobia is as disqualifying as fear of heights.

**Weather does not stop the work—until it does.** Wind farms need maintenance year-round. You work in summer heat, winter cold, rain, and high humidity. Climbing a tower in freezing temperatures or light rain is standard. Work only stops for severe weather: lightning, extreme winds, or dangerous ice. Your tolerance for uncomfortable conditions matters.

**Travel is relentless for many positions.** Traveling technicians may be home one week every six weeks. Hotel living gets old. Relationships strain. If you have family commitments or want to put down roots, traveling positions are difficult. Site-based positions exist but are harder to get without experience.

**The work is physically demanding.** You carry tools while climbing. You work overhead. You lift, pull, and push heavy components. Physical fitness is not optional—it is survival. Many companies require annual fitness certifications. Injuries are possible.

**Injury risk is real.** Wind farm technicians have one of the highest rates of work-related injuries. Falls, electrical shocks, burns, and crushing injuries have all occurred. OSHA tracks fatalities in this industry. Safety protocols exist because the dangers are genuine. Complacency kills.

**Remote locations mean isolation.** Wind farms are in rural areas—often hours from cities. Site-based technicians live in small towns. Entertainment and social options are limited. Some people thrive in this environment. Others struggle with isolation.

**The work can be repetitive.** Much of the job is routine maintenance: inspections, lubrication, and standard checks. Not every day involves complex troubleshooting. Some technicians find the routine satisfying. Others get bored.

**Industry stability has some uncertainty.** While growth projections are strong, the wind industry depends on federal incentives and energy policy. Some offshore projects have been delayed or restructured. The overall trajectory is positive, but individual projects can face setbacks.

## Where This Can Lead

**Within wind energy:**
- Lead Technician: Supervise a crew. Requires 3-5 years experience. $75,000-$95,000.
- Site Supervisor/Manager: Oversee entire wind farm operations. Requires leadership skills and experience.
- Trainer/Instructor: Teach at training centers or within companies.
- Technical Specialist: Focus on specific systems (electrical, hydraulic, blade). Higher expertise, higher pay.

**Offshore wind:**
The U.S. offshore wind industry is growing, particularly on the East Coast. States like New York, New Jersey, and Massachusetts are developing major offshore projects. Offshore technicians earn higher salaries ($83,000+ average) due to additional certifications (including GWO sea survival), more challenging work conditions, and different logistics.

Offshore work involves:
- Sea transfer vessel or helicopter transport to turbines
- Potentially staying offshore for extended periods
- Additional safety training
- Higher physical and mental demands

**Related industries:**
- Solar Energy: Some skills transfer. Different work environment.
- Power Plant Operations: Utility-scale operations experience is valuable.
- Industrial Maintenance: Mechanical and electrical skills apply broadly.
- Elevator/Escalator Technician: Similar height work and mechanical systems.

**Further education:**
- Engineering degree: Some technicians pursue degrees to become wind energy engineers.
- Project management: Move into development and construction project management.
- Business ownership: Start a specialized service or contracting company.
