# Wildland Firefighting

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** FC-Claude

## Overview

Wildland firefighting offers one of the few entry-level careers where you can earn $40,000-$80,000 in a six-month season with no prior experience or certifications required. Federal agencies—the U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, and Fish and Wildlife Service—hire thousands of seasonal firefighters every year. You show up, they train you, and within weeks you are on the fireline earning overtime.

This is hard work. You will hike miles through rugged terrain carrying 45 pounds of gear. You will dig fireline for 14-16 hours a day in smoke and heat. You will be away from home for weeks at a time. Some firefighters die every year doing this job—between 2013 and 2022, 96 wildland firefighters died on duty in the U.S. Smoke exposure has been classified as carcinogenic by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.

For young adults, wildland firefighting checks important boxes: no upfront costs, training provided, immediate income, outdoor work, and a clear career ladder from seasonal grunt to permanent fire management. The work is seasonal—typically May through October—which leaves winters free for other pursuits. Many firefighters spend off-seasons traveling, working other jobs, or building skills.

The catch: getting hired is competitive, the work destroys your body over time, and seasonal positions do not include health insurance or retirement benefits. The path to permanent federal employment with full benefits takes years.

## What You'll Do

### Daily Reality During Fire Season

You report to your duty station by 7:00 or 8:00 AM most days. During slow periods, you do project work—trail maintenance, equipment checks, fitness training, chainsaw practice. When fires start, everything changes.

On an active fire, you work 16-hour days for 14 or more consecutive days. The work is physical and repetitive. You hike to the fire, often several miles through steep terrain. You dig fireline using hand tools—Pulaskis, McLeods, and shovels—creating a bare mineral break in the vegetation. You cut brush and small trees with chainsaws. You "mop up" after the fire passes, digging through ash and debris to find and extinguish smoldering hotspots.

Fire assignments take you away from your duty station for days or weeks. You sleep in fire camps—tent cities with hundreds or thousands of firefighters—or on the ground near the fire with just a sleeping bag. Food comes from caterers or MREs. Showers are infrequent. You wake up, eat, work, eat, sleep, and repeat.

When not on assignment, you return to normal 8-hour days at your station. Engine crews run drills and maintain equipment. Hand crews do project work like fuels reduction—clearing brush to prevent future fires. The pace varies wildly: some weeks you sit around waiting; other weeks you work 200+ hours.

### Types of Crews

**Engine Crews (3-10 people):** Work from fire engines, responding to fires in their district. Good for beginners—the work is less physically demanding than hand crews, and you have the engine as a mobile base. Most common entry point.

**Hand Crews (18-20 people):** On-the-ground firefighters who build fireline with hand tools. More hiking, more digging, more physical labor than engine work. Many entry-level positions available.

**Hotshot Crews (20 people):** Elite hand crews placed on the most difficult parts of the most dangerous fires. Hotshots work longer hours, hike farther, and take on the hardest assignments. You cannot start as a hotshot—they only hire experienced firefighters who can pass additional fitness and skill requirements.

**Helitack Crews (7-24 people):** Delivered to fires by helicopter. Some crews rappel from hovering helicopters. Work combines helicopter operations with ground firefighting. More competitive to get hired.

**Smokejumpers:** Parachute into remote fires from fixed-wing aircraft. The most elite wildland firefighters—highly experienced, physically exceptional. Smokejumpers have years of prior fire experience before they even apply.

For a first-time firefighter, engine crew or hand crew positions are realistic targets. Hotshot, helitack, and smokejumper roles come later in your career.

## Requirements

**Age/Eligibility:**
- Minimum age: 18 years old
- U.S. citizen or national (federal positions)
- Must register with Selective Service if male born after December 31, 1959
- Valid driver's license
- Must pass drug screening (marijuana included, even in legal states)
- Must pass background check for federal employment
- Age limit for apprenticeship positions: 18-37 (veterans may be exempt)

**Physical Requirements:**
- Pass the "Arduous" Work Capacity Test (pack test): Walk 3 miles in 45 minutes or less while carrying a 45-pound pack
- Ability to hike 5-10+ miles per day over rough terrain
- Lift and carry 50+ pounds regularly
- Work at high altitudes and in remote locations
- Tolerate smoke, heat, dust, and irregular sleep for extended periods
- No running allowed on the pack test—it is a fast walk

**Skills Needed:**
- No prior experience required for entry-level positions
- Basic fitness (start training for the pack test now if you are not already active)
- Ability to follow instructions and work as part of a team
- Willingness to be away from home for extended periods

**Time Commitment:**
- Seasonal positions: approximately 6 months (typically May-October, varies by region)
- Expect 40-80+ hours per week during fire season
- Fire assignments: 14-21 consecutive days away from home, followed by a few days off
- Permanent positions: year-round with different duties in off-season

## Training Provided

Federal agencies provide all required training at no cost to you. You get paid during training.

**Basic Training (S-130/S-190):**
- S-130: Firefighter Training—fire suppression techniques, safety, hand tool use
- S-190: Introduction to Wildland Fire Behavior—how fires spread, weather effects
- L-180: Human Factors in the Wildland Fire Service—fatigue, decision-making, leadership
- Training takes approximately 32-40 hours over one week

**Additional Training:**
- Chainsaw operation and safety
- First aid and CPR
- Engine operations (for engine crews)
- Annual refresher training required to maintain your "red card"

**The Red Card:**
After completing S-130/S-190, L-180, and passing the pack test, you receive an Incident Qualification Card—the "red card." This certifies you as a qualified firefighter and is required to work on any fire. You must complete annual refresher training to keep your card current.

**Career Development:**
As you gain experience, you can pursue additional qualifications: squad boss, crew boss, engine boss, helicopter manager, division supervisor, and eventually fire management officer. Each step requires specific training courses and documented experience on fires. The qualification system is standardized across all federal agencies.

**Apprenticeship Program:**
The Forest Service offers a Wildland Firefighter Apprenticeship Program—a 3,000-hour program at a residential academy. Apprentices receive pay during training, and the government covers all costs. Completing the apprenticeship grants "journey-level wildland firefighter" status, which accelerates career advancement. This is a permanent position with full benefits, not seasonal work.

## The Money Reality

### Base Pay (2025 Federal Rates)

Federal wildland firefighters are paid on the GW (General Wildland) pay scale, which replaced the old GS scale for fire positions in 2025-2026.

**Entry-Level Hourly Rates (Rest of U.S., effective March 23, 2025):**
- GW-03 Step 1: $20.93/hour (entry-level, no experience)
- GW-04 Step 1: $22.97/hour (some experience or college)
- GW-05 Step 1: $25.12/hour (more experience or degree)

Your grade depends on education and experience. Most first-time firefighters start at GW-03 or GW-04.

### What You Actually Earn

Base pay is just the beginning. The real money comes from overtime and hazard pay.

**Overtime:** 1.5x base pay for all hours over 8 per day or 40 per week. During fire season, 60-80+ hour weeks are common.

**Hazard Pay:** 25% of base pay for each hour spent on uncontrolled fireline. If you are fighting fire (not just sitting in camp), you get hazard pay.

**Incident Response Premium Pay (IRPP):** New in 2025—an additional 4.5x your hourly rate per day when assigned to qualifying fires away from your duty station for more than 36 hours.

**Example Day on the Fireline (GW-04 at $22.97/hour):**
- Base pay (8 hours): $183.76
- Overtime (8 hours at $34.46): $275.68
- Hazard pay (16 hours at $5.74): $91.84
- IRPP (if applicable): $103.37
- **Daily total: $551-654 before taxes**

Note: IRPP is capped at the equivalent daily earnings of a GS-10, step 10, and limited to no more than $9,000 per year.

**Seasonal Earnings:**
- Entry-level firefighters with 700 overtime hours: approximately $40,000-50,000
- Hotshot crews with 1,000+ overtime hours: approximately $60,000-80,000
- Earnings vary significantly based on fire activity—busy fire years pay more

### State and Private Options

**State Agencies (Example: Oregon Department of Forestry):**
- Entry-level pay: $16-24/hour depending on position and experience
- Season: June through September
- May include state benefits for seasonal employees

**CalFire (California):**
- Fire Fighter I: $4,403-5,566/month
- 72-hour shifts (three consecutive 24-hour days on duty)
- Combines wildland and structural fire response
- More year-round work than federal seasonal positions

**Private Contractors:**
- Pay varies widely—some pay more than federal, some less
- Less job security and fewer benefits
- Work on federal contracts when fire agencies need additional resources
- Can be a way to get experience if federal positions are unavailable

### Housing

**During Fire Season:**
Some duty stations have bunkhouses available—free or reduced rent. Availability varies by location; it is first-come, first-served. Many firefighters find their own housing: shared rentals, camping on public land, or living in vehicles.

**On Fire Assignments:**
Housing is provided. You sleep in fire camps (tent cities) or on the ground near the fire. Food is provided by camp caterers.

**Off-Season:**
You are on your own. Seasonal positions end in October or November. You need savings or another job to cover winter months. Some firefighters travel, work ski resorts, or find other seasonal work.

### Startup Costs

Federal positions have minimal upfront costs:
- Work boots (leather, 8" minimum): $150-300
- Transportation to your duty station: varies
- Savings for first 2-4 weeks before first paycheck arrives

**What's Provided:**
- All firefighting gear and equipment
- Training at no cost
- Personal protective equipment (PPE)
- Food and lodging on fire assignments

### Benefits

**Seasonal/Temporary Positions:**
- No health insurance
- No retirement contributions
- No paid leave
- This is the trade-off for high overtime earnings

**Permanent Positions (Including Apprenticeship):**
- Federal health insurance (FEHB)
- Retirement contributions (FERS)
- Paid annual and sick leave
- Life insurance
- Access to Thrift Savings Plan (401k equivalent)

**All Positions:**
- Workers' compensation for injuries
- Access to federal Employee Assistance Program

### Hidden Costs

- Winter unemployment—seasonal positions end and you need income
- Moving expenses if stationed far from home
- Vehicle wear if commuting long distances
- Health care costs during the season if you lack coverage
- Physical therapy and injury recovery (this work hurts your body)

## How to Get Started

### Step 1: Get Fit for the Pack Test

You must walk 3 miles in 45 minutes while carrying 45 pounds. Start training now.

**Training Plan (minimum 4 weeks before the test):**
1. Week 1-2: Walk 3 miles daily on flat terrain, no pack
2. Week 2-3: Add a pack, start at 20 pounds, build to 35 pounds
3. Week 3-4: Increase weight to 45 pounds, practice on hilly terrain
4. Test yourself: complete the full test before applying

You cannot run during the test—it is a fast walk. Focus on leg strength and cardio endurance. Stretch your calves and shins before training; shin splints are the most common reason people fail.

### Step 2: Create a USAJobs Account

Go to [usajobs.gov](https://www.usajobs.gov) and create an account. This is the only way to apply for federal firefighter positions.

**Build your resume in the USAJOBS system.** Federal resumes can be longer than private-sector resumes—include all relevant experience in detail. Match your qualifications to job announcement requirements.

**Set up saved searches** for:
- "Wildland Firefighter"
- "Forestry Aid Fire"
- "Forestry Technician Fire"

Select the states where you want to work. USAJOBS will email you when positions open.

### Step 3: Apply During the Hiring Window

Federal hiring follows a specific schedule:

**Fall Hiring (October 2025 for 2026 Season):**
- BLM and NPS positions: October 1-21 (some close by October 15)
- USFS positions: October 15-29 (some open until November 11)
- These deadlines are strict—applications submitted late are not considered
- Check individual forest or regional websites for specific dates

**Summer/Fall Hiring (August-September 2025):**
- USFS permanent positions: August 22-September 23, 2025
- Some permanent positions and apprenticeships open at different times

Apply broadly. Do not limit yourself to one location or agency. First-time applicants should apply to 10-20+ positions to maximize chances.

### Step 4: Contact Local Fire Management Offices

Before applying, call or email fire management offices in areas where you want to work.

**What to ask:**
- Are you hiring seasonal firefighters this year?
- What positions are available?
- What do you look for in candidates?
- Can I visit the station to learn more?

Making personal contact shows initiative and helps your application stand out. Many hiring managers remember candidates who called ahead.

### Step 5: Be Patient and Persistent

Federal hiring is slow. You may not hear back for weeks or months after applying. Check your application status regularly on USAJOBS.

**If you do not get hired the first year:**
- Consider state or private contractor positions to gain experience
- Volunteer with a local fire department for basic training
- Reapply next year with documented fire-related experience
- Get certified in first aid, CPR, and chainsaw operation on your own

Many firefighters apply 2-3 times before getting hired. Persistence matters.

### Step 6: Complete Training and Testing

Once hired, you will:
1. Complete medical examination and receive clearance
2. Pass the arduous pack test (3 miles, 45 pounds, 45 minutes)
3. Complete S-130, S-190, and L-180 training
4. Receive your red card
5. Begin work at your assigned duty station

All training is provided and paid. You do not need certifications before applying.

## The Hard Parts

### Physical Destruction

This job damages your body. You carry heavy loads over rough terrain for hours. You swing tools until your arms fail. You work through exhaustion, heat, and smoke. Knee problems, back problems, and chronic injuries accumulate over seasons.

The first weeks are brutal. Experienced firefighters call it "getting your fire legs." Blisters, sore muscles, and fatigue are normal. Some people quit after their first fire assignment because they cannot handle the physical demands.

### Smoke Exposure

You breathe smoke constantly during fire assignments. Wildfire smoke contains particulates, carbon monoxide, and carcinogens. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified wildland firefighter smoke exposure as a Group 1 carcinogen—the same category as asbestos and tobacco.

A 2019 study by Navarro et al. estimated wildland firefighters face 8-43% increased risk of lung cancer mortality and 16-30% increased risk of cardiovascular disease mortality over their careers, depending on years worked (5-25 years) and fire days per season (49-98 days). N95 masks help but are not perfect, and many firefighters do not wear them consistently.

### Danger

Firefighters die every year. Between 2013 and 2022, 96 wildland firefighters died on duty in the United States—roughly 10 per year. Wildland fatalities increased from 2% to 10% of total firefighter fatalities over this period—a 500% relative increase—despite a 23% decrease in the average annual number of wildfires. This suggests individual fires have become more dangerous. Burn-related fatalities have increased from 9% to 27% of wildland deaths.

Common causes of death: burnovers (fire trapping firefighters), vehicle accidents, aircraft crashes, falling trees and debris, cardiac events from exertion, and heat-related illness. The deadliest incidents involve entire crews—the 19 Granite Mountain Hotshots killed in 2013, the 14 South Canyon firefighters killed in 1994.

### Time Away From Home

Fire assignments last 14-21 days. You may deploy multiple times per season. Some firefighters spend more time on the road than at home. Relationships suffer. You miss birthdays, holidays, and family events.

Communication during assignments is limited. Cell service in remote areas is unreliable. Satellite phones and radios are for official use. You may go days without talking to family.

### Seasonal Uncertainty

Your income depends on fire activity. Wet years mean fewer fires, less overtime, and less money. Some seasons you earn $70,000; others you scrape by on $30,000. You cannot predict how much you will make before the season ends.

Seasonal positions end in fall. You need savings or another job for winter. Many firefighters combine fire seasons with ski patrol, construction, or other seasonal work. The instability is exhausting.

### No Benefits (Seasonal)

Seasonal/temporary positions do not include health insurance. You work one of the most dangerous jobs in America without medical coverage. Injuries happen—and medical bills follow.

Converting to permanent status takes years. You need multiple seasons of experience, documented qualifications, and luck with hiring. Some firefighters work 10+ seasons before getting a permanent position.

### Career Health Decline

The longer you fight fire, the greater your health risks. Studies show "number of years on the job was an independent risk factor for death from stress/exertion and heart attacks, while age was not." This means cumulative exposure matters more than how old you are.

Many career firefighters transition to less physical roles—dispatch, fire prevention, management—as their bodies wear out. Planning for career progression early matters for long-term health.

## Where This Can Lead

### Career Ladder (Federal)

**Entry Level → Journey Level (2-4 seasons):**
- Start as Firefighter Type 2 (FFT2) at GW-03 or GW-04
- Gain experience and training
- Qualify as Firefighter Type 1 (FFT1) and Squad Boss
- Increase to GW-05

**Journey Level → Supervisor (4-8 seasons):**
- Engine Captain, Crew Boss, or Assistant Engine Operator
- GW-06 to GW-08
- Lead crews on fires
- Supervise entry-level firefighters

**Supervisor → Management (8+ seasons):**
- Hotshot Superintendent, Fire Management Officer, Division Supervisor
- GW-09 to GW-12
- Oversee multiple crews or entire fire districts
- Year-round permanent positions with full benefits

**Senior Management:**
- District Fire Management Officer, Forest FMO, Regional positions
- GW-13 to GW-15
- Strategic planning and leadership roles
- Fewer physical demands, more administrative work

### Permanent Status

The Wildland Firefighter Apprenticeship Program offers a direct path to permanent employment with full federal benefits. After completing 3,000 hours of training, you receive journey-level status and permanent position—skipping years of seasonal uncertainty.

Without the apprenticeship, the typical path is:
1. 3-5 seasons as a seasonal/temporary employee
2. Apply for "term" positions (1-4 year appointments with benefits)
3. Convert to permanent status through competitive hiring

The Land Management Workforce Flexibility Act allows qualified seasonal employees to compete for permanent positions after three years of service.

### Related Career Paths

**Structural Firefighting:** Wildland experience helps with municipal fire department hiring. Many departments value candidates with fire experience.

**Emergency Management:** Fire experience leads to roles in emergency operations centers, FEMA, and disaster response.

**Fire Prevention and Investigation:** Fire behavior knowledge applies to fire investigation, arson investigation, and prevention education.

**Aviation:** Helicopter and fixed-wing pilots can specialize in firefighting aviation.

**Dispatch and Logistics:** Year-round positions in fire dispatch centers coordinate firefighting resources.

**Conservation and Land Management:** Fire experience provides pathways to other land management roles with the Forest Service, BLM, and NPS.

### Transferable Skills

Even if you leave wildland fire, you take:
- Ability to work under extreme stress and physical demands
- Teamwork and communication in high-stakes situations
- Decision-making with incomplete information
- Equipment operation and maintenance
- Medical response and first aid
- Leadership experience
- Seasonal work ethic employers recognize
