# Peace Corps

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** FC-001

## Overview

The Peace Corps is a U.S. government agency that sends American volunteers to work in communities abroad. Volunteers serve for 27 months. The first 10-12 weeks is pre-service training in-country. The remaining two years is active service at your assigned site.

This is a serious commitment. You will live in a foreign country with limited amenities. You will work on community development projects in one of six sectors: education, health, agriculture, environment, youth development, or community economic development. You will learn a new language. You will experience culture shock, isolation, and professional frustrations.

**Critical for young adults:** Most Peace Corps positions require a bachelor's degree. However, some positions accept candidates with 3-5 years of relevant work experience instead of a degree. At 20 without a degreeyou would need to either gain several years of relevant experience first or pursue this opportunity after completing education. This document explains both the standard path and the alternatives.

What makes Peace Corps worth considering despite the barriers:
- Full support while serving. Housing, living allowance, health care, and travel costs are covered.
- Real skills development. Language training, cross-cultural competency, and technical skills in your sector.
- Significant post-service benefits. $10,000 readjustment allowance, noncompetitive eligibility for federal jobs, student loan benefits, and graduate school fellowship opportunities.
- Career credibility. Employers and graduate programs recognize Peace Corps service as evidence of commitment, adaptability, and leadership.

The catch: This is 27 months abroad with modest living conditions, limited personal freedom, and a mission-driven focus that requires genuine buy-in. People who treat this as an extended vacation or resume builder tend to quit early.

## What You'll Do

Your daily work depends entirely on your sector assignment and your specific site. Here is what each sector involves:

### Education
You teach English, literacy, math, or science in local schools. You work with teachers to improve instruction methods. You engage parents and communities in supporting student learning. This is the largest sector. Most education positions require a bachelor's degree, though some accept equivalent experience plus TEFL certification.

### Health
You promote healthy behaviors in communities. Topics include nutrition, hygiene, maternal health, sexual health, and HIV/AIDS prevention. You work with local health organizations, clinics, and community groups. Health positions typically require a bachelor's degree in a health-related field or significant relevant experience.

### Agriculture
You work with small-scale farmers to increase food security and production. You teach sustainable farming practices and help communities adapt to climate change. Some agriculture positions accept candidates with farming experience or agricultural education in lieu of a bachelor's degree.

### Environment
You lead community efforts to protect natural resources. You teach environmental awareness in schools and community organizations. You may work on conservation, climate adaptation, or natural resource management projects. Environmental positions vary in requirements.

### Youth in Development
You work with young people on a range of programs. Projects include life skills education, sports, environmental stewardship, sexual health, gender awareness, and information technology. Youth development positions sometimes have more flexibility in education requirements.

### Community Economic Development
You work with development banks, NGOs, and local businesses to create economic opportunities. You teach business skills, help entrepreneurs develop products, and support community economic initiatives. These positions typically require relevant education or professional experience.

### Daily Reality

A typical day might include:
- Morning language practice with your host family or neighbors
- Walking or biking to your work site. There may be no vehicle.
- Teaching a class, leading a workshop, or meeting with community partners
- Administrative tasks with limited technology. Handwritten reports are common.
- Afternoon community engagement. Visiting homes, attending events, building relationships.
- Evening meal with your host family or community members
- Limited electricity, internet, or entertainment options

The work is relationship-based. You do not arrive with a predetermined project and execute it. You spend months building trust and understanding community needs before meaningful work begins. Many volunteers find this slow pace frustrating. Others find it transformative.

## Requirements

### Basic Eligibility
- **Citizenship:** U.S. citizen only. No exceptions.
- **Age:** 18 or older. No upper age limit. The average volunteer age is 28, but volunteers range from 18 to over 80.
- **Education:** This is the critical barrier for young adults. Most positions require a bachelor's degree. Some positions accept 3-5 years of relevant professional experience instead. A few positions may accept an associate's degree combined with relevant work experience.
- **Health:** You must pass medical clearance. Peace Corps will assess whether your physical and mental health conditions can be accommodated at overseas sites with limited medical facilities.
- **Legal:** You must pass background and legal clearance. Some criminal history may be disqualifying.
- **Availability:** You must be able to commit to 27 months abroad. You cannot maintain a U.S. job or take classes during service.

### Positions Without a Degree

Peace Corps states that some positions are open to candidates with work experience instead of a degree. The exact requirements vary by position and change over time. Current guidance suggests:

- **5 years of relevant experience** may substitute for a degree in some sectors
- **Associate's degree plus relevant work experience** may qualify for some positions
- **3-5 years of professional experience** can make candidates competitive, especially in agriculture, trades, or community development

To be realistic: positions that do not require a degree are less common. They tend to be in sectors where hands-on skills matter more than formal education. you would need to spend several years building relevant experience before becoming a competitive candidate.

**Practical path for young adults:** Spend 3-5 years gaining experience in agriculture (farming, food security organizations), community development (nonprofit work, business development), or youth programming. Then apply to positions in those sectors. Alternatively, pursue education and apply after completing a degree.

### Physical and Mental Preparation
- You must handle living without consistent electricity, running water, or internet
- You must adapt to unfamiliar food, climate, and living conditions
- You must manage isolation from family, friends, and American culture for extended periods
- You must work effectively despite language barriers and cultural differences
- You must tolerate ambiguity and frustration when projects do not proceed as planned

## Training Provided

### Pre-Service Training (PST)
The first 10-12 weeks in-country are intensive training. You stay with a host family during this period. Training includes:

**Language instruction:** Small-group classes several days per week. Practice with host family and community. You must meet a minimum language proficiency benchmark to swear in as a volunteer.

**Technical training:** Skills relevant to your sector assignment. This builds on experience you bring and emphasizes adapting skills to the local context.

**Cross-cultural training:** Understanding local customs, norms, and expectations. Learning to navigate cultural differences respectfully.

**Health and safety:** Preventing illness, managing risks, emergency protocols.

**Community assessment:** Methods for understanding community needs and building effective partnerships.

Training is demanding. You must demonstrate competence in language, technical skills, and cultural adaptation to officially become a volunteer. Some trainees do not make it through PST.

### Language Skills
Peace Corps provides intensive language training in the local language. This may be a national language, a regional language, or a local dialect. Many countries require proficiency in multiple languages.

Volunteers typically achieve intermediate proficiency during PST and continue improving throughout service. Language ability directly affects your effectiveness and quality of life.

### Credentials
Peace Corps service is not a credentialing program. You do not earn certifications or licenses. What you gain is experience, language skills, and the Peace Corps credential on your resume. This is recognized by employers and graduate programs, but it is not a formal professional certification.

## The Money Reality

### Pay

There is no salary. You receive a living allowance intended to cover basic needs at a level comparable to local community members.

- **Living allowance:** Varies by country. Covers housing, food, transportation, and basic living expenses. This is modest. You will not live comfortably by American standards.
- **Net savings:** Effectively zero. The allowance covers basic needs, not savings.

### Readjustment Allowance

At the end of service, you receive a completion bonus:
- **Full service (27 months):** $10,000 before taxes
- **One year of service:** $5,000 before taxes
- **Accumulation rate:** $400 per month during service (as of May 2022)

This money helps you transition back to U.S. life. It is taxable income.

### Housing

Housing is provided at no cost to you. Quality varies dramatically by country and site.

**Possible conditions:**
- Small brick house with tin or thatched roof
- Cement block walls and cement floors
- No electricity or limited electricity with frequent outages
- No running water. You may draw water from a well or communal pump.
- Pit latrine instead of flush toilet
- Bucket bathing instead of shower

**Better-resourced sites may have:**
- Running water (may be cold only)
- Reliable electricity
- Indoor bathroom
- More durable construction

You do not choose your housing. You accept what is available at your assigned site. Some sites are in cities with more amenities. Some are in remote villages with minimal infrastructure. You will not know until you are placed.

### Health Care

Peace Corps provides comprehensive health care during service:
- Medical officers at every post provide primary care
- Medications and supplies provided
- Specialist referrals when needed
- Medical evacuation for serious conditions
- Mental health support

After service:
- Limited eligibility for continued care for conditions related to service through the Federal Employees' Compensation Act (FECA)
- Option to purchase up to 18 months of AfterCorps limited-benefit health insurance to help with transition
- FECA claims must be filed within three years of completion of service for service-related conditions

### Startup Costs

Peace Corps covers virtually all costs:
- **Airfare to country of service:** Paid
- **Airfare home after service:** Paid
- **Visa and passport assistance:** Provided
- **Vaccinations:** Provided and paid
- **Pre-departure materials:** Provided

**You pay for:**
- Personal clothing and gear suitable for your country
- Personal items beyond basic necessities
- Travel for personal reasons during leave time

**Estimate:** A few hundred dollars for personal preparation. Possibly more depending on what gear you need.

### Student Loan Benefits

Peace Corps service provides several student loan benefits:

**Deferment:** Federal student loans can be deferred during service.

**Interest payment:** For some loans, Peace Corps may pay interest that accrues during deferment.

**Perkins Loan cancellation:** Up to 70% cancellation for Perkins loans (15% for each of the first two years, 20% each for years 3-4).

**Public Service Loan Forgiveness:** Peace Corps service counts as qualifying employment. Your 27 months of service equals 27 qualifying payments toward the 120 required for PSLF.

**Important:** Peace Corps does not grant or deny loan benefits directly. Contact your loan servicer to understand your specific options.

### Hidden Costs

- **Career opportunity cost:** 27 months not building experience in a traditional career path
- **Relationship strain:** Extended absence affects relationships with family and friends
- **Limited earning years:** You cannot save money during service
- **Reintegration costs:** Returning to the U.S. after 27 months requires rebuilding your life

### Vacation Time

You accrue 2 vacation days per month. That is 48 days total over two years.

**Restrictions:**
- No leave during first 6 months (training and settling in)
- No leave during last 3 months of service
- Peace Corps may restrict when and where you travel
- Leave during critical program periods may be denied

You can travel within your country or internationally with approval. Some volunteers use leave to visit other countries in the region.

## How to Get Started

The application process takes 7-9 months from submission to departure. Here are the concrete steps:

1. **Research positions on peacecorps.gov.** Browse current openings. Note which positions require degrees and which accept experience. Identify sectors that match your skills and interests.

2. **Create an account on the Peace Corps application portal.** You need an email, personal information, and details about your education and experience.

3. **Complete the application.** This includes:
   - Personal information and background
   - Education history
   - Work and volunteer experience
   - Health history questionnaire (not yet full medical clearance)
   - Motivational statement explaining why you want to serve
   - Two references who can speak to your qualifications

4. **Wait for interview invitation.** Not all applicants receive interviews. Competitive applications demonstrate relevant skills, cultural openness, and genuine commitment to service.

5. **Complete the interview.** This is typically conducted via video call. Expect questions about your motivations, adaptability, relevant experience, and understanding of Peace Corps service.

6. **Receive an invitation (if selected).** The invitation specifies your country and sector. You can accept or decline. You have limited ability to negotiate placement.

7. **Complete medical clearance.** After accepting an invitation, you submit comprehensive medical documentation:
   - Physical examination
   - Dental examination
   - Lab work
   - Vaccination records
   - Mental health assessment (if indicated)
   - Specialty evaluations for specific conditions

   This process takes time. Respond promptly to all requests.

8. **Complete legal clearance.** Background check and legal review. Some criminal history may be disqualifying.

9. **Complete onboarding.** About two months before departure, you access additional portals for paperwork, reading materials, and pre-departure preparation. You may have video calls with staff and begin language practice.

10. **Depart for your country of service.** Peace Corps coordinates and pays for your travel.

**Timeline expectations:**
- Application to interview: 1-3 months
- Interview to invitation: 1-2 months
- Invitation to departure: 3-5 months
- Total: 7-9 months typical

**For young adults specifically:** Given degree requirements, consider applying 3-5 years from now after gaining relevant work experience. Use the time to:
- Gain experience in agriculture, community development, youth work, or health
- Volunteer with relevant organizations
- Learn basic skills in a commonly-needed language (Spanish, French)
- Research which positions historically accept non-degree candidates

## The Hard Parts

**The 27-month commitment is real.** You cannot easily leave. Your community, colleagues, and Peace Corps have invested in your presence. Early termination affects your benefits and leaves your community without the support they expected. Historical early termination rates from entry to close of service have been around 30%, though recent fiscal year reports show varying rates depending on demographics and measurement method. People quit for many reasons. This is a significant commitment to make without certainty.

**Culture shock is disorienting.** Everything familiar is gone. Food, language, social norms, daily routines, entertainment, relationships. The adjustment takes months. Some volunteers never fully adapt. Reverse culture shock when returning home is often worse than the initial adjustment abroad.

**Isolation is profound.** You may be the only American in your community. You may be hours from other volunteers. Communication with home is limited by technology and time zones. Loneliness is a major reason people quit.

**You have limited control over placement.** You express preferences. Peace Corps decides where you go. You may end up in a country or community you would not have chosen. The sector may not be your first choice. The site may be more rural or urban than you wanted.

**Health risks are real.** You serve in regions with diseases uncommon in the U.S. Malaria, waterborne illnesses, and tropical diseases are possibilities. Peace Corps provides prevention and treatment, but risks exist. Approximately 10% of volunteers in Africa report having malaria during service.

**The work is frustrating.** Projects move slowly. Community buy-in takes time. Bureaucracy and cultural differences create obstacles. You may spend months feeling like you accomplish nothing. Some volunteers finish service uncertain whether they made any lasting impact.

**Living conditions are basic.** Depending on your site:
- No electricity or unreliable power
- No running water
- No internet or very slow connections
- Outdoor latrine
- Limited food variety
- Extreme heat, cold, or humidity

**Mental health challenges are common.** Isolation, culture shock, frustration, and limited support systems strain mental health. Peace Corps provides support, but resources are limited at remote sites.

**Career timing is complex.** 27 months is a significant gap. You must explain it to future employers. The experience is valuable, but reintegrating into the U.S. job market requires effort.

**Relationships suffer.** Long-distance relationships rarely survive 27 months. Family relationships change. Friendships fade. You return to a social network that has moved on without you.

## Where This Can Lead

### Immediate Post-Service Benefits

**Readjustment allowance:** $10,000 (pre-tax) for full service completion provides a financial cushion while you transition.

**Noncompetitive eligibility (NCE):** For one year after service, federal agencies can hire you without competing through the standard application process. This is a significant advantage for federal employment. Extensions up to three years are possible for education, military service, or other qualifying activities.

**Paul D. Coverdell Fellows Program:** Over 200 graduate programs at 150+ universities offer financial assistance to returned volunteers. Based on a 2017-2024 survey, the average award package is nearly $22,000. Programs span public policy, international affairs, education, public health, social work, and other fields.

**Student loan benefits:** Perkins loan cancellation, deferment, and PSLF eligibility continue providing value after service.

### Career Pathways

**Federal government:** NCE provides a direct path into federal employment. USAID, State Department, and other internationally-focused agencies actively recruit returned volunteers.

**International development:** Many returned volunteers work for USAID implementing partners such as Chemonics, FHI 360, Catholic Relief Services, and similar organizations. Peace Corps experience is highly valued.

**Nonprofit sector:** Organizations focused on education, public health, community development, and international issues recognize Peace Corps service as relevant experience.

**Education:** Some returned volunteers pursue teaching careers, often in diverse or underserved communities.

**Graduate school:** Peace Corps experience strengthens applications to programs in public policy, international relations, public health, social work, and related fields. The Coverdell Fellows program provides financial support.

### Skills Gained

**Language proficiency:** You return with working fluency in at least one foreign language. This is valuable for international careers, translation, and cultural work.

**Cross-cultural competency:** You understand how to work effectively across cultural differences. This skill transfers to diverse workplaces.

**Adaptability:** You have proven ability to function in uncertain, resource-limited environments.

**Community development skills:** Practical experience building relationships and implementing projects in communities.

**Independence and resilience:** You survived and succeeded in challenging conditions for over two years.

### What Returned Volunteers Say

Common themes from returned Peace Corps volunteers:
- The experience fundamentally changed their worldview
- Relationships formed with community members remain meaningful years later
- The frustrations were real but ultimately outweighed by the rewards
- Language skills and cultural competency opened career doors
- Reintegration to U.S. life was harder than expected
- They would recommend Peace Corps to others, with honest caveats about the challenges
