# National Park Service Seasonal Positions

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** TJ

## Overview

The National Park Service hires thousands of seasonal employees each year to staff America's 400+ national parks. These temporary positions last up to six months and cover everything from leading tours to maintaining trails to staffing entrance stations.

This opportunity works for young adults because many positions require no prior experience. Some parks provide housing. You work in spectacular natural settings. The pay is modest but reliable. And if you want to build a career in land management, seasonal work is how almost everyone starts.

The catch: these jobs are extremely competitive. Iconic parks like Yellowstone and Yosemite receive hundreds of applications for a handful of openings. Housing shortages force many parks to limit hiring. The work is temporary by definition. And the federal application process is notoriously slow and confusing.

## What You'll Do

Your daily reality depends entirely on which position you land. The NPS fills seasonal jobs across many divisions.

**Interpretation and Education**
- Lead guided walks, talks, and campfire programs
- Staff visitor centers and answer questions
- Develop educational materials
- Work with school groups and youth programs
- Explain natural and cultural history to visitors

**Visitor Services**
- Staff entrance stations and collect fees
- Work at information desks
- Help visitors plan their trips
- Respond to basic visitor needs and questions
- Manage campground check-ins

**Maintenance**
- Repair and maintain trails
- Clean restrooms and facilities
- Groundskeeping and landscaping
- Building repairs and upkeep
- Remove litter and keep parks presentable

**Resource Management**
- Assist with wildlife surveys
- Invasive species removal
- Vegetation monitoring
- Archaeological site protection
- Water quality testing

**Law Enforcement (Protection Rangers)**
- Patrol park areas
- Emergency medical response
- Search and rescue operations
- Enforce park regulations
- Wildfire response

Most people start in interpretation, visitor services, or maintenance. Law enforcement positions require completing the Park Ranger Law Enforcement Academy (PRLEA) before you can apply.

A typical day in visitor services means 8 hours at an entrance station or visitor center. You greet visitors, answer the same questions hundreds of times, handle fee collection, and respond to whatever situations arise. Interpretation rangers spend hours preparing programs, then deliver them to groups of all sizes. Maintenance crews start early and work outdoors in whatever weather the park has that day.

## Requirements

- **Age:** 18 or older for most positions. Some youth programs hire at 15-17.
- **Citizenship:** U.S. citizen or national for most positions. Some jobs open to lawful permanent residents.
- **Education:** High school diploma or GED for GS-3 and GS-4 positions. One year of college qualifies for GS-5. A bachelor's degree qualifies for GS-5 directly.
- **Experience:** Many entry-level positions accept education in place of experience. Six months of general experience qualifies for GS-4. One year of specialized experience qualifies for GS-5.
- **Background check:** Required for all federal positions. Arrest records, drug use, and financial history are reviewed.
- **Physical ability:** Varies by position. Maintenance and trail work require physical labor. Desk jobs have minimal physical requirements.
- **Driver's license:** Many positions require a valid license. Some require operating government vehicles.
- **Time commitment:** Seasonal positions last up to 1,039 hours in a service year. Most summer positions run May through September. Winter positions run October through February.

**Law Enforcement Positions:** To work as a protection ranger, you must complete the Park Ranger Law Enforcement Academy (PRLEA) at your own expense before applying. The program runs 16-18 weeks and costs $7,600-$9,000 depending on the training center and residency status.

## Training Provided

Training varies by position and park. Expect:
- Park orientation covering safety, policies, and emergency procedures
- Position-specific training for your role
- First aid and CPR certification for many positions
- Defensive driving for positions requiring vehicle operation

Interpretation rangers receive training in presentation skills, park history, and visitor engagement. Maintenance workers learn equipment operation and safety protocols. Fee collectors learn the fee system and cash handling.

**Credentials earned:** Most training is internal and does not transfer to recognized certifications. Exceptions include first aid, CPR, and occasionally chainsaw or wildfire certifications depending on your specific role.

The real value is experience. Six months working in a national park teaches you how federal land management works. You learn visitor services, outdoor work, emergency response, and public speaking. These skills transfer to other parks, forests, and agencies.

## The Money Reality

### Pay

Seasonal positions are paid on the General Schedule (GS) scale at locality-adjusted rates.

| Grade | Base Annual (2026) | Approximate Hourly | Typical Positions |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| GS-3 | $27,708 | $13.28 | Park Guide (entry) |
| GS-4 | $31,103 | $14.91 | Visitor Use Assistant |
| GS-5 | $34,799 | $16.68 | Park Ranger (Interpretation) |
| GS-6 | $38,791 | $18.60 | Park Ranger with experience |
| GS-7 | $43,106 | $20.66 | Park Ranger, Maintenance Worker |

These are base rates. Locality pay adjustments add 15-30% in higher-cost areas. A GS-5 in the San Francisco locality earns roughly $41,000 annually. In rural areas, expect closer to base pay.

Overtime is common during busy season. Time-and-a-half applies after 40 hours per week.

### Housing

This is the biggest variable. Some parks provide housing. Many do not.

**When housing is provided:**
- Rent is deducted from your paycheck (typically $200-500/month)
- Quality ranges from dorm rooms to shared cabins to trailer parks
- Expect shared quarters with other seasonal employees
- Some parks have waitlists for housing
- Housing may include utilities or charge separately

**When housing is not provided:**
- You find your own accommodations near the park
- Gateway communities often have limited and expensive rentals
- Six-month leases are hard to find
- RV living is possible near some parks if you have a rig
- Some seasonals commute long distances

The housing situation is a genuine crisis across the NPS. Parks cannot hire enough staff because there is nowhere for them to live. Remote parks in expensive tourist areas are hit hardest. At Acadia National Park, for example, the park has about 85 beds for 150+ seasonal positions. Before accepting a position, ask directly: Is housing available? If not, what do current employees do?

### Startup Costs

- **Travel to the park:** You pay your own way
- **Uniforms:** Some items provided, others purchased. Expect $100-300 for boots, appropriate clothing
- **Housing deposit:** First month's rent plus deposit if no park housing
- **Vehicle:** Many locations require personal transportation

### Benefits

Seasonal employees working expected schedules may qualify for:
- **Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB):** Available if you work 130+ hours per month for 90+ days. Government pays 72-75% of premiums.
- **Annual leave:** Accrued at 4 hours per pay period
- **Sick leave:** Accrued at 4 hours per pay period
- **Federal holidays:** Paid if the holiday falls on a scheduled workday

**What seasonal employees do NOT get:**
- Retirement benefits (no FERS contributions)
- Life insurance eligibility in most cases
- Job security beyond the season

### Hidden Costs

- **Isolation expenses:** Remote parks mean long drives for groceries, limited internet, few entertainment options
- **Gear:** Outdoor work requires appropriate clothing, boots, rain gear
- **Vehicle maintenance:** Rough roads and long commutes add up
- **Healthcare gaps:** Coverage may lapse between seasons

## How to Get Started

1. **Create a USAJOBS account at usajobs.gov.** This is the only way to apply for federal jobs. Complete your profile with education, work history, and contact information.

2. **Build your resume to federal standards.** Federal resumes differ from private sector resumes. Include month/year dates for all positions. Specify hours worked per week. List supervisor names and phone numbers. Detail your duties in specific terms. Maximum two pages for seasonal positions.

3. **Search for NPS positions.** Go to usajobs.gov and search "National Park Service." Filter by "Temporary" work type. Browse open announcements.

4. **Understand the hiring timeline.** Summer positions open October through February. Winter positions open July through August. Popular parks fill early. Check daily during hiring windows.

5. **Apply to multiple parks.** Your odds improve dramatically when you apply broadly. Smaller parks and less-famous sites receive fewer applications. Consider parks you have never heard of.

6. **Complete the assessment questionnaire honestly.** Each application includes questions about your qualifications. Your answers are compared to your resume. Inflating your responses can disqualify you.

7. **Wait.** Federal hiring takes weeks to months. You receive an acknowledgment email. Later, you may receive notification of referral to the hiring manager. Then, if selected, a phone or video interview.

8. **Respond quickly to interview requests.** Hiring managers often contact multiple candidates. Delayed responses cost opportunities.

9. **Accept and complete onboarding.** Selected candidates complete background checks, paperwork, and orientation before starting.

**Pro tip:** Mark your calendar for October 1. This is when summer hiring ramps up. Set USAJOBS alerts for "National Park Service" and check daily. Announcements open and close quickly.

## The Hard Parts

**Competition is fierce.** The most desirable parks receive hundreds of applications for each opening. Yellowstone, Yosemite, Grand Canyon, Acadia—everyone wants these. Your chances improve at lesser-known sites, but popular parks are brutal.

**The federal application process is confusing.** USAJOBS is not intuitive. Requirements are written in bureaucratic language. Self-assessment questionnaires feel like traps. Many qualified people give up or make disqualifying mistakes. Budget time to learn the system.

**Housing shortages are real.** The NPS has a well-documented housing crisis. Parks want to hire more staff but cannot house them. Acadia National Park, for example, has 85 beds for 150+ seasonal positions. Some parks have no housing at all. This limits who can realistically take these jobs.

**Remote locations mean isolation.** Many parks are hours from the nearest town. Limited cell service. Slow internet. No restaurants, bars, or entertainment. You share a small community with coworkers. Not everyone thrives in this environment.

**The work is temporary by design.** Seasonal positions end. Every year, you reapply. There is no job security until you land a permanent position—which can take years of seasonal work. Some people do five or more seasons before converting.

**Pay does not match the cost of living.** GS-4 and GS-5 wages are modest. In expensive gateway towns, finding affordable housing on seasonal wages is nearly impossible without park-provided housing.

**Physical demands are real.** Maintenance work means manual labor in heat, cold, rain, and altitude. Even interpretation positions require standing for hours and hiking to program locations. This is not desk work.

**Visitor interactions can be difficult.** You answer the same questions hundreds of times per day. Some visitors are rude, entitled, or aggressive. Managing crowds during peak season is exhausting.

## Where This Can Lead

### Pathways to Permanent Employment

**Land Management Workforce Flexibility Act (LMWFA)**
After 24 months of cumulative seasonal work without a break longer than two years, you gain competitive eligibility for permanent federal positions. This is the primary pathway from seasonal to permanent status. You can apply for permanent positions across the NPS, Forest Service, BLM, and other land management agencies with hiring preference.

**Pathways Program**
For current students and recent graduates. Internships through this program can convert to permanent positions upon completion.

**Direct Hire Authority**
Some positions in high-demand fields allow agencies to hire directly without full competitive process.

**Building Your File**
Each successful season adds to your federal employment history. Positive performance evaluations, expanding responsibilities, and good references from supervisors matter. The path is long but well-traveled.

### Skills That Transfer

- **Public speaking:** Interpretation work builds presentation skills
- **Customer service:** Visitor interactions teach patience and communication
- **Emergency response:** First aid, search and rescue, crisis management
- **Outdoor skills:** Trail work, land management, wildlife awareness
- **Federal employment knowledge:** Understanding how the government works

### Common Next Steps

- **More seasons:** Many people do 3-5 seasons while working toward permanent status
- **Other agencies:** Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, Fish and Wildlife, state parks
- **Concessioner work:** Companies like Xanterra, Aramark, and Delaware North run lodges and services in parks
- **Graduate school:** Experience strengthens applications for natural resource programs
- **Local government:** County and city parks departments value NPS experience
- **Environmental nonprofits:** Conservation organizations recruit from seasonal staff

### The Realistic Timeline

Year 1: First seasonal position, likely GS-3 or GS-4
Years 2-3: Return to same park or try others, build experience and references
Years 4-5: Begin qualifying for LMWFA, apply for permanent positions
Years 5+: Possible conversion to permanent status if positions open and you compete successfully

Some people convert faster. Many take longer. Some never convert and either make peace with seasonal life or move on.
