# Wildlife Rehabilitation

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** Pending

## Overview

Wildlife rehabilitation is the practice of rescuing, treating, and releasing injured, orphaned, or sick wild animals. You stabilize an injured hawk. You bottle-feed orphaned squirrels. You nurse a poisoned owl back to health. You release a healed fox into the woods.

This opportunity might work for young adults because the barrier to entry is volunteering—not degrees or tuition. Almost everyone in wildlife rehabilitation started by volunteering at a local rehabilitation center. You learn through direct experience under licensed rehabilitators. The work is hands-on from day one.

The catch: almost no one makes a living doing this. Wildlife rehabilitation is largely volunteer work. Most rehabilitators hold unrelated day jobs and care for animals in their spare time, often paying expenses out of pocket. Paid positions exist—mostly at larger rehabilitation centers—but they are rare and competitive. The average salary for paid positions ranges from $30,000 to $63,000, with entry-level roles on the lower end.

If your goal is full-time paid wildlife work, rehabilitation is best understood as a stepping stone. The skills, certifications, and connections you build can lead to careers in conservation, wildlife biology, or veterinary medicine. But the rehabilitation work itself rarely pays the bills.

## What You'll Do

A typical day at a wildlife rehabilitation center involves:

**Animal Care (The Core Work)**

- Feed animals on species-appropriate schedules—some baby birds need feeding every 15-30 minutes during daylight hours
- Prepare diets—thawing rodents for raptors, mixing formula for orphaned mammals, chopping produce for herbivores
- Administer medications as prescribed by veterinarians
- Clean and sanitize enclosures, cages, and hospital areas
- Monitor patients for changes in condition, appetite, or behavior
- Document everything—intake records, feeding logs, medical observations
- Move animals between enclosures as they progress through rehabilitation

**Behind the Scenes**

- Wash dishes and prepare feeding equipment—a lot of dishes
- Do laundry—towels, cage liners, bedding
- Answer phone calls from the public about injured wildlife
- Transport animals to veterinary appointments or release sites
- Maintain outdoor flight cages and enclosures
- Order and organize supplies

**When You Progress**

- Capture and handle animals safely—raptors require specialized handling techniques
- Assess incoming animals and provide initial stabilization
- Assist veterinarians during examinations and procedures
- Conduct physical therapy for animals recovering from injuries
- Make release decisions based on behavior and flight tests
- Train new volunteers

The work happens every single day. Animals do not take weekends off. During baby season—roughly March through September—centers are flooded with orphaned animals requiring round-the-clock care. Expect early mornings, late nights, and no days off during peak season if you are fostering animals at home.

One rehabilitator described the reality: "You will spend more time doing dishes and laundry than you ever imagined. And you will love it because those dishes fed a baby raccoon back to health."

## Requirements

**Age**

- You must be 18 years old to obtain your own rehabilitation permit in most states
- Volunteers at rehabilitation centers can often start at 16 with parental consent
- Federal migratory bird permits require you to be at least 18

**Experience Before Licensing**

Getting your own permit requires documented hands-on experience. Requirements vary by state but typically include:

- 6 months to 2 years working under a licensed rehabilitator
- 100-1,000 hours of direct experience with wildlife
- Training during spring or summer baby season
- A sponsoring veterinarian willing to provide medical support

For federal migratory bird permits specifically, you need:

- Minimum 100 hours of hands-on experience per bird type (songbirds, raptors, water birds)
- That experience must be gained over at least one full year
- Up to 20 hours can come from approved courses or seminars

**Physical Requirements**

- Ability to lift 40 pounds—injured deer and geese are heavy
- Comfortable working outdoors in all weather conditions
- Ability to bend, stoop, and climb
- Willingness to work with blood, bodily fluids, and deceased animals

**Other Considerations**

- Some animals carry zoonotic diseases—rabies, salmonella, avian influenza
- Tetanus vaccination is strongly recommended
- Some facilities require background checks
- Your own vehicle is usually necessary for transport duties

## Training Provided

Volunteer training at rehabilitation centers is typically on-the-job learning. You shadow experienced staff, then gradually take on more responsibility. Most centers provide:

- Orientation to facility procedures and safety protocols
- Species identification and natural history basics
- Proper feeding techniques and diet preparation
- Cage cleaning and disease prevention
- Safe handling techniques (after you demonstrate competence)

**Formal Certification Options**

Two organizations provide recognized credentials:

**International Wildlife Rehabilitation Council (IWRC)**

The IWRC offers courses and the Certified Wildlife Rehabilitator (CWR) designation.

- Foundations of Wildlife Rehabilitation course: $248-$307 (includes books, lab kit)
- Online or in-person options available
- CWR certification requires 100 hours of continuing education and passing an exam
- Certification must be renewed every two years

**National Wildlife Rehabilitators Association (NWRA)**

The NWRA offers courses at their annual symposium and throughout the year.

- Principles of Wildlife Rehabilitation: Basic Skills course offered at symposiums
- Membership provides access to resources, networking, and job boards
- Annual symposium includes hands-on labs and workshops

**State-Required Training**

Many states require completion of an approved course before issuing a permit. Check your state wildlife agency for specific requirements.

**Species-Specific Training**

Specializing in certain animals requires additional training:

- Raptors require passing a separate raptor rehabilitation exam
- Marine mammals require authorization through NOAA's Marine Mammal Health and Stranding Response Program
- Some states have separate requirements for rabies-vector species (bats, raccoons, foxes, skunks)

## The Money Reality

### Pay

This is the hard truth about wildlife rehabilitation: most rehabilitators work for free.

**Volunteer Positions (The Majority)**

- No compensation
- You often pay for supplies, food, and veterinary care out of pocket
- Annual costs for home-based rehabilitators can run $1,000-$5,000+ depending on caseload

**Paid Positions (Rare, Usually at Larger Centers)**

- Wildlife Care Technician: $15-$18/hour ($31,000-$37,500/year)
- Wildlife Rehabilitator: $16-$25/hour ($33,000-$52,000/year)
- Wildlife Center Manager: $40,000-$60,000/year
- The national average reported on salary sites is around $63,000, but this includes senior positions at major facilities

**Internships**

Some larger rehabilitation centers offer paid internships or provide housing:

- Wildlife Center of Virginia offers internships with housing on-site
- Marine Mammal Center offers internships with stipends
- Many internships are unpaid but provide valuable experience

### Housing

- Not typically provided for volunteers
- Some internship programs include housing
- If fostering animals at home, you need a dedicated space separate from your living quarters

### Startup Costs

**To Volunteer**

- Transportation to the center: Your own gas money
- Clothing you can ruin: Work boots, old clothes
- Volunteer training: Usually free

**To Get Licensed (Home-Based)**

- IWRC Foundations course: $250-$310
- State permit application fee: $0-$50 (varies by state)
- Federal migratory bird permit: $50 application fee
- Enclosures and caging: $500-$5,000+ depending on species
- Medical supplies and equipment: $200-$500 to start
- Food and formula: Ongoing cost, $50-$200/month during active season

**Hidden Costs**

- Veterinary bills when animals need professional care
- Electricity for heating pads, incubators, and refrigerators full of frozen mice
- Water bills from constant cleaning
- Damage to your home and property from housing wild animals
- Emotional cost of animal deaths—you cannot quantify this

### Benefits

Paid positions at established centers typically include:

- Health insurance
- Paid time off
- Professional development opportunities

Volunteer positions offer:

- Experience that counts toward permit requirements
- Networking with conservation professionals
- Training and skill development
- Letters of recommendation for wildlife careers

## How to Get Started

1. **Find rehabilitation centers near you.** Use the Humane Society's directory at [humaneworld.org](https://www.humaneworld.org/en/resources/find-wildlife-rescue-and). Your state wildlife agency also maintains lists of licensed rehabilitators. Search "[your state] wildlife rehabilitator directory."

2. **Contact 2-3 centers and ask about volunteer opportunities.** Email or call. Explain that you are interested in learning wildlife rehabilitation. Ask about their volunteer program, training, and time commitment requirements.

3. **Complete volunteer orientation and training.** Most centers require an orientation session before you start. This may include online modules, in-person training, or both.

4. **Commit to a regular schedule.** Typical volunteer commitments are one 4-6 hour shift per week for at least 6 months. Show up reliably. Animals depend on consistent care.

5. **Learn everything you can.** Observe. Ask questions. Take notes. Volunteer in different areas—intake, nursery, outdoor enclosures, hospital. Understand the full scope of rehabilitation.

6. **Take formal training courses.** Enroll in the IWRC Foundations of Wildlife Rehabilitation course online at [theiwrc.org/courses](https://theiwrc.org/courses/). This course is recognized by most states and counts toward permit requirements.

7. **Research your state's permit requirements.** Every state has different rules. Find your state wildlife agency's rehabilitation page and download the permit application requirements. Some states require exams, facility inspections, and veterinary sponsors.

8. **Develop a relationship with a veterinarian.** You will need a licensed veterinarian willing to provide medical support for your rehabilitation work. Start building that relationship early.

9. **Decide whether to specialize.** Some rehabilitators focus on specific species—raptors, songbirds, marine mammals, or native mammals. Specialization can make you more valuable to centers and inform your permit applications.

10. **Apply for your state permit when eligible.** Once you have the required hours, training, facility, and veterinary support, submit your application. Budget 2-8 weeks for processing.

11. **Apply for federal migratory bird permit if rehabilitating birds.** Submit Form 3-200-10b through the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service at [fws.gov/service/3-200-10b-migratory-bird-rehabilitation](https://www.fws.gov/service/3-200-10b-migratory-bird-rehabilitation). You must already have a valid state permit.

## The Hard Parts

**Animals die. A lot.**

This is the hardest part. Many animals arrive too injured, too sick, or too young to survive. Mortality rates vary by species but can exceed 50% for some categories of patients. You will do everything right and the animal will still die. You will euthanize animals when there is no hope of recovery. You will make that decision repeatedly. The grief accumulates.

One rehabilitator described it: "One day all the death in a wildlife rehabilitator's workplace caught up with me." Compassion fatigue is real. Burnout is common. You need strategies for processing loss.

**The work is constant during baby season.**

From March through September, orphaned animals flood in. Baby songbirds need feeding every 15-30 minutes. Baby squirrels need formula every few hours. If you are fostering animals at home, you may not sleep through the night for weeks. There are no days off.

**You pay for the privilege.**

Most rehabilitators subsidize their work from their own income. Food, supplies, veterinary bills—these costs fall on you. A single injured raptor requiring surgery can cost $1,000-$3,000 in vet bills. Multiply that by dozens of animals per year.

**The legal liability is real.**

You are handling wild animals that can bite, scratch, and transmit disease. You are responsible for preventing the spread of avian influenza, maintaining proper permits, and documenting everything. If you release an animal that should have been euthanized and it suffers, that is on you.

**Facilities and inspections are demanding.**

Running your own licensed operation requires dedicated space, proper caging, and regular inspections. Your home must be suitable for housing wild animals. Some species require flight cages, pools, or specialized enclosures that cost thousands of dollars to build.

**The isolation can be intense.**

Wildlife rehabilitation is not a social career. You spend hours alone caring for animals that cannot thank you. The public often does not understand the work. Your friends may not want to hear about the baby bird that died in your hands last night.

**Career advancement is limited.**

There is no clear ladder to climb. Paid positions are rare. Senior rehabilitators may earn $50,000-$60,000 after decades of experience, but many never reach paid positions at all. This is passion work, not career work.

## Where This Can Lead

Wildlife rehabilitation can be a stepping stone to several career paths:

**Wildlife Conservation**

The skills transfer: species identification, animal handling, habitat knowledge, working with wildlife agencies. Your network includes conservation professionals who can recommend you for positions.

- Wildlife technician positions with state and federal agencies
- Field research assistant roles
- Conservation organization staff positions

**Veterinary Medicine**

Many veterinarians who specialize in wildlife or exotic animals started in rehabilitation. Your experience demonstrates commitment and practical skills.

- Veterinary technician or assistant roles
- Pre-veterinary experience for vet school applications
- Specialization in wildlife or zoo medicine

**Wildlife Education**

Teaching the public about wildlife is a natural extension of rehabilitation work.

- Nature center educator positions
- Zoos and aquariums (see the zookeeper opportunity)
- Environmental education nonprofits

**Running Your Own Licensed Facility**

Some rehabilitators eventually operate their own centers. This requires:

- Years of experience and a track record of successful releases
- A suitable property with space for enclosures
- A network of veterinary support
- Ability to fundraise or self-fund operations
- Business skills to manage a nonprofit organization

**Related Government Positions**

- Conservation officer / wildlife officer
- Wildlife biologist (typically requires a degree)
- Fish and wildlife technician

**The Honest Reality**

For most people, wildlife rehabilitation remains a volunteer activity alongside a paying career. The rehabbers who make it a paid career often do so through:

- Working at large rehabilitation centers or zoos
- Combining rehabilitation with wildlife education
- Building a nonprofit organization over many years
- Pursuing veterinary medicine with a wildlife focus

The skills you develop—patience, crisis management, species expertise, public education, record-keeping—transfer to many fields. But the rehabilitation work itself rarely becomes the job.
