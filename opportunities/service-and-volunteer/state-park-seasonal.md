# State Park Seasonal Positions

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** Claude (Opus 4.5)

## Overview

State parks across all 50 states hire thousands of seasonal workers each year. These positions cover the same roles as federal parks—visitor services, maintenance, interpretation, lifeguarding—but with a more accessible application process.

This opportunity works for young adults because state parks are generally easier to get into than federal National Park Service or Forest Service jobs. Most states offer entry-level positions with few experience and educational requirements. Many positions accept applicants at age 18 or even 16. The application process is simpler than the federal USAJOBS system. And some states provide housing for seasonal staff.

The catch: pay is often lower than federal positions. Housing availability varies wildly by state and individual park. Benefits for seasonal workers are limited or nonexistent in many states. And like all seasonal work, the job ends when the season does. You may work 4-9 months and then need another plan.

State parks offer an excellent entry point into outdoor careers. The experience transfers to federal positions. The competition is less intense than NPS jobs. And you can start building relevant work history immediately.

## What You'll Do

Your daily work depends on the position type and park. State parks fill seasonal roles across several divisions.

**Visitor Services / Park Aide**
- Staff entrance stations and collect fees
- Provide information to visitors about park features
- Work at camp check-in stations
- Sell park passes and permits
- Answer questions—the same ones, hundreds of times per day
- Handle basic visitor complaints and requests

**Maintenance**
- Clean restrooms and facilities daily
- Pick up litter and empty trash receptacles
- Groundskeeping: mowing, trimming, clearing brush
- Trail maintenance and repair
- Basic building repairs
- Prepare campsites and picnic areas for visitors

**Interpretation / Naturalist**
- Lead guided walks and educational programs
- Develop and deliver campfire talks
- Work with school groups and youth programs
- Create interpretive materials and displays
- Staff nature centers and answer questions about wildlife, plants, and park history

**Lifeguarding**
- Monitor swimming areas for safety
- Perform water rescues when needed
- Enforce beach and pool rules
- Administer first aid
- Maintain rescue equipment
- Work in sometimes extreme heat for hours

**Park Ranger (Entry-Level)**
- Patrol park grounds
- Enforce park regulations
- Respond to emergencies and accidents
- Assist with wildlife management
- Complete paperwork and reports
- Handle campground issues

A typical maintenance shift starts early—often 6 or 7 AM—and involves physical outdoor work regardless of weather. You may clean restrooms, haul trash, mow grass, repair fences, and respond to whatever the park needs that day. Visitor services shifts center on the entrance station or visitor center, where you greet arrivals, collect fees, answer questions, and solve problems.

## Requirements

Requirements vary by state, but most entry-level seasonal positions share common qualifications.

- **Age:** Most positions require 18 or older. Some states hire at 16-17 for certain roles. Lifeguards often require minimum age of 15-16. Park ranger positions in some states (like Colorado) require age 21.
- **Education:** High school diploma or GED typically required. No college degree needed for most seasonal roles.
- **Driver's License:** Required for many positions. You may need to operate park vehicles.
- **State Residency:** Some states prefer or require state residents. Others hire from anywhere.
- **Background Check:** Required by all states. Criminal history review is standard.
- **Physical Ability:** Maintenance and trail work require lifting 50+ pounds, working in extreme temperatures, and sustained physical activity. Desk-based positions have fewer physical demands.
- **Certifications:** Lifeguard positions require current certifications in lifeguarding, CPR, first aid, and AED. American Red Cross certification is most common. Some states provide this training.

**Lifeguard-Specific Requirements:**
- Current American Red Cross Lifeguard certification (or equivalent)
- CPR for Professional Rescuer
- First Aid certification
- Waterfront module for lake/ocean positions
- Physical ability to rescue struggling swimmers
- Ability to work in sun and heat for extended periods

**Interpreter-Specific Requirements:**
- Comfort with public speaking
- Knowledge of natural history, wildlife, or cultural topics (can be learned on the job)
- Ability to engage diverse audiences including children
- No degree required, but coursework in biology, ecology, environmental education, or history is helpful

## Training Provided

Training varies by state and position. Expect:

- Park orientation covering safety, emergency procedures, and policies
- Position-specific training for your role
- First aid and CPR for many positions
- Customer service training for visitor-facing roles
- Equipment operation training for maintenance workers

**Florida:** Park rangers attend a weeklong Ranger Academy covering all aspects of park operations—maintenance, administration, visitor services, protection, and resource management.

**California:** Lifeguards receive 8 days of paid training in public safety first aid, CPR, AED, and open water lifesaving techniques.

**Oregon:** New seasonal staff receive comprehensive orientation and on-the-job training from experienced rangers.

**Credentials earned:** Most training is internal and does not lead to transferable certifications. Exceptions include first aid, CPR, and occasionally specialized certifications like chainsaw operation. The real value is experience working in parks, which transfers to future positions.

## The Money Reality

### Pay

State park seasonal wages vary significantly by state and position type.

| State | Seasonal Position | Hourly Rate (2025-2026) |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------|
| California | Park Aide | $19.00-$27.00 |
| New York | Park & Recreation Aide | $15.97-$24.13 |
| Oregon | Ranger Assistant | $19.78 |
| Oregon | Seasonal Ranger | $23.21 |
| Colorado | Seasonal Positions | $17.00-$19.00 |
| Georgia | Seasonal Interpreter | $11.00-$12.00 |
| Minnesota | Seasonal | $16.00-$18.00 |

On average, seasonal workers make $12-$24 per hour depending on the state, specific position, and experience. Entry-level positions typically start at $11-$16 per hour. Experienced seasonal workers and those in higher-cost states earn $18-$27 per hour.

Overtime may be available during peak season. Some states pay time-and-a-half after 40 hours.

### Housing

Housing is the biggest variable and often the biggest challenge.

**When housing is provided:**
- Rent is typically deducted from your paycheck
- Quality varies from modern dormitories to basic cabins
- Expect shared quarters with other seasonal staff
- Some parks have limited spaces and waitlists
- Minnesota's Itasca State Park and northeast region parks offer seasonal housing
- Some Georgia state parks provide free housing

**When housing is not provided:**
- You find your own accommodations near the park
- Short-term rentals near parks are often expensive and scarce
- Some seasonals live in RVs or converted vans
- Commuting long distances is common
- The housing search can be harder than getting the job

**The reality:** Most state parks do not provide housing for seasonal staff. Unlike some federal positions, housing is the exception rather than the rule. Ask specifically about housing availability before accepting a position. If the park does not provide housing, ask what current employees do. Some parks have informal arrangements or can connect you with local landlords.

### Startup Costs

- **Travel to the park:** You pay your own relocation costs
- **Housing deposit:** If finding your own housing, expect first/last month rent plus deposit
- **Uniforms:** Some items provided, others purchased. Budget $50-$200 for appropriate footwear and clothing
- **Vehicle:** Essential for most parks. Reliable transportation required for commuting if no housing provided
- **Certifications:** Lifeguard certification courses cost $200-$400 if not already certified

### Benefits

Benefits for seasonal state employees vary dramatically by state.

**Oregon stands out:** Both seasonal rangers and ranger assistants receive comprehensive medical, vision, and dental plans for employees and qualified family members. They also receive paid sick leave, vacation, personal leave, and 11 paid holidays per year.

**Most states offer minimal benefits to seasonal staff:**
- Some paid sick leave (varies by state)
- Possible holiday pay if working on holidays
- No retirement benefits
- No health insurance or limited enrollment options

Check the specific state's employee benefits information. Some states have recently improved benefits for seasonal workers. Others provide almost nothing beyond the hourly wage.

### Hidden Costs

- **Housing shortages:** The biggest hidden cost is potentially not finding affordable housing
- **Isolation expenses:** Remote parks mean long drives for groceries and supplies
- **Weather gear:** Outdoor work requires rain gear, sun protection, and layers
- **Vehicle wear:** Gravel roads and long commutes add maintenance costs
- **Off-season survival:** You need a plan for income when the season ends

## How to Get Started

**1. Identify which states you can work in.** Some states prefer residents. Others hire from anywhere. If you have flexibility on location, you have more options.

**2. Go directly to state park job websites.** Each state has its own application system. Here are several with strong seasonal programs:

- California: parks.ca.gov/jobs
- New York: parks.ny.gov/employment
- Oregon: stateparks.oregon.gov (use Workday)
- Texas: tpwd.texas.gov/jobs
- Colorado: cpw.state.co.us/careers
- Minnesota: dnr.state.mn.us/careers
- Florida: jobs.myflorida.com
- Washington: parks.wa.gov/about-us/jobs

**3. Apply early.** Application windows vary by state:
- Minnesota accepts applications February through September
- Tennessee hiring peaks in February and March
- Oregon posts positions January through June on a rolling basis
- New York accepts applications throughout the year
- Most summer positions are filled by April

**4. Create accounts on state job portals.** Most states use centralized government job websites. California uses CalCareers. New York has StateJobsNY. Oregon uses Workday. Set up accounts before positions open.

**5. No exam needed for most seasonal positions.** Unlike permanent government jobs, seasonal positions at state parks typically skip the examination process. California explicitly states no exam is required for seasonal work.

**6. Apply to multiple parks and regions.** Your odds improve when you apply broadly. Select multiple locations if the application allows. Less popular parks receive fewer applications.

**7. Be flexible on position type.** Maintenance jobs are often easier to get than interpretation roles. Take what you can get to build experience. You can change roles in future seasons.

**8. Contact parks directly.** If no positions are posted, call or email the park you are interested in. Ask about upcoming seasonal hiring. Some positions are advertised locally or filled before posting online.

**9. Check CoolWorks.com.** This site aggregates seasonal outdoor jobs including state park positions. It covers Ohio, Washington, Michigan, Alaska, South Dakota, Florida, and others.

## The Hard Parts

**Pay is modest.** Most seasonal positions pay $15-20 per hour. In expensive areas, this does not go far—especially if you must pay for housing out of pocket.

**Housing is often your problem.** Unlike federal positions at some parks, most state parks do not provide employee housing. Finding a short-term rental near a rural park is difficult. You may end up commuting long distances or living in less-than-ideal conditions.

**The work is physically demanding.** Maintenance crews work outdoors in heat, cold, rain, and humidity. You will clean restrooms, haul trash, and perform manual labor. Desk jobs exist but are fewer.

**You answer the same questions constantly.** Visitor services means greeting hundreds of people daily. "Where are the restrooms?" "Can I see a bear?" "Is this trail hard?" Patience is essential.

**Seasons end.** Most positions run 4-9 months. When the season ends, so does your income. You need a plan for the off-season—another seasonal job, savings, or returning home.

**Benefits are limited.** Most states do not offer health insurance, retirement, or meaningful benefits to seasonal staff. Oregon is an exception. Check your state carefully.

**Advancement is slow.** Converting from seasonal to permanent can take years. You may work multiple seasons before a full-time position opens—if one opens at all.

**Dealing with difficult visitors.** Some visitors are demanding, rude, or entitled. You must remain professional. Handling conflict is part of the job.

**Remote locations mean isolation.** Parks in rural areas are far from cities. Limited cell service. Few entertainment options. Small communities where everyone knows everyone.

## Where This Can Lead

### Pathways to Permanent State Employment

State park systems promote from within. Seasonal experience is often the first step to permanent positions. Many current full-time rangers started as seasonal staff.

Building a track record of successful seasons—showing up on time, working hard, being reliable—creates opportunities. Supervisors remember good employees when permanent positions open.

### Pathway to Federal Positions

State park experience counts when applying for federal jobs with the National Park Service, U.S. Forest Service, or Bureau of Land Management. The skills transfer directly:

- Visitor services experience
- Facility maintenance skills
- Interpretation and education program delivery
- Emergency response training
- Understanding of public land management

Federal positions are harder to get but offer better pay and benefits. State park work gives you the experience to compete for those jobs.

### Skills That Transfer

- **Customer service:** Handling visitors teaches patience and communication
- **Public speaking:** Interpretation work builds presentation skills
- **Outdoor work skills:** Trail maintenance, equipment operation, groundskeeping
- **Emergency response:** First aid, incident management, crisis communication
- **Physical fitness:** Manual labor keeps you in shape

### Common Next Steps

- **More seasons at the same park:** Build relationships and become the first call for next year
- **Seasonal work at different parks:** Explore new regions and park types
- **Federal seasonal positions:** Apply to NPS, USFS, or BLM with your experience
- **Permanent state positions:** Apply for full-time ranger or maintenance jobs
- **Environmental nonprofits:** Conservation organizations value park experience
- **Private outdoor industry:** Guide companies, outdoor education programs, recreation companies
- **Graduate school:** Experience strengthens applications for natural resource or recreation programs

### The Realistic Timeline

Year 1: First seasonal position, entry-level. Learn the work, prove reliability.
Years 2-3: Return to same park or explore others. Build references. Possibly move into better seasonal roles.
Years 3-5: Apply for permanent state positions or federal seasonal work. Competition increases.
Years 5+: Possible conversion to permanent status if positions open.

Some people move faster. Others treat seasonal work as a lifestyle rather than a career ladder. Both are valid paths.
