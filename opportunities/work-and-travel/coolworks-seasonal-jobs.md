# Coolworks Seasonal Jobs

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-08
> **Fact Checker:** Claude Opus 4.5

## Overview

Coolworks is a job board that connects people with seasonal work in national parks, ski resorts, dude ranches, summer camps, fishing lodges, and other outdoor destinations. It is not an employer. Coolworks lists jobs from hundreds of employers across the United States.

This matters for young adults because many of these jobs provide housing. When you have limited funds and want to travel, a job that includes a place to live solves two problems at once. You earn money while experiencing places most people only visit on vacation.

The trade-off is clear. These are entry-level jobs with modest pay. You will not build savings quickly. But you will have minimal expenses, meet people from around the world, and live in places like Yellowstone, Grand Teton, or a ski resort in Colorado.

## What You'll Do

The work depends on the employer and location. Here are the most common job categories:

**Hospitality and Food Service**
- Housekeeping in lodges and hotels
- Front desk and guest services
- Food service—dishwashing, cooking, serving
- Bartending

**Outdoor Operations**
- Lift operator at ski resorts
- Trail crew and maintenance
- Wrangler at dude ranches
- Fishing guide assistant

**Retail and Guest Experience**
- Gift shop attendant
- Tour guide
- Recreation attendant
- Activity instructor at camps

**Specialized Roles**
- Camp counselor
- Kitchen management
- Maintenance technician
- CDL driver in Alaska

The daily reality: you work 30-50 hours per week doing tasks that are often repetitive. Housekeeping means cleaning the same rooms day after day. Dishwashing means standing at a sink for hours. Food service means rush periods where you move constantly, followed by slow periods of prep work.

What makes it different from a regular job is the location. You finish your shift and walk into Yellowstone. Your day off means hiking a trail that tourists drive hours to reach. Your coworkers become your social circle because you all live and work in the same place.

## Requirements

**Age and Eligibility**
- Minimum age: 18 for most positions, 21 for some roles involving alcohol service or driving
- Must be legally authorized to work in the United States
- Background check required by most employers

**Skills Needed**
- Most positions require no prior experience
- Customer service attitude matters more than specific skills
- Reliability is essential—employers are in remote locations and cannot easily replace workers who quit
- Physical stamina for on-your-feet work

**Physical Requirements**
- Ability to stand for 8+ hours
- Lifting up to 50 pounds for many roles
- Working at altitude in mountain locations
- Outdoor work in variable weather conditions

**Time Commitment**
- Summer season: May through September (some positions start as early as March)
- Winter season: November through April
- Year-round positions exist but are less common
- Most employers expect a full season commitment—3 to 6 months minimum

## Training Provided

Training is on-the-job and position-specific. You will learn:
- Company procedures and guest service standards
- Safety protocols for your specific role
- Food handling certification if working in food service (usually employer-provided)
- Equipment operation for specialized roles

This training does not lead to recognized credentials in most cases. The exception is if you work in a role that requires specific certifications—food handler permits, CDL licenses, or outdoor guide certifications. Some employers will help you obtain these.

The real value is in transferable skills: customer service experience, ability to work in a team, problem-solving in a fast-paced environment, and experience living independently in a new place.

## The Money Reality

### Pay by Job Type

**Entry-Level Hospitality (housekeeping, dishwashing, food service)**
- $14-18 per hour depending on location and role
- Yellowstone National Park Lodges: $15.70-16.25 per hour for summer season (April-October 2026)
- Yellowstone winter positions: $20.75-22.75 per hour (higher due to harsher conditions)

**Lift Operators at Ski Resorts**
- $18-28 per hour depending on experience and location
- Most common range: $20-22 per hour
- Vail Resorts: $20-22 per hour typically (as of February 2026)

**Summer Camp Counselors**
- $365-440 per week for overnight camps (some camps pay lower)
- Housing and meals included
- End-of-season bonuses common at many camps
- Hourly equivalent roughly $12-17 per hour

**Dude Ranch Staff**
- $300-500 per week plus room and board
- Tips possible for guest-facing roles

**Alaska Fishing Lodges**
- Pay varies widely by position and lodge
- Deckhands and fishing industry: average $24-26 per hour
- Meals and lodging typically included
- Salmon fishing season: earnings of $10,000-50,000 possible over 3 months depending on role
- Crab fishing and specialized roles pay significantly more

**Alaska Cannery Work**
- $1,200+ per week during peak season
- 16-hour days, 7 days a week when salmon are running
- Up to $7,000 per month at peak
- Overtime paid after 8 hours/day or 40 hours/week at time-and-a-half

### Housing

Most seasonal employers provide housing. This is the key benefit. Here is what "housing provided" actually means:

**Dormitory-Style (most common)**
- Shared room with 1-3 other employees
- Communal bathrooms
- Basic furniture—bed, desk, limited storage
- No private space

**Cost Deductions**
- Yellowstone (Xanterra): $17 per day for housing AND all meals combined (deducted from paycheck)
- This works out to roughly $510 per month for room and board together
- Yellowstone (YPSS): $7.50 per day for housing only (meals separate)
- Grand Teton: Free dormitory housing, but mandatory meal plan at $112 per week ($448/month)
- Grand Teton (Jackson Hole Golf & Tennis): $98-119 per week for rooms
- Some employers: free housing with meal plan required

**What is Included**
- Utilities
- Wi-Fi (often limited bandwidth)
- Laundry facilities
- Cafeteria meals at subsidized rates

**Quality Reality**
- Housing units average 61 years old in National Park Service locations
- Expect basic, functional, and sometimes dated facilities
- Singles housing is rarely available
- Couples housing sometimes available but limited

### Startup Costs

- Travel to the job location: $50-500 depending on distance
- Initial supplies before first paycheck: $100-200
- Deposit for housing or meals if required: $0-300 (typically deducted from first paycheck)

### Benefits

- Free or discounted access to park and resort amenities
- Ski resorts often provide free season passes
- Discounts on food, retail, and activities
- Health insurance available from some larger employers (Xanterra, Delaware North)
- End-of-season bonuses at some locations

### Hidden Costs

- Remoteness means expensive grocery runs or eating at the employee cafeteria for every meal
- Limited cell service may require WiFi calling or different phone plan
- If you leave early, you may owe housing costs or travel reimbursement
- Off-season gaps mean no income for 1-3 months between seasons

## How to Get Started

1. **Create a Coolworks account.** Go to coolworks.com and sign up. This is free. You can save searches, favorite employers, and get alerts for new postings.

2. **Filter for housing.** Click "Jobs with Housing" to see only positions that provide a place to live. This is essential for young adults's situation.

3. **Research the hiring timeline.** Summer jobs: apply January through March. Winter jobs: apply July through September. Earlier is better.

4. **Pick your locations and job types.** Use the search filters to narrow by state, park, or job category. Read through employer profiles—they explain housing, pay, and the work environment.

5. **Apply directly through each employer.** Coolworks lists the jobs but you apply through the employer's own system. Some have online applications. Some want a resume and cover letter. Follow their specific instructions.

6. **Apply to multiple positions.** Competition is real, especially for popular parks like Yellowstone and Grand Teton. Apply to 5-10 positions to increase your odds.

7. **Prepare for phone or video interviews.** Many employers hire after a single phone call. Be ready to discuss your availability, why you want the job, and your ability to commit to a full season.

8. **Confirm housing details in writing.** Before accepting, get clear answers about housing cost deductions, roommate situations, and what happens if you need to leave early.

## Scam Warnings for Seasonal Job Seekers

Seasonal work attracts scammers who prey on job seekers eager for opportunities. Between May and July 2025, job scams grew more than 1,000% according to McAfee. Here is what to watch for:

### Red Flags That Mean Walk Away

**Payment requests.** Legitimate employers never ask you to pay for job applications, training fees, or equipment before starting. If anyone asks for money to "secure" your position or "process" your visa, it is a scam.

**Too-good-to-be-true pay.** Entry-level seasonal work does not pay $5,000 per month. If the pay seems unrealistic for housekeeping or food service, it probably is.

**Vague job descriptions.** Real employers can explain exactly what you will do, how much you will earn, and who employs you. Scammers stay vague.

**Text message job offers.** Most legitimate seasonal jobs are posted on company websites or job boards. Unsolicited text messages promising "easy pay and flexible hours" are scams.

**Personal email addresses.** Real recruiters use company email domains. If someone claims to represent Xanterra but emails from a Gmail or Yahoo account, verify independently.

**Immediate requests for personal information.** Scammers want your Social Security number, bank details, or passport copies before any interview. Real employers collect this after you accept a formal offer.

### Yellow Flags to Investigate

**Third-party recruiters.** Some are legitimate, but verify they are actually authorized by the employer. Contact the employer directly through their official website to confirm.

**Duplicate or cloned job postings.** Scammers copy real job listings and redirect applicants to fake sites. Always apply through the employer's official website, not links in emails or messages.

**Urgency and pressure.** "This opportunity won't last" or "spots filling up fast" are manipulation tactics. Real employers have application deadlines, not pressure campaigns.

### Seasonal Work Specific Scams

**Fake Coolworks listings.** While Coolworks reviews every employer, scammers may impersonate Coolworks or create fake listings outside the platform. Only apply through coolworks.com directly.

**National park impersonation.** The National Park Service has investigated online scamming attempts where scammers posed as NPS recruiters. Federal jobs are only posted on USAJobs.gov.

**Housing deposit scams.** Some scammers collect "housing deposits" for jobs that do not exist. Legitimate employers deduct housing costs from your paycheck—they do not require upfront payment.

**Fake recruitment agencies.** Scammers pose as placement agencies for resort or park jobs, charging fees to "place" you in positions. Real seasonal employers hire directly or through Coolworks at no cost to job seekers.

### How to Verify a Seasonal Job

1. **Go to the employer's official website.** Search for their careers page independently—do not use links sent to you.

2. **Verify the job exists.** Call the employer's main number and ask if the position is real.

3. **Check Coolworks directly.** If someone claims a job is on Coolworks, verify by searching coolworks.com yourself.

4. **Research the employer.** Check Glassdoor, Indeed reviews, and search "[employer name] + scam" before accepting.

5. **Trust your instincts.** If something feels wrong, it probably is. There are enough legitimate seasonal jobs that you do not need to take risks on sketchy ones.

### Report Scams

If you encounter a job scam, report it to:
- FTC: reportfraud.ftc.gov
- Coolworks: report through their help center at help.coolworks.com
- State attorney general

## The Hard Parts

**You will not save much money.** Entry-level pay minus housing and food deductions leaves modest take-home. One worker at a remote Alaska location reported earning $15,000 for a season but spending most of it on food and transportation because the nearest town was 225 miles away.

**Housing quality varies dramatically.** Some locations have updated dormitories with reasonable amenities. Others have 60-year-old buildings with thin walls, unreliable plumbing, and shared bathrooms down the hall. You will not know until you arrive.

**You live with your coworkers.** This sounds fun until you have a conflict with your roommate or someone on your shift. There is no separation between work and home. Drama can be intense in these small communities.

**Schedules are unpredictable.** Work hours depend on visitor flow. You may be scheduled for 50 hours one week and 25 the next. Days off change. Planning anything is difficult.

**Remote locations limit options.** In many park locations, you cannot leave without a car. If you do not have a vehicle, you are dependent on coworker rides, limited shuttle services, or staying put on your days off.

**Employers have mixed reputations.** Xanterra, Delaware North, and Aramark employ thousands of seasonal workers. Reviews are mixed. Some workers love the experience. Others describe low pay, poor housing, and management that does not care. Your experience depends heavily on your direct supervisor and specific location.

**Gaps between seasons are real.** Summer ends in September. Winter does not start until November. That is two months without income. Some workers do "shoulder season" jobs. Others rely on savings. This is a recurring challenge of the seasonal lifestyle.

**Mental health can suffer.** Long hours, isolation from family, limited privacy, and the intensity of living in a small community affect people differently. Burnout is common. Loneliness is real, even when surrounded by coworkers.

**You might miss things that matter.** Family events, friends' weddings, holidays—seasonal work does not pause for your personal life. You are committed to being there during your season.

## Where This Can Lead

**Seasonal career path.** Many people string together seasons: summer in the parks, winter at ski resorts, shoulder seasons in shoulder-season destinations. This is a lifestyle, not a stepping stone—but some people do it for decades.

**Hospitality management.** Entry-level work can lead to supervisor and management roles if you return for multiple seasons. These pay better and sometimes include better housing.

**Park Service careers.** Seasonal concessioner work is not the same as working for the National Park Service, but it gives you exposure. Federal park jobs (through USAJobs) offer better pay and benefits but are more competitive.

**Outdoor industry skills.** Wrangling, guiding, ski instruction, and similar roles build specific skills that transfer to other outdoor employers. Guide certifications open doors.

**Location independence experience.** Living and working in different places teaches you how to adapt quickly, travel light, and build community wherever you go. These are skills that support other paths—van life, travel, remote work.

**Connections and community.** The seasonal worker community is real. People you meet become connections for future seasons, housing leads, and job referrals. This network can be valuable.
