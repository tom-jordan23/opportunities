# Ski Resort Seasonal Jobs

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** Claude (Opus 4.5)

## Overview

Ski resorts hire thousands of seasonal workers every winter. You work at a mountain—operating lifts, serving food, renting gear, or patrolling slopes—and in exchange you get paid, often get housing, and typically receive a free season pass. The job funds your winter while you live in a ski town.

This appeals to people who want to ski or snowboard regularly without burning through savings. The trade-off is real: the pay is low, housing is often cramped, and mountain towns are expensive. You work while others ski. But if you want a winter in the mountains and you are willing to work for it, this is one of the most accessible paths.

## What You'll Do

Daily work depends on your position. Here are the main categories:

**Lift Operations:** You load and unload guests onto chairlifts. You scan passes, manage lines, and stop the lift when someone falls. You work outside in cold weather for your entire shift. Expect 8-hour days standing in the elements.

**Ski Patrol:** You respond to injuries on the mountain, conduct avalanche control, and enforce safety rules. This is one of the most skilled positions. Many patrollers are volunteers who work in exchange for a season pass rather than wages. Paid positions exist but require certifications.

**Hospitality and Food Service:** You work in lodges, restaurants, and bars. Positions include servers, bartenders, line cooks, baristas, and hosts. Tips can significantly boost income for front-of-house roles.

**Rental Shops:** You fit guests with skis, snowboards, boots, and helmets. You work indoors, interact with guests all day, and learn equipment well. This is considered one of the better entry-level positions.

**Retail:** You sell gear, apparel, and accessories in resort shops. You typically get significant discounts on merchandise.

**Housekeeping and Lodging:** You clean rooms and maintain guest accommodations at resort properties.

**Snowmaking and Grooming:** You operate equipment to make snow and groom runs. These positions often require night shifts and technical skills.

The reality: you are working while guests ski. Peak hours—weekends, holidays, powder days—are your busiest times. You get to ski on your days off and sometimes during breaks, but this is a job first.

## Requirements

- **Age:** 18 years old minimum for most positions. Roles involving alcohol service require 21.
- **Skills Needed:** Most entry-level positions require no prior experience. You will be trained on the job. Food service and bartending benefit from prior experience but are not required.
- **Physical Requirements:** Lift operations and ski patrol require standing in cold weather for extended periods. Hospitality involves being on your feet all shift. Some positions require lifting 50+ pounds regularly.
- **Time Commitment:** Full winter season—typically November through April, though dates vary by resort and snowpack. Full-time positions are 30-40 hours per week, with mandatory availability during holidays.

**For Ski Patrol specifically:** You need Outdoor Emergency Care (OEC) certification through the National Ski Patrol. The course requires 80-120 hours of training and covers emergency care at the EMT-B level. Some resorts train new patrollers; others require you to arrive certified.

## Training Provided

Resorts train you for your specific role. Lift operators learn safety protocols and equipment operation. Food service workers learn menus and POS systems. Rental shop employees learn boot fitting and equipment adjustment.

What you gain:
- Customer service experience in high-pressure environments
- Equipment knowledge if working rentals or retail
- Food handler and alcohol service certifications where applicable
- Cold-weather work experience
- For ski patrol: nationally recognized emergency care certification

These skills transfer to other hospitality, outdoor industry, and customer service roles.

## The Money Reality

### Pay

Entry-level positions pay $18-25 per hour at most major resorts. Vail Resorts advertises a $20 per hour starting wage. Smaller independent resorts may pay less.

- **Lift operators:** $18-22 per hour typical
- **Food service:** $15-20 per hour plus tips. Servers and bartenders in busy locations can earn significantly more with tips.
- **Rental shops:** $18-22 per hour
- **Ski patrol:** Wide range. Volunteer patrollers receive only a season pass and no wages. Paid professional patrol averages $35 per hour, but these positions are competitive and require experience.

Working 35 hours per week at $20 per hour yields roughly $700 per week gross—around $2,800 per month before taxes.

### Housing

Housing is the biggest challenge in ski resort employment. Mountain towns have some of the highest housing costs in the country, and affordable rentals are scarce.

**Resort-provided housing:** Some resorts offer employee housing. Expect:
- Shared dormitory-style rooms with 2-4 people
- Rent deducted from your paycheck—typically $150-200 per week
- Basic accommodations: bed, shared bathroom, minimal privacy
- Waitlists at popular resorts

Examples:
- Copper Mountain: Affordable housing at the base of the mountain for full-time employees
- Winter Park: Employee housing available during winter season
- Revelstoke (Canada): All-inclusive housing starting at $600 per month for shared rooms

**Finding your own housing:** At many resorts—including most Alterra properties—employee housing is extremely limited. Most workers find their own accommodations. This means:
- Competing with tourists and remote workers for rentals
- Paying $800-1,500 per month or more for shared rooms
- Searching Facebook groups, Craigslist, and local bulletin boards
- Arriving weeks early to secure housing before the season starts

**Reality check:** Housing can consume half or more of your paycheck if you are paying market rent in a ski town.

### Startup Costs

Before you earn your first paycheck:
- Travel to the resort: $50-500+ depending on distance
- First 2-4 weeks of expenses before payday: $500-1,500
- Housing deposit if required: $200-600
- Cold weather work gear: $100-300 (layers, boots, gloves)
- Food and incidentals during training: $200-400

Budget $1,000-2,500 to get started, depending on your situation.

### Benefits

The primary benefit is the season pass. Most resorts provide:
- **Free season pass** to the home resort
- **Access to partner resorts** depending on the parent company—Vail Resorts' Epic Pass includes 40+ resorts; Alterra's Ikon Pass includes 50+ destinations
- **Discounts** on gear, lessons, food, and rentals—typically 30-50% off
- **Free ski or snowboard lessons** where available

Some full-time positions include health insurance after a waiting period. Part-time positions generally do not.

### Realistic Savings

With resort housing and disciplined spending:
- Take-home pay: $2,000-2,400 per month
- Expenses (housing, food, incidentals): $800-1,200 per month
- Possible savings: $800-1,600 per month
- Over a 5-month season: $4,000-8,000 saved

Without resort housing, in an expensive town:
- Take-home pay: $2,000-2,400 per month
- Expenses (market rent, food, incidentals): $1,800-2,500 per month
- Possible savings: $0-500 per month—or you may break even or go into debt

Many seasonal workers break even. Some spend more than they earn. The ones who save treat it as a job, not a vacation.

## How to Get Started

1. **Decide when to apply.** Ski resorts' biggest hiring push happens August through October. Apply in July or August for the best selection. Some resorts start posting jobs as early as April.

2. **Choose your target resorts.** Research which resorts offer employee housing—this dramatically affects your financial situation. Check Coolworks.com and filter for "housing provided."

3. **Apply to multiple resorts.** The major employers are:
   - [Vail Resorts](https://jobs.vailresortscareers.com/) — 40+ resorts including Vail, Breckenridge, Park City, Whistler Blackcomb
   - [Alterra Mountain Company](https://www.alterramtn.co/en/careers) — 19 destinations including Mammoth, Deer Valley, Steamboat, Winter Park
   - Individual resort websites for independent mountains

4. **Prioritize housing.** If the resort does not provide housing, start searching immediately. Join Facebook groups for the town. Post on local classifieds. Some seasonal workers spend weeks looking before finding a place.

5. **Save startup money.** Have at least $1,500-2,500 saved before you leave—enough to cover travel, deposits, and living expenses until your first full paycheck.

6. **Get certifications if needed.** For ski patrol, find an OEC course through the [National Ski Patrol](https://nspserves.org/). For food service, get your food handler's card. For bartending, get your alcohol service certification.

7. **Arrive early.** If possible, arrive 1-2 weeks before your start date to secure housing, get oriented, and complete onboarding.

## The Hard Parts

**Low pay in expensive places.** You earn $18-22 per hour in towns where a beer costs $10 and rent for a shared room costs $1,000 per month. The math is brutal. Many workers break even or lose money.

**Housing crisis.** Ski towns are in a perpetual housing crunch. Affordable rentals are rare. Resort housing—when available—means shared rooms with little privacy. Some workers live in their cars or drive an hour from cheaper towns.

**Seasonal layoffs.** The season ends in April. You have no job, often no housing, and need to figure out what comes next. The gap between winter and summer seasons can be 6-8 weeks of no income.

**You work when others ski.** Powder days, weekends, and holidays are your busiest shifts. You ski on Tuesdays when the mountain is empty. The fantasy of skiing every day does not match reality.

**Physical demands.** Lift operators stand in freezing temperatures for 8-hour shifts. Ski patrol carries heavy equipment up steep terrain. Food service workers spend entire shifts on their feet.

**Limited advancement.** Moving up is difficult. Management positions are few and often go to returning staff with years of experience. Most seasonal workers stay entry-level.

**Workloads during peak times.** Holiday weeks can mean 50-70 hour workweeks with mandatory overtime. You earn more, but burnout is real.

**The party culture.** Ski towns have a reputation for late nights and heavy drinking. This can be fun or destructive depending on your choices. Many workers spend their earnings at the bar.

## Where This Can Lead

**Return for multiple seasons.** Many people make seasonal ski work a lifestyle, alternating between winter ski resorts and summer national park or outdoor jobs. Returning workers get priority for housing and better positions.

**Hospitality management.** After several seasons, you can move into supervisor and manager roles—restaurant manager, lift operations supervisor, guest services manager. These are year-round positions with better pay.

**Outdoor industry careers.** Ski resort experience opens doors to guiding, outdoor retail, adventure tourism, and parks and recreation jobs.

**Ski patrol as a career.** Dedicated patrollers can pursue professional patrol positions, avalanche forecasting, or wilderness medicine careers.

**Connections for remote work.** You build a network of people who have figured out location-independent income. Many seasonal workers transition to remote work that lets them keep the mountain lifestyle.

**Clarifying what you want.** A season on a mountain teaches you whether you thrive on adventure and community—or whether you need stability and routine. Both are valuable lessons.
