# Summer Camp Counselor

> **Status:** Verified
> **Last Verified:** 2026-02-09
> **Fact Checker:** Claude (Opus 4.5)

## Overview

Summer camp counselor work combines seasonal employment with an immersive experience supervising and mentoring young people. Camps hire thousands of counselors each summer across the United States. Most overnight camps provide housing and meals, making this a viable option when you have limited funds and want meaningful work.

This is not easy money. You will be responsible for children 24 hours a day at overnight camps. The pay is low relative to the hours worked. But you will develop leadership, problem-solving, and communication skills that employers value. You will also live in a community with other young adults in settings ranging from lakeside cabins to mountain wilderness.

For young adults, this is worth considering if he enjoys working with kids and wants a summer experience that builds real skills. The low barrier to entry—no special training required for most positions—makes this accessible right now.

## What You'll Do

The daily work depends on the type of camp. Here are the main categories:

### Day Camps

You work during daytime hours. Campers arrive in the morning and leave in the afternoon. You supervise activities, manage transitions between programs, and handle conflicts. Days run roughly 7 AM to 5 PM with some prep time on either end. You go home at night.

Day camps are typically located in suburban or urban areas. Pay is hourly. No housing provided.

### Overnight Camps (Residential)

You live on-site with campers. This is the classic summer camp experience. Campers sleep in cabins or bunks with counselors. You are responsible for your group around the clock—waking them up, supervising activities, handling nighttime issues, and everything in between.

Typical overnight camp sessions run 1-8 weeks. Some counselors work the entire summer (8-10 weeks). You live in the same cabin as your campers—usually 6-12 kids per cabin with 1-2 counselors.

The schedule looks something like this:
- 7:00 AM: Wake up, cabin cleanup, get campers to breakfast
- 8:00 AM - 12:00 PM: Morning activities (swimming, sports, arts)
- 12:00 PM: Lunch
- 1:00 PM: Rest hour (your only break most days)
- 2:00 PM - 5:00 PM: Afternoon activities
- 6:00 PM: Dinner
- 7:00 PM - 9:00 PM: Evening programs, campfires, activities
- 9:00 PM - 10:00 PM: Getting campers to bed
- 10:00 PM onward: Possible staff meetings, handling camper issues, or finally free time

### Specialty Camps

These focus on specific activities or populations:

- **Sports camps**: Tennis, basketball, soccer, gymnastics, etc.
- **Arts camps**: Theater, music, visual arts, dance
- **Academic camps**: STEM, coding, robotics, language immersion
- **Adventure camps**: Wilderness survival, rock climbing, kayaking
- **Special needs camps**: Working with campers who have physical or developmental disabilities
- **Religious camps**: Faith-based programming with spiritual components

Specialty camps often pay more and expect specific skills or certifications. A sailing camp wants sailing experience. A rock climbing camp wants certified climbers. These positions are harder to get without background in the specialty.

## Requirements

### Age and Eligibility

- Minimum age: 18 for most counselor positions, 16-17 for junior counselor or CIT (Counselor-in-Training) roles
- Must be legally authorized to work in the United States (international applicants can use J-1 visa programs—see Resources section)
- Background check required by all reputable camps
- Reference checks are standard

### Skills Needed

Most general counselor positions require no prior experience. Camps train you. What they look for:

- Genuine interest in working with young people
- Patience and emotional maturity
- Ability to follow safety protocols
- Physical stamina for active days
- Willingness to be supervised and accept feedback
- No attitude about doing basic tasks—you will clean, do dishes, and handle unpleasant situations

### Physical Requirements

- Ability to participate in camp activities—hiking, swimming, sports
- Standing and walking for most of the day
- Lifting up to 50 pounds
- Working outdoors in heat, humidity, bugs, and weather
- Being active for 12+ hours per day

### Time Commitment

- Summer season: Late May or early June through mid-August
- Pre-camp training: 1-2 weeks before campers arrive
- Most camps expect commitment for the full summer (8-10 weeks)
- Some camps offer shorter sessions (2-4 weeks) but prefer longer commitments

## Training Provided

Camps provide training before the season starts. This typically includes:

- Child development and age-appropriate expectations
- Behavior management techniques
- Emergency procedures and safety protocols
- Activity-specific training
- Camp philosophy and culture
- Recognizing and reporting child abuse
- CPR and First Aid basics (some camps provide full certification)

This training is paid time in most cases. It usually runs 5-10 days.

The training does not lead to formal credentials in most cases. But the experience itself builds skills employers recognize: leadership, crisis management, communication, and working with diverse groups.

## Certifications That Help

While not required for most positions, these certifications make you more hirable and often increase your pay:

### Lifeguard Certification

Camps with waterfront activities desperately need lifeguards. This is the single most valuable certification for camp work.

- American Red Cross Lifeguarding certification
- Cost: $200-350 for the course
- Time: 25-30 hours of training
- Valid: 2 years
- Benefit: Often adds $50-100+ per week to pay; makes you essential staff

### CPR and First Aid

- American Heart Association or American Red Cross certification
- Cost: $75-150
- Time: 4-8 hours
- Valid: 2 years
- Benefit: Required for some camps; shows initiative to others

### Wilderness First Aid (WFA)

For outdoor and adventure camps.

- Cost: $200-300
- Time: 16-20 hours
- Valid: 2 years
- Benefit: Preferred or required for wilderness programs

### Specialty Certifications

Depending on camp type:
- Ropes course/challenge course facilitator
- Archery instructor
- Sailing or canoeing instructor
- Horseback riding instructor
- Rock climbing belay certification

These specialty skills can qualify you for higher-paying activity director positions rather than general counselor roles.

## The Money Reality

### Pay Ranges

**Day Camps**
- $12-18 per hour
- 35-45 hours per week typical
- No housing or meals—you cover those expenses
- Weekly take-home: $400-700 before taxes

**Overnight Camps (General Counselor)**
- $300-600 per week for most camps
- Some premium camps pay $500-750 per week
- Housing and meals included
- Total compensation value when factoring room and board: $700-1,100 per week

**Specialty or Senior Positions**
- Activity directors: $400-800 per week
- Head counselors: $500-900 per week
- Waterfront/lifeguard positions: Add $50-150 per week to base counselor rate

**YMCA Camps**
- $350-420 per week plus room and board for overnight positions
- Some positions offer $15/hour for day camp
- Free training including CPR, First Aid, and lifeguard certification

### What is Included at Overnight Camps

- Housing: Dorm-style or cabin living with other staff
- Meals: All meals during camp session (value: roughly $240/week)
- Laundry: Often included
- WiFi: Usually available but bandwidth may be limited
- Activities: Access to camp facilities on time off

### Startup Costs

- Travel to camp: $50-500 depending on distance
- Certifications before arrival: $0-400 if you get lifeguard or specialty certs
- Personal gear: $50-150 (good shoes, water bottle, flashlight, appropriate clothing)
- Before first paycheck: Budget $100-200 for incidentals

### Hidden Costs

- Days off in a remote location—limited options without a car
- Tips for counselors are rare (unlike service industry jobs)
- End-of-summer gap before fall work or school
- If you leave early, you may forfeit end-of-season bonus

### End-of-Season Bonuses

Many camps offer completion bonuses for staff who stay the full season:
- Typical range: $200-500
- Some camps: Up to $1,000 for returning staff or critical positions
- Forfeit policy: Leave early, lose the bonus

## Application Timeline

Summer camp hiring follows a predictable cycle. Apply early.

**October - December**: Applications open at many camps. Premium positions get filled early.

**January - February**: Peak hiring season. Most camps actively interviewing and offering positions.

**March - April**: Second wave of hiring. Some positions still available but selection narrowing.

**May**: Last-minute positions only. Less choice, but camps are sometimes desperate to fill gaps.

**Key insight**: Apply in January or February for best selection. The camps you actually want to work for fill early.

### Application Process

1. Research camps and identify 5-10 you want to apply to
2. Prepare resume highlighting youth work, leadership, and any relevant activities
3. Write a short personal statement about why you want to work at camp
4. Gather 2-3 references (professors, coaches, employers, volunteer supervisors)
5. Submit applications through camp websites or job boards
6. Complete phone or video interviews
7. Accept offer and complete background check paperwork
8. Attend pre-camp training

## Major Camp Networks and Organizations

### ACA-Accredited Camps

The American Camp Association accredits camps that meet up to 300 health and safety standards. ACA accreditation means:
- Background checks on all staff
- Staff-to-camper ratios meet industry standards
- Emergency procedures in place
- Regular safety inspections

There are approximately 3,000 ACA-accredited camps. This is your best starting point for finding legitimate, well-run camps.

### YMCA Camps

The YMCA operates hundreds of camps nationwide. Benefits include:
- Established organization with consistent standards
- Free training and certifications (CPR, First Aid, lifeguard)
- Benefits for full-time summer staff at some locations
- Network of camps if you want to return future summers

### Scout Camps

Scouting America (formerly Boy Scouts of America) and Girl Scouts of the USA operate extensive camp networks:
- Hundreds of local council camps nationwide
- Philmont Scout Ranch in New Mexico is 140,000 acres—the largest
- Often prefer or require Scouting background
- Strong outdoor skills focus

### Religious and Faith-Based Camps

- Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, and other faith-based camps
- Often include spiritual programming alongside traditional camp activities
- May expect staff alignment with faith values
- Some provide excellent compensation and community

### Private and Independent Camps

- Range from small family-run operations to luxury camps
- Pay and conditions vary dramatically
- Research individual camps carefully
- Some premium private camps pay significantly more than average

## How to Get Started

1. **Decide on camp type.** Day camp near home for hourly pay, or overnight camp for the full experience with housing? Specialty camp if you have specific skills?

2. **Get certified if you can.** Lifeguard certification before applying makes you significantly more hirable. CPR/First Aid is easier and still valuable.

3. **Search the job boards.** Use these sites:
   - ACA Job Board: acacamps.org/jobs
   - CampChannel: campchannel.com (2,200+ camps)
   - American Summer Camps: americansummercamps.com
   - CoolWorks: coolworks.com/camp-jobs
   - YMCA Careers: ymca.org/get-involved/careers

4. **Apply to multiple camps.** Do not put all your eggs in one basket. Apply to 5-10 camps that interest you.

5. **Prepare for interviews.** Common questions: Why do you want to work at camp? Describe a time you handled a difficult situation with a child. What would you do if a camper refused to participate?

6. **Check references and reviews.** Search "[camp name] + reviews" and "[camp name] + Glassdoor" before accepting. Ask to speak with former staff.

7. **Read the contract carefully.** Understand pay schedule, deductions, bonus terms, and what happens if you leave early.

8. **Plan your arrival.** Confirm travel logistics, training dates, and what to bring.

## The Hard Parts

**You are responsible for children around the clock.** At overnight camps, there is no clocking out. A camper gets sick at 2 AM—you handle it. A camper is homesick and crying—you comfort them. A conflict erupts between kids—you de-escalate. This responsibility does not pause.

**The pay is objectively low for the hours worked.** If you calculate hourly rate at an overnight camp, you may be making $4-8 per hour when you factor in all the time you are "on." The room and board have real value, but the cash in your pocket is modest.

**Camp burnout is real and documented.** The combination of long hours, physical exhaustion, emotional labor, isolation from normal support systems, and constant responsibility leads to burnout. As one camp worker described: "On a day to day, you're dealing with like 10,000 issues and you're hot and you're hungry and you're tired and you have to keep going."

**You live with your coworkers.** There is no separation between work and personal life. Drama in staff housing affects your job. A conflict with a coworker is also a conflict with your roommate.

**Camper behavior challenges can be intense.** Some campers have behavioral issues, trauma backgrounds, or special needs that require significant patience and skill. Not every child is easy to work with. Some will push every boundary you set.

**Privacy is limited.** Shared cabins, shared bathrooms, shared meals. If you need solitude to recharge, this environment is difficult.

**Days off are few and remote.** Many camps give 1-2 days off per week. If the camp is in a rural area without transportation, your day off options are limited. You might spend it on-site because there is nowhere else to go.

**Mental health can suffer.** The intensity of the environment affects people differently. Some thrive. Others struggle with anxiety, depression, or emotional exhaustion. Be honest with yourself about whether this structure works for you.

**You will miss things.** Friend's wedding in July? Family reunion? Summer concert you wanted to attend? Camp runs on its schedule, not yours. You commit to being there.

## Skills You Will Gain

Despite the challenges, camp counselor work builds genuinely valuable skills:

**Leadership.** You are responsible for a group of young people. You make decisions, solve problems, and guide others. Employers notice this.

**Communication.** You learn to give clear instructions, provide feedback, resolve conflicts, and communicate with parents, campers, and supervisors with different needs.

**Adaptability.** Every day brings unexpected situations. You learn to think on your feet and remain calm when plans fall apart.

**Crisis management.** Emergencies happen at camp. You learn to respond effectively under pressure.

**Time management.** Camp schedules are tight. You learn to move groups, manage transitions, and stay on track.

**Working with diverse groups.** Campers come from different backgrounds, abilities, and personalities. You learn to connect with people who are different from you.

**Patience.** Working with children requires patience you may not know you have. You develop it.

**Resume translation.** Camp counselor experience translates well to management, education, healthcare, and human services careers. Employers in these fields recognize the value.

## Where This Can Lead

**Career in camping and outdoor education.** Camp directors, program coordinators, and outdoor education specialists often started as counselors. Return for multiple summers and take on increasing responsibility.

**Youth development work.** Experience with young people opens doors to after-school programs, mentoring organizations, recreation departments, and youth-serving nonprofits.

**Teaching and education.** Camp experience demonstrates classroom management skills, lesson planning ability, and comfort with young people. Useful for teaching careers.

**Social work and counseling.** The emotional skills developed at camp—active listening, crisis intervention, behavior management—align with social work and counseling fields.

**Outdoor recreation careers.** Adventure camps and outdoor programs connect to careers in outdoor recreation, wilderness therapy, and environmental education.

**Strong references.** Camp directors and supervisors who see you work intensively for 10 weeks provide powerful reference letters. They know you in ways a typical employer cannot.

**Network of peers.** Camp staff often stay connected. Your fellow counselors become friends, references, and professional contacts across industries.
