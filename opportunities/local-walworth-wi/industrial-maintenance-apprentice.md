# Industrial Maintenance / Manufacturing Technician Apprentice

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** Not yet fact-checked — drafted 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous demand, employer-dependent entry. Manufacturers in this corridor have chronic unfilled maintenance positions.

## Overview

If you asked manufacturers across southern Wisconsin what job they cannot fill, a large share would say maintenance technician. This is the highest-demand skilled role in the region's largest industry, and it is reachable through a registered apprenticeship where the employer pays you to learn.

Maintenance technicians keep production equipment running. When a machine goes down, the line stops and the company loses money by the minute, which is why these people are valued and well paid.

Two Wisconsin registered apprenticeships cover this territory. The **Industrial Manufacturing Technician** apprenticeship trains for **1.5 years with paid related instruction**, and reported earnings reach **up to $30.33/hour** as an established manufacturing technician. The **Maintenance Technician** apprenticeship is a separate, longer program covering deeper mechanical and electrical work.

The phrase that matters in the program description: **"you'll get paid to attend classes with no student debt."** Related instruction is paid time. You are not taking evening classes on your own dime — you are on the clock.

## What You'll Do

You diagnose and fix industrial equipment, and you prevent it from breaking in the first place.

**Reactive work.** A machine stops. You troubleshoot it under pressure while a supervisor watches the line sit idle. This means reading electrical schematics and mechanical drawings, using meters and diagnostic tools, and reasoning systematically about what changed. It is genuinely intellectual work — the satisfying kind of problem-solving with an immediate answer.

**Preventive work.** Scheduled inspections, lubrication, belt and bearing replacement, alignment, and calibration. Less exciting, and it is what separates a good maintenance department from a constant emergency.

The technical range is broad: mechanical systems (bearings, gearboxes, hydraulics, pneumatics, conveyors), electrical systems (motors, controls, sensors, three-phase power), welding and fabrication for repairs, and increasingly PLCs and industrial controls, which is where the field is heading and where the money is.

You also work whatever hours the equipment demands. Breakdowns do not respect shift boundaries, and weekend or shutdown work is common because that is when lines can be taken out of service.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. **You must be hired by an employer first** — registered apprenticeships in Wisconsin are sponsored by individual employers, and the state approval follows employment.
- **Skills Needed:** None formally required, but mechanical aptitude is genuinely necessary. If you have taken things apart to understand them, that instinct is the core qualification. Basic algebra helps for the coursework. Comfort with electricity is essential and learnable.
- **Physical Requirements:** Lifting, climbing, working in confined and awkward positions, standing on concrete, exposure to heat, noise, and grease. Reasonably demanding but not extreme.
- **Time Commitment:** Industrial Manufacturing Technician is reported at **1.5 years**. Maintenance Technician apprenticeships typically run longer — confirm the term with the sponsor. Full-time employment throughout, plus paid classroom instruction.

## Training Provided

**A registered apprenticeship with paid related instruction — structurally one of the best arrangements available without a degree.**

- **Employment first.** You are hired as an employee, then enrolled and registered with the state.
- **Paid classes.** Apprentices are paid their regular hourly wage during related instruction time in the classroom.
- **No tuition debt.** The employer covers related instruction costs.
- **Defined wage progression.** Your rate rises as you complete hours and coursework.
- **A recognized credential** at completion, portable to other employers.

**Where the classroom instruction happens locally:**
- **Gateway Technical College** (Racine, Kenosha, Elkhorn) — runs Maintenance Technician Apprentice, Industrial Manufacturing Technician Apprentice, and Facilities Maintenance Technician Apprentice programs. Apprenticeship department: **(262) 564-2954**, apprenticeship@gtc.edu
- **Blackhawk Technical College** (Janesville, Beloit) — Industrial Maintenance Mechanic technical diploma covering mechanical systems, electrical maintenance, and industrial troubleshooting, plus apprentice programs

**The single most useful phone number in this document:** Gateway states that if you need help finding an employer who will take you on as an apprentice, you should contact the local **WI DWD-BAS Apprenticeship Training Representative at (414) 216-8417.** That is a person whose job includes matching applicants to sponsoring employers. Very few applicants call.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** Reported earnings **up to $30.33/hour** as an established manufacturing technician under the Industrial Manufacturing Technician program. **Apprentice starting wages are lower and were not established in the sources reviewed** — get the starting rate and the progression schedule in writing before signing. Established industrial maintenance technicians in this region generally earn well above production wages, and maintenance overtime is frequent and often at premium rates.
- **Housing:** Your own.
- **Startup Costs:** Low. Steel-toe boots and basic hand tools — some employers supply tools, some expect you to build a set over time. **Ask about the tool expectation**, because a full mechanic's tool set is a real expense if it falls on you.
- **Benefits:** Employer-dependent, but manufacturers offering apprenticeships generally provide full benefits including health insurance and retirement.
- **Hidden Costs:** Tools, if not supplied. Otherwise close to none — this is a paid-to-learn path.

## Hiring Pattern

Demand is continuous and unmet across the region's manufacturers. Entry depends on finding a sponsoring employer, which is the actual bottleneck.

**Two routes in, and you should work both:**

**Route one — get hired into maintenance directly.** Some manufacturers hire maintenance helpers, apprentices, or junior technicians outright. Watch employer career pages.

**Route two — get hired into production and transfer.** This is the more common path and it is reliable. Take a production job at a plant with a maintenance department, perform well, make your interest known, and apply internally. Manufacturers strongly prefer promoting a known reliable employee into maintenance over hiring a stranger. See [Kikkoman](kikkoman-production-operator.md) and [Generac](generac-production.md).

**Employers to target in the radius:** Generac (Whitewater), Kikkoman (Walworth), Snap-on (Kenosha, Elkhorn), SC Johnson (Racine), Nestlé (Burlington), Kerry and Frito-Lay (Beloit), plus the machining and fabrication cluster around Rockford and the many smaller plants across Walworth and Rock counties. Hospitals, school districts, and municipalities also employ facilities maintenance technicians, which is a quieter version of the same trade.

## How to Get Started

1. **Call the DWD Bureau of Apprenticeship Standards representative at (414) 216-8417.** Say you want an industrial maintenance or manufacturing technician apprenticeship and ask which employers in Walworth, Rock, Racine, or Kenosha county are sponsoring. This is the highest-leverage call available.
2. **Call Gateway Technical College's apprenticeship department at (262) 564-2954** or email apprenticeship@gtc.edu. Ask which local employers sponsor their maintenance and manufacturing technician programs.
3. **Call Blackhawk Technical College** about their Industrial Maintenance Mechanic program and apprentice pathways on the Janesville and Beloit side.
4. **Apply directly to manufacturers for any maintenance opening**, including helper and junior titles. Use employer career sites.
5. **If nothing in maintenance is open, take a production job at a plant with a maintenance department** and state at the interview that maintenance is your goal. Then apply internally the moment you are eligible.
6. **Ask every employer the same question: "Do you sponsor a registered apprenticeship, and what is the apprentice wage progression?"** Get it in writing.
7. **Ask the Walworth County Job Center** whether WIOA funds manufacturing pathways — this is a named priority sector for the board.
8. **Start learning electrical fundamentals now**, free. Understanding three-phase power, motor controls, and how to read a ladder diagram will distinguish you immediately.

## The Hard Parts

**Getting the sponsoring employer is the hard part, not the work.** The apprenticeship cannot exist without one, and there is no application portal for "become an apprentice." You have to find the employer. That is why the phone numbers above matter more than any job board.

**Breakdown pressure is real.** When a line is down, you are the person everyone is waiting on, and they will tell you so. Some people thrive on that. Others find it miserable.

**Call-ins and off-hours work.** Equipment fails at 2 a.m. and during holiday shutdowns, which is precisely when maintenance work gets scheduled.

**The work is dirty and physical.** Grease, oil, coolant, confined spaces, hot equipment, and awkward positions.

**Real hazards.** Electrical work, stored energy, and moving machinery. Lockout/tagout procedures exist because people have been killed. There is no acceptable shortcut, ever.

**The learning curve is steep and long.** Mechanical systems, electrical systems, hydraulics, pneumatics, and controls. Feeling incompetent for the first year is normal and not a sign you chose wrong.

**Tool costs can be significant** if the employer expects you to supply your own. Clarify early.

**Older maintenance crews can be hard on apprentices.** The trade has a tradition of testing new people. Most of it is harmless; some of it is not. It helps to know it is coming.

## Where This Can Lead

This is one of the strongest career floors available without a degree. Every manufacturer, hospital, food plant, warehouse, school district, and municipality needs maintenance technicians, and the skill set does not become obsolete.

Progression: maintenance technician to senior technician, maintenance lead, maintenance supervisor, and maintenance or plant manager. Manufacturing management is commonly promoted from maintenance because those people understand the equipment.

**Specializations that pay more:** industrial controls and PLC programming, robotics and automation, instrumentation and calibration, and millwright work. Controls and automation are where the field is heading and where the highest non-degreed manufacturing wages are.

Adjacent moves: field service technician for equipment manufacturers (travel, strong pay, company vehicle), facilities engineering, and technical sales. Equipment vendors actively recruit experienced maintenance technicians.

Also worth noting: these skills work for yourself. Maintenance technicians understand mechanical and electrical systems well enough to run their own contracting or repair business later.
