## Overview

This is the office-work entry in this category, and it is the only one that puts you in business clothes in a heated building with a predictable schedule.

Banks and credit unions **hire people with no experience and train them on the job** in cash handling, customer service, and banking procedures. The stated requirements are typically a high school diploma or equivalent, decent communication skills, comfort handling cash, and basic computer ability.

Reported Wisconsin pay: **around $17.20/hour average in Milwaukee and $17.49 in Waukesha** for no-experience teller roles, with most in a **$15.14 to $18.94** band. Those are metro figures, and Walworth County rates may run lower.

Be direct about the trade-off. This pays less than most other paths in this category — meaningfully less than [corrections](correctional-officer.md), [dispatch](911-telecommunicator.md), [public works](county-highway-public-works.md), or [manufacturing](kikkoman-production-operator.md). What it offers instead is indoor work, normal daytime hours, no physical risk, a professional environment, and an unusually clear internal ladder into banking, which is a real career with a high ceiling.

If the appeal of the other paths is money and the appeal of this one is the kind of work, that is a legitimate basis for choosing it — as long as the wage comparison is made honestly.

## What You'll Do

You work the teller line, or the member services desk at a credit union.

The transactional core: processing deposits, withdrawals, checks, loan payments, and cashier's checks; balancing your cash drawer at the start and end of every shift; handling the vault and ATM; and processing account maintenance. Accuracy is absolute — your drawer must balance, and it is noticed when it does not.

The customer core: greeting people, answering account questions, resolving problems, and explaining products. **Increasingly, tellers are expected to sell** — identifying customers who might want a credit card, a savings account, or a loan referral, and meeting referral targets. This is the part that surprises new hires most, and whether you find it reasonable or uncomfortable matters a lot.

You also handle fraud awareness, identity verification, and compliance procedures. Banking is heavily regulated, and there are documented procedures for nearly everything.

**Credit unions differ from banks** in a way worth understanding. Credit unions are member-owned nonprofits, generally with less aggressive sales pressure and a more service-oriented culture. Many people find them substantially better places to work. Community banks sit between credit unions and large national banks.

Hours are typically weekday business hours plus Saturday mornings. No nights.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. **A thorough background check including credit history** — this is a real gate. Banking positions involve handling money and access to customer financial data, and a poor credit record or any theft-related history can disqualify you. Bonding requirements apply.
- **Skills Needed:** None required. Basic arithmetic, comfort with cash, keyboard ability, and genuine customer service temperament.
- **Physical Requirements:** Minimal. Standing or sitting at a station, light lifting of coin. Along with [IT service desk](it-service-desk-apprentice.md), the most physically accessible option here.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time and part-time both common. Weekday business hours plus rotating Saturdays. **No nights, no holidays** — genuinely unusual in this category.

## Training Provided

**Entirely on the job, and paid.**

New teller training typically covers the institution's core banking system, cash handling and drawer balancing procedures, transaction types and processing, identity verification and fraud detection, regulatory compliance including Bank Secrecy Act and anti-money-laundering requirements, privacy rules, robbery response procedures, and product knowledge.

Larger institutions run structured multi-week onboarding programs with classroom instruction before you take a line position. Community banks and small credit unions train alongside an experienced teller.

**What you do not get is a portable certificate**, and that is the honest weakness of this path. Banking knowledge is real and transferable between institutions, but there is no license or credential you carry out.

**Where the ladder gets formal:** institutions typically fund training and licensing for advancement. That includes lending training, **NMLS registration** for mortgage loan originators, and sometimes tuition assistance for finance coursework. Ask about all of it.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** Reported Wisconsin no-experience teller averages of **$17.20/hour (Milwaukee)** and **$17.49/hour (Waukesha)**, with most between **$15.14 and $18.94**. **These are metro figures — Walworth County likely runs at or below the bottom of that band, and this needs local verification.** Some institutions pay incentives for referrals and product sales.
- **Housing:** Your own. **Every community in this radius has bank and credit union branches**, so a five to fifteen minute commute is realistic — which matters more at this wage than at higher ones.
- **Startup Costs:** Low but not zero. **Business or business-casual clothing is required** and is a genuine expense if you do not own any. Budget a few hundred dollars.
- **Benefits:** Generally solid. Full-time bank and credit union positions typically include health insurance, retirement with employer match, paid time off, and often tuition assistance. Bank holidays are real days off, which is worth something.
- **Hidden Costs:** Clothing maintenance. Otherwise minimal.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous. Teller turnover is steady across the industry, and the radius contains a large number of branches.

**Employers in the radius:** community banks across Walworth County including institutions headquartered in Lake Geneva, Elkhorn, Delavan, and Whitewater; regional banks with branch networks; national banks including Chase, US Bank, BMO, and Associated; and **credit unions** including Blackhawk Community Credit Union, First Community Credit Union, Summit, Landmark, and others serving Walworth and Rock counties.

**A structural caution worth knowing:** branch banking employment has been declining nationally for years as transactions move to apps and ATMs. Branches consolidate and teller headcount shrinks. This does not make it a bad first job, but it makes staying a teller a poor long-term plan — the path forward is into lending, operations, or management, not staying on the line.

## How to Get Started

1. **Check your credit report before applying**, free at annualcreditreport.com. A poor credit record is the most common disqualifier and you should know what is on it. If there are problems, address them first.
2. **Apply to credit unions before banks.** Generally lower sales pressure, more service-focused culture, and reported by employees as better places to work.
3. **Apply to community banks headquartered locally**, which tend to promote internally and value long-term employees.
4. **Apply to every branch within fifteen minutes.** At this wage, commute distance materially affects take-home pay.
5. **Ask directly about sales expectations and referral quotas** at the interview. This is the difference between a pleasant job and a stressful one, and the answer varies enormously between institutions.
6. **Ask about the advancement path and how long it typically takes** to move from teller to personal banker or loan processor. An institution with a clear answer has a real ladder.
7. **Ask about tuition assistance.** At 21 this may be the most valuable part of the offer.
8. **Dress for the interview the way you would dress for the job.** Presentation is part of the assessment in this field in a way it is not in the trades.

## The Hard Parts

**The pay is the weakest in this category, and that should be stated plainly.** At roughly $16-18/hour, this pays substantially less than corrections, dispatch, public works, line work, or manufacturing. If income is the priority, other documents here serve better.

**Sales pressure is the most common complaint in the industry.** Referral quotas, product targets, and pressure to open accounts for people who did not ask. Some institutions are reasonable about it; some are not, and the difference is enormous. Ask before accepting.

**Your drawer must balance, every day.** Being out of balance is stressful and repeated shortages are a serious matter. It is a low-tolerance environment for a normal human error rate.

**Difficult customers are routine.** People are emotional about money — overdraft fees, held checks, declined transactions, fraud on their account. You absorb that reaction without having caused it.

**Robbery is a genuine, if uncommon, risk.** Banks train for it. It is rare, and it is real.

**Repetitive and confining.** You cannot leave your station freely. The day is structured and the work is transactional.

**Branch banking is a shrinking sector.** Automation and consolidation reduce teller headcount over time. Treat this as an entry point, not a destination.

**The background and credit check eliminates some people** before the interview.

**No portable credential.** Unlike a CDL, a CNA, or a journeyworker card, you leave with experience rather than a qualification.

## Where This Can Lead

The ladder is the argument for this path, and it is a real one.

**Standard progression:** teller to senior teller or head teller, then **personal banker** or member services representative (better pay, more responsibility), then loan processor, **loan officer**, branch operations, and branch manager. Branch managers earn well and the role does not require a degree.

**The lending track is where the money is.** Mortgage loan originators require NMLS registration, which employers typically fund, and lending roles pay substantially more than branch positions. Commercial lending is a genuine career with high earning potential.

**Operations and back-office:** fraud investigation, compliance, BSA/AML analysis, treasury operations, and underwriting. Compliance in particular is a growing, well-paid, unglamorous specialty that hires people who understand branch operations from the inside.

**Credit union specific:** credit unions promote internally aggressively and are often good places to build a long career.

**Adjacent:** insurance, financial services, and title work all recruit people with banking backgrounds.

**Tuition assistance is the real lever at 21.** A finance or business degree paid for by an employer while you work, combined with branch experience, positions you well for the higher rungs. That is a legitimate route to a professional career without student debt — and it is the strongest reason to take this over higher-paying manual work if the office environment is what appeals.
