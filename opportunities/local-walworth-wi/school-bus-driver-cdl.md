# School Bus Driver (Employer-Paid CDL)

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** Not yet fact-checked — drafted 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous, with a strong surge in July and August before the school year. Chronic nationwide shortage.

## Overview

This is the cheapest way to get a commercial driver's license in this region, and possibly the cheapest anywhere.

A CDL normally costs thousands of dollars at a private truck driving school. School districts and bus contractors will train you for it **for free, and pay you while you train**, because they cannot find enough drivers. The shortage is severe and has been for years.

Reported terms in the Janesville and Walworth County area: positions at **$24.00/hour with a $2,000 sign-on bonus for non-CDL applicants and $4,000 for already-credentialed drivers**; other postings with **$1,500 non-CDL and $2,000 credentialed** bonuses; and one contractor reported at **$30/hour for routed drivers.** Training is provided with no experience necessary. Schedules are split-shift with no nights or weekends.

Two ways to think about this. As a career, it is a decent, stable, part-time-to-full-time job with a schedule that leaves your evenings free. As a **stepping stone, it is unusually valuable** — a Class B CDL with a passenger and school bus endorsement, earned for free, opens doors in trades, utilities, municipal work, and trucking. Several other paths in this category either require a CDL or prefer one.

## What You'll Do

You drive a route, twice a day, with children on board.

A morning run typically starts early — pre-trip inspection, then picking up students along a fixed route and delivering them to school. Then a gap of several hours in the middle of the day. Then an afternoon run in reverse.

The driving itself is the easy part after training. The hard part is **managing thirty to seventy children behind you while operating a large vehicle in traffic.** You are responsible for their safety, you cannot turn around to deal with a fight, and you must enforce rules while driving. Bus drivers describe behavior management, not driving, as the real job.

Additional duties: daily pre-trip and post-trip inspections, fueling and cleaning, incident reporting, radio communication with dispatch, and driving in Wisconsin winter conditions on rural roads. Field trips and athletic runs are usually optional extra hours at extra pay.

The split schedule is the defining feature. You work roughly 6:00-9:00 a.m. and 2:00-4:30 p.m., with a long midday gap. For some people that gap is dead time; for others it is a second job, school, or genuine freedom.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** **21 is the typical minimum for a school bus driver** in Wisconsin — districts commonly require it and insurers often insist. **At 21, this gate is cleared.** Valid driver's license with a clean record — a poor driving history is disqualifying. High school diploma is often not required. Background check including fingerprinting, plus a check against child abuse and neglect registries.
- **Skills Needed:** None. You need a good driving record and patience with children.
- **Physical Requirements:** **A DOT physical is required.** Vision and hearing standards apply. Ability to perform pre-trip inspections, operate the door and lift, and assist students. Not physically strenuous otherwise.
- **Licensing:** A **Class B CDL with Passenger (P) and School Bus (S) endorsements.** The employer trains you for this. You also need an air brake endorsement in most cases.
- **Time Commitment:** Split shift, roughly 25-35 hours per week for route work. School-year calendar with summers off — which is either an advantage or an income gap depending on your plans.

## Training Provided

**This is the whole point of the document: the employer pays for your CDL and pays you to earn it.**

Training typically includes classroom instruction on regulations and student management, behind-the-wheel training in a bus with an instructor, preparation for the CDL knowledge and skills tests, and the endorsement testing itself. Federal **Entry-Level Driver Training** requirements apply to new CDL applicants, and employers registered as training providers deliver it.

Districts and contractors generally cover the permit fee, the training, the testing, and the license. **Confirm this specifically with each employer**, and confirm whether any of it is contingent on staying a set period.

**What you walk away with is portable and valuable.** A Class B CDL with P and S endorsements is a real credential. Upgrading later to a Class A for tractor-trailer work is far easier and cheaper once you already hold a CDL and have commercial driving history.

**Cross-reference this against the other documents here.** [Electric line worker](electric-line-worker-apprentice.md) requires a CDL Class A within six months. [Water and wastewater operator](water-wastewater-operator.md) roles often require or prefer a CDL. Municipal public works and county highway jobs usually require one. Getting a CDL paid for is a genuine strategic move even if bus driving is not your destination.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** Reported figures in the Janesville area include **$24.00/hour** with a **$2,000 sign-on bonus for non-CDL applicants** and **$4,000 for credentialed drivers**; another posting at **$1,500 / $2,000**; and a contractor reported at **$30/hour for routed drivers.** **These are aggregated figures and the $30 claim in particular needs verification** — it is high for this market and may reflect a specific route type or a misreported figure. Note that hourly rate matters less than it looks: at 30 hours a week for a school-year calendar, a $24/hour job is roughly $25,000-27,000 a year, not $50,000.
- **Housing:** Your own. Commutes are short — every district in the radius runs buses, so there is almost certainly one within fifteen minutes.
- **Startup Costs:** **Close to zero, which is the headline.** The employer pays for CDL training and testing. You may pay a small amount for the permit or a document fee. Compare with $3,000-$7,000 at a private CDL school.
- **Benefits:** Varies significantly. Some districts offer health insurance and Wisconsin Retirement System participation to drivers at sufficient hours; many part-time driver positions do not. **This is the most important question to ask**, because a district job with WRS participation is worth far more than a contractor job at a dollar more per hour.
- **Hidden Costs:** **Summers are unpaid** unless you find summer work — a real annual income gap. Training-repayment clauses are possible; see the warning below.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous, with a heavy surge in **July and August**. Districts and contractors are desperate before the school year starts, which is when you have the most negotiating leverage and the fastest hiring.

**Two kinds of employers, and the difference matters:**
- **School districts directly** — Walworth, Fontana, Lake Geneva, Elkhorn, Delavan-Darien, Whitewater, Big Foot, Janesville, Beloit, Burlington and others. District employment may include better benefits and possible WRS participation.
- **Private contractors** — Summit School Services, Durham School Services, First Student, and others contract to run district transportation. Often faster to hire, sometimes higher hourly rate, generally weaker benefits.

Apply to both, and understand which one you are talking to.

## How to Get Started

1. **Check your driving record first.** Order your Wisconsin driving abstract from the DMV. A poor record is the most common disqualifier and you should know what is on it before applying.
2. **Apply to your nearest school district's transportation department directly**, and to Summit, Durham, and First Student. Applying in July or August gets the fastest response.
3. **Ask the critical question: "Do you pay for CDL training, and is there any repayment obligation if I leave?"** Get the answer in writing. Free training with an 18-month repayment clause is a different deal from free training.
4. **Ask whether the position includes benefits and Wisconsin Retirement System participation**, and at how many hours. This is the largest hidden difference between otherwise similar offers.
5. **Ask about summer work** — some districts offer summer maintenance, custodial, or summer-school routes.
6. **Ask about extra hours**: field trips, athletic runs, and charter work usually pay extra and are how drivers reach full-time income.
7. **Study for the CDL permit written test now**, free. Wisconsin practice tests are available online. Arriving with the permit already passed makes you immediately more attractive.
8. **Be honest with yourself about children.** If managing a bus full of middle schoolers sounds intolerable, this is the wrong job even for the free CDL.

## The Hard Parts

**The children are the job, and it is harder than people expect.** Fights, bullying, students who will not stay seated, and occasionally a genuinely difficult child, all while you are driving a forty-foot vehicle. You cannot leave the driver's seat. Drivers consistently name this, not the driving, as what makes people quit.

**The split shift eats your day without paying for it.** Early morning and mid-afternoon, with a long unpaid gap. It is difficult to hold a conventional second job around it, though it works well alongside school or evening work.

**The hours are limited and so is the income.** Route work at 25-35 hours per week on a school-year calendar is not a full-time income. Treat it as a part-time job with a free CDL attached, or plan on extra runs and summer work.

**Summers are unpaid** unless you arrange something. Nine months of pay across twelve months of expenses.

**Winter driving with a bus full of kids.** Icy rural roads, poor visibility, and full responsibility. This is genuinely stressful.

**Early mornings, permanently.** A 5:30 a.m. start every school day.

**The responsibility is heavy and constant.** You are accountable for children's lives every trip. Most drivers describe that weight as real.

**Watch for training repayment clauses.** "Free CDL training" that carries an obligation to repay if you leave within a year or two converts a benefit into a debt. It is legal and it appears in this industry. Ask before signing, every time.

**Contractor versus district matters.** Contractor pay can look better while benefits are much worse. Compare total compensation, not hourly rate.

## Where This Can Lead

The CDL is the asset, and this is the rare case where the stepping stone may be worth more than the job.

**Direct uses of a Class B CDL with P and S endorsements:** municipal public works and county highway departments (which need CDL drivers for plows and trucks), transit and coach driving, utility line crews, waste collection, and heavy equipment work.

**Upgrading to Class A** for tractor-trailer work is much easier and cheaper once you hold a CDL with commercial driving experience. That opens regional and over-the-road trucking, which pays substantially more. See [CDL / Trucking](../skills-and-trades/cdl-trucking.md).

**Inside school transportation:** trainer, dispatcher, and transportation supervisor or director. District transportation management is a stable career with a pension, reachable without a degree.

**Adjacent:** motorcoach and charter driving, which pays better and travels. Van Galder, a long-established regional bus operator in this area, is worth investigating.

Also worth noting: a district job can be a way into district employment generally — food service, custodial, maintenance, and paraprofessional roles, all with WRS participation.
