# Phlebotomy & Patient Care Technician

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** Not yet fact-checked — drafted 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous. High turnover roles across hospitals, clinics, labs, and blood centers.

## Overview

Wisconsin does not license phlebotomists. There is no state credential you must hold before someone can hire you to draw blood, which means employers are free to train you — and several do.

**The Red Cross offers paid phlebotomy training** with positions across Wisconsin. **Versiti**, the regional blood center, hires phlebotomists with paid training at Wisconsin locations. Blood centers are the most reliable train-from-zero employers in this space, because their entire operating model depends on a steady supply of trained collection staff.

For hospital work, the parallel role is **patient care technician** — typically a CNA with additional skills including phlebotomy, EKG, and point-of-care testing. Hospitals frequently train CNAs into PCT roles internally.

Where training is not employer-funded, it is short and cheap by healthcare standards. Wisconsin technical college phlebotomy courses **typically run about six weeks** and prepare you for national certification. Several appear on the state's Eligible Training Provider List, which means WIOA funding may cover them.

This is a low-cost, fast entry into healthcare. It is also low-paid, and honest about that below.

## What You'll Do

You draw blood, and you do it many times a day.

The technical skill is venipuncture — locating a suitable vein, positioning correctly, drawing into the right tubes in the right order, labeling accurately, and handling specimens properly. Order of draw matters, labeling errors are serious, and both are drilled into you.

The rest of the job is people. Patients are frightened, dehydrated, elderly with fragile veins, obese, heavily tattooed, or have had chemotherapy that ruined their veins. Some faint. Some cry. Some are children. Your job is to keep them calm and get the sample on the first attempt, because a second stick is a bad day for both of you.

**Settings differ substantially:**
- **Hospital lab** — inpatient draws on floors, early morning rounds, emergency department draws, and difficult patients. Fast-paced and varied.
- **Outpatient clinic or draw station** — scheduled patients, calmer pace, more predictable hours.
- **Blood center (Red Cross, Versiti)** — donor collection at fixed centers and mobile blood drives. Healthy donors rather than sick patients, larger volume draws, and travel to drive locations.
- **Patient care technician** — phlebotomy plus CNA duties plus EKG and point-of-care testing on a hospital unit.

Hospital roles include nights, weekends, and holidays. The 5:00 a.m. inpatient draw round is a standard assignment.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. Background check, drug screen, immunizations including hepatitis B, TB test.
- **Skills Needed:** None required where the employer trains. **Wisconsin does not license phlebotomists**, so there is no legal barrier to being hired untrained — only employer preference.
- **Physical Requirements:** Standing and walking most of a shift, pushing carts, fine motor control, and good vision. Blood center mobile work involves setting up and tearing down equipment at drive sites. **You must be genuinely comfortable with blood and needles** — this is not something to discover on day one.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time and part-time both common. Blood center work may involve variable schedules and travel to mobile drives.

## Training Provided

**Three routes, in order of preference:**

**1. Employer-paid training.** The Red Cross advertises paid phlebotomy training, and Versiti hires phlebotomists with paid training in Wisconsin. This is the best version — you are paid from day one and pay nothing. Blood centers are the most likely employers to offer it.

**2. Hospital internal training.** Hospitals frequently train existing employees — CNAs, unit clerks, lab assistants — into phlebotomy and patient care technician roles. This is a strong argument for getting hired into any hospital role first. See [Mercyhealth Entry-Level](mercyhealth-entry-level.md).

**3. Short technical college course.** Wisconsin technical college phlebotomy programs **typically run about six weeks** and prepare you for national certification. Several are listed on the **Eligible Training Provider List**, which means the Walworth County Job Center may fund one through WIOA. Mid-State Technical College's program is noted as one of the few in Wisconsin accredited by NAACLS, though it is far from this radius — check Gateway, Blackhawk, and Madison College for local options.

**Certification** is optional in Wisconsin but improves hiring odds and pay. National certifications come from the NHA (CPT), ASCP (PBT), and AMT. Employers often reimburse the exam.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** **Not established for this region and it needs verification.** Phlebotomy is among the lower-paid healthcare technical roles nationally — generally comparable to or slightly above CNA pay. Patient care technician roles typically pay somewhat more than straight phlebotomy because the scope is broader. Shift differentials apply for nights and weekends.
- **Housing:** Your own.
- **Startup Costs:** **Near zero if the employer trains you.** Scrubs and shoes. If you take a technical college course instead, expect a modest tuition — small by healthcare standards, and potentially WIOA-funded.
- **Benefits:** Hospital and blood center employment generally includes health insurance, retirement, paid time off, and often tuition assistance. Blood centers are typically nonprofits with reasonable benefits.
- **Hidden Costs:** Certification exam fees if not reimbursed. Renewal and continuing education.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous. Phlebotomy has high turnover because it is demanding, lower-paid, and frequently used as a stepping stone — which is precisely what makes it consistently available to new entrants.

**Employers in the radius:** Mercyhealth (Walworth, Lake Geneva, Janesville, Beloit, Rockford), Advocate Aurora (Burlington, Elkhorn, Kenosha, Racine), Beloit Health System, SSM Health Janesville, OSF Rockford, plus independent labs including Quest and Labcorp draw stations, and the blood centers — **American Red Cross** and **Versiti**.

**Blood centers deserve first attention** because paid training is part of their model rather than an exception.

## How to Get Started

1. **Apply to the American Red Cross and Versiti first**, and specifically ask about paid phlebotomy training positions. This is the clearest employer-funded route.
2. **Search hospital career sites for "phlebotomist," "phlebotomy technician," and "patient care technician,"** and look for "will train" or "training provided" language.
3. **Filter for the Walworth and Lake Geneva Mercyhealth sites** for the shortest commute.
4. **Ask the Walworth County Job Center whether WIOA will fund a phlebotomy course** — these programs are on the state ETPL and run about six weeks, which makes them exactly the kind of short-term training WIOA is designed for.
5. **Check Gateway, Blackhawk, and Madison College** for local phlebotomy programs and their schedules and costs.
6. **If you cannot get a phlebotomy job directly, take any hospital job and transfer internally.** Hospitals train their own people, and internal candidates have real advantages.
7. **Ask who pays for national certification** and whether it comes with a raise.
8. **Be honest with yourself about needles and blood** before investing time. Some people discover they cannot do this, and that is worth finding out early.

## The Hard Parts

**The pay is low, and that is the central honest fact.** Phlebotomy sits near the bottom of healthcare technical wages. Compared with other options in this category — a [correctional officer](correctional-officer.md) near $33/hour or a [911 telecommunicator](911-telecommunicator.md) near $26 — this is a substantially lower-paid path. Its case rests on low entry cost and where it leads, not on what it pays.

**Difficult draws are genuinely stressful.** Elderly patients with fragile veins, dehydrated patients, oncology patients whose veins are scarred, and infants. Missing a stick twice and having to call someone else is demoralizing and it happens to everyone.

**Some patients are frightened or hostile.** People faint, panic, cry, and occasionally become combative. You manage that while holding a needle.

**Early mornings.** Hospital inpatient draw rounds start before dawn so results are ready for morning rounds.

**Needlestick risk is real.** Safety devices have reduced it substantially, but exposure incidents happen and the follow-up testing is stressful.

**High turnover means chronic short staffing**, which means heavy patient loads.

**Repetitive strain.** Thousands of draws a year is hard on hands and wrists.

**It can be a dead end if you treat it as a destination.** Phlebotomy is an excellent starting point and a poor career terminus. Plan the next step from the beginning.

## Where This Can Lead

The value here is access and direction, not the job itself.

**Within the lab:** medical laboratory assistant, then **medical laboratory technician** (a two-year degree, often employer-tuition-supported, and a substantially better wage), then medical laboratory scientist. The lab career ladder is well defined and hospitals fund people up it.

**Within patient care:** patient care technician, then CNA-to-LPN-to-RN using hospital tuition assistance. Phlebotomy experience is directly relevant and hospitals value internal candidates.

**Sideways:** [sterile processing](sterile-processing-technician.md) (better pay, less patient contact), EKG and cardiac monitoring technician, surgical technologist, and dialysis technician — dialysis in particular values phlebotomy skill and pays better.

**Blood center specific:** donor services supervision, mobile drive coordination, and apheresis, which is a specialized and better-paid collection role.

The strategic point: this is a cheap, fast way to get a hospital badge and become eligible for tuition assistance at 21. That combination is worth more than the hourly wage suggests.
