# Electric Line Worker Apprentice

> **Status:** Draft — apprentice pay and training-cost premise VERIFIED 2026-08-26
> **Last Verified:** 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Cyclical and competitive. Utilities open apprentice applications in windows rather than continuously. Being ready when the window opens is the whole game.

## Overview

This is the highest-ceiling path in this category, and the hardest to get into.

Line workers build and maintain the electrical distribution system — the poles, wires, and transformers that carry power to houses and businesses. It is a registered apprenticeship, which means you are a paid employee from day one, your wage rises on a defined schedule, and you finish with a credential recognized nationwide.

The pay is not a rumor. An Alliant Energy Line Technician posting in Mineral Point, Wisconsin advertised **$43.34-$55.56 per hour** at apprentice-or-journey level, with tuition reimbursement. Mineral Point is outside this radius — the point is what the career pays, not that specific job.

The Wisconsin apprenticeship is **four years and 8,000 hours**: **7,360 hours of on-the-job training** plus **640 hours of related classroom instruction** delivered in one-week blocks.

Be realistic about the difficulty. These positions are competitive, physically demanding, and genuinely dangerous. Utilities can be selective because many people want the job.

## What You'll Do

You work outside, at height, on energized equipment, in whatever weather Wisconsin produces.

The core work: setting and climbing poles, framing crossarms, stringing and tensioning conductor, installing and replacing transformers, switching and sectionalizing circuits, and operating bucket trucks and digger derricks. Some of it is done on de-energized lines, some on energized lines using rubber gloving or hot stick methods.

Storm work is the part that defines the job. When a windstorm or ice storm takes out circuits, crews work extended shifts until power is restored — sixteen-hour days, overnight, in the conditions that caused the outage. This is where the overtime pay comes from, and where the exhaustion comes from.

As an apprentice you start on the ground: groundman work, handling material, rigging, digging, keeping the crew supplied, and learning by watching. You earn your way up the pole. Expect the first year to involve a lot of physical labor and not much glory.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. **A Commercial Driver's License, Class A, is required — or the ability to obtain one.** Reported terms require obtaining and maintaining a Wisconsin CDL Class A within the first six months.
- **Skills Needed:** No prior line experience required, but most employers test applicants on **basic verbal, arithmetic, and abstract reasoning**. Some also test **physical ability — balance, coordination, strength, and mechanical aptitude.** Algebra and basic trigonometry help.
- **Physical Requirements:** Substantial. Climbing poles with tools and a belt, working at height, lifting heavy material, and working in extreme heat and cold. No fear of heights, and no casual attitude about it either.
- **Time Commitment:** Four years, 8,000 hours. Full-time plus on-call and storm response. Classroom instruction in one-week blocks scattered through the year.
- **Additional certifications required during the program:** Transition to Trainer, OSHA, CPR, and First Aid.

## Training Provided

**A registered apprenticeship — the strongest training arrangement documented in this guide.**

- **Term:** four years
- **Total:** 8,000 hours
- **On-the-job:** 7,360 hours
- **Related instruction:** 640 hours, delivered in one-week blocks staggered through the year
- **Wage progression:** defined, rising as you complete hours and coursework
- **Credential:** a nationally recognized journeyworker certification

Related instruction is delivered through the Wisconsin Technical College System. Because instruction comes in week-long blocks, you are pulled off the crew for a week at a time rather than attending night classes.

**The employer is the gatekeeper.** Applicants apply **directly to the electric utility**. Registered apprenticeships in Wisconsin are sponsored by individual employers, which means there is no way to enroll in the apprenticeship first and find a job second. Employment comes first, always.

**A pre-apprenticeship route exists.** One documented path is to complete a **nine-month Electric Power Distribution program** at a technical college first, then secure a job with a utility or municipality willing to sponsor the apprenticeship. This costs tuition and time, and it is not required — but it makes you a much stronger applicant in a competitive field, and WIOA funding may cover it. Northwest Wisconsin Technical College and Chippewa Valley Technical College both run line worker programs; confirm what is available at Gateway or Blackhawk for this region.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay — apprentice rate now separated from journey rate.** The posting cited above advertised $43.34-$55.56/hour at "apprentice or journey level" without distinguishing the two. Aggregated Alliant-specific data puts a **line mechanic apprentice at roughly $29/hour, in a $25-$38 range**, against a journeyman lineman at roughly **$44/hour** — which works out to an apprentice starting near **two-thirds of journey scale**. These apprentice figures are aggregated employee-reported estimates rather than a published schedule, so **still get the starting rate and progression in writing** — but the earlier version of this document conflated the two rates, and roughly $29/hour is a far more realistic expectation for year one than $43.
- **Training cost — VERIFIED by the employer.** Alliant Energy states of its apprenticeship: **"Not only is your apprenticeship education free of charge, you get full-time wages and benefits while you learn."** The same page advertises **70% tuition reimbursement** for degree or career-advancement coursework. That is a direct employer confirmation of the paid-to-learn premise. Overtime and storm work add substantially to annual earnings; line workers commonly earn well above their base hourly rate over a year.
- **Housing:** Your own. Note that storm response may send you away from home for days at a time, with lodging and per diem typically covered.
- **Startup Costs:** Low to moderate. Utilities generally supply safety equipment, tools, and rubber goods. **The CDL is the real cost if you do not have one** — either you pay for training, the employer covers it, or the Walworth County Job Center funds it. Work boots and personal gear.
- **Benefits:** Utility employment benefits are among the best available without a degree — health insurance, retirement, and **70% tuition reimbursement at Alliant**. Many line positions are union-represented.
- **Hidden Costs:** The CDL if unfunded. Pre-apprenticeship program tuition if you take that route.

## Hiring Pattern

Cyclical and competitive. Utilities post apprentice openings in defined windows, often once or twice a year, and receive many applications.

**Prepare before the window opens.** Applicants who already hold a CDL, have completed a line worker program, or have relevant physical work history get through. Applicants who see a posting and apply cold generally do not.

**Utilities and sponsors serving this radius:** Alliant Energy (Wisconsin Power and Light), We Energies, ComEd on the Illinois side, plus municipal electric utilities and rural electric cooperatives in the area. Line construction contractors also hire and sometimes sponsor apprentices — a separate route worth investigating, though contractor work involves more travel.

## How to Get Started

1. **Read the Wisconsin DWD Electric Line Worker apprenticeship page** to understand the term, hours, and requirements precisely. Note: this page returned an access error when tested on 2026-08-26 — try a browser, or call the Bureau of Apprenticeship Standards.
2. **Call the Wisconsin DWD-BAS Apprenticeship Training Representative at (414) 216-8417.** This person's job includes helping applicants find employers who will take on apprentices. Very few people call.
3. **Get your CDL Class A moving now.** It is the most common hard requirement and takes real time. Ask the Walworth County Job Center whether WIOA funding covers CDL training.
4. **Set up job alerts on the careers pages of Alliant Energy, We Energies, and ComEd directly**, and check them weekly. Alliant posts openings at **alliantenergy.wd1.myworkdayjobs.com/alliant** and maintains a dedicated apprenticeship page at alliantenergy.com/careers/apprenticeships. Apprentice windows close fast.
5. **Contact the municipal electric utilities and rural cooperatives in the region.** Smaller sponsors get fewer applications than the big utilities.
6. **Ask Gateway and Blackhawk Technical College** whether they offer or can refer you to an Electric Power Distribution or line worker program, and whether WIOA will fund it.
7. **Get in physical condition and practice the aptitude test material** — verbal, arithmetic, and abstract reasoning. These are screening gates, and they are studiable.
8. **Consider a line construction contractor as a way in.** More travel, less selective, and real experience that strengthens a later utility application.

## The Hard Parts

**This job kills people.** Electrocution, falls, and struck-by incidents are real occupational risks, and line work consistently appears among the more dangerous trades. Utilities take safety seriously and the procedures work — but the margin for carelessness is small and permanent. This is the most important thing to understand before applying.

**Storm work is brutal.** You go out in the conditions everyone else is sheltering from, for as long as it takes, sometimes for consecutive days. It happens on holidays and during family events.

**On-call restricts your life.** Rotation weeks mean staying reachable and sober and near home.

**The physical demand does not let up.** Climbing, lifting, and working at height in extreme weather, for a full career. It is hard on knees, shoulders, and backs.

**Getting in is genuinely competitive.** Many applicants for few slots. Expect to apply more than once, and expect to need the CDL and possibly a pre-apprenticeship program to be taken seriously.

**Four years is a long commitment.** The wage progression rewards finishing. Leaving at year two leaves you with experience but no journeyworker card.

**The first year is grunt work.** Digging, hauling, and handing tools up while someone else does the interesting part.

**Fear of heights is disqualifying, and you may not know you have it** until you are forty feet up on a wooden pole in the wind. Find out early if you can.

## Where This Can Lead

Journeyworker line worker is a career destination in its own right, with high pay, strong job security, and demand everywhere in the country. The credential is portable nationally.

Advancement from there: crew leader, foreman, line superintendent, troubleshooter, substation technician, system operator, and utility management. Many utilities promote operations leadership from the line.

Related directions: line construction contracting (including your own business eventually), utility training and safety roles, and transmission work, which pays even more and travels more.

The demand outlook is strong. Grid replacement, electrification, and renewable interconnection all require line workers, and the existing workforce is aging out.
