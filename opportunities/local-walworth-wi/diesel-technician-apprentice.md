# Diesel / Heavy Truck Technician Apprentice

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** Not yet fact-checked — drafted 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous. Dealership and fleet shops in the region have persistent openings.

## Overview

Trucks, buses, and heavy equipment break, and the people who fix them are in short supply. Dealerships and fleets have responded by building apprentice programs that hire people with no experience and pay for their training.

**Lakeside International Trucks**, which operates locations across Wisconsin and Illinois, states that it covers **100% of training costs** for its technicians. That is a specific, checkable claim from a specific regional employer, and it is the kind of arrangement that makes this path work without tuition.

Reported apprentice pay in the region runs roughly **$20-$21/hour in the Chicago market**, with broader Illinois figures for apprentice diesel mechanics reported around **$27/hour** — a wide spread that reflects how much experience level and employer type matter.

The trade suits someone who likes machinery, diagnostics, and the specific satisfaction of taking something broken and making it work. It rewards patience and systematic thinking more than raw strength.

## What You'll Do

You diagnose and repair diesel engines and the systems around them.

The work spans a lot of territory: engine repair and rebuilds, fuel and emissions systems including diesel particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction, transmissions and drivetrains, brakes and air systems, hydraulics, electrical and electronic systems, and increasingly **computer diagnostics**. Modern trucks are rolling computer networks, and a large share of the job is plugging in a laptop and reading fault codes intelligently.

As an apprentice you start with preventive maintenance — oil changes, filters, greasing, inspections, tires, and brakes. That is not busywork; PM work teaches you where everything is and what normal looks like. You then move into diagnosis and repair as you demonstrate competence.

The environment is a shop: concrete, hoists, air tools, noise, and grease. Some work is done outside or on the road if the employer runs mobile service. Emergency and after-hours work happens, because a truck down is a customer losing money.

**Distinguish the shop types**, because they are different jobs. **Dealership shops** (International, Freightliner, Peterbilt, Kenworth, Volvo) work on one brand, have factory training and diagnostic tools, and offer structured certification paths. **Fleet shops** (trucking companies, municipalities, school districts, waste haulers) maintain their own vehicles, with steadier hours and less variety. **Independent shops** see everything and vary widely in quality.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent generally expected. **Valid driver's license required; a CDL is often required or expected within a set period** so you can road-test what you repair. Background check and drug screen.
- **Skills Needed:** None formally required for apprentice roles. Mechanical aptitude is the real qualification. Comfort with computers matters more every year — this is no longer a purely mechanical trade.
- **Physical Requirements:** Lifting heavy components, working under vehicles, awkward positions, standing on concrete, and exposure to cold in unheated bays and outdoor work. Hard on hands, back, and knees over time.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time. Apprenticeship programs typically run one to four years depending on structure. Overtime and occasional emergency call-outs are normal.

## Training Provided

**Employer-paid, and in the best cases fully paid.**

**Employer and dealership programs.** Lakeside International Trucks reportedly covers 100% of technician training costs. Dealerships generally send technicians to **factory training** from the truck or engine manufacturer, which is expensive, employer-funded, and genuinely valuable — factory certifications are recognized industry-wide and raise pay directly.

**Registered apprenticeship.** Apprentice diesel mechanics typically participate in formal programs combining classroom instruction with hands-on training, starting with basic maintenance and progressing to complex repairs. Wisconsin's registered apprenticeship system covers automotive and diesel occupations, and Gateway and Blackhawk Technical Colleges provide related instruction in this region. **Ask employers whether they sponsor a registered apprenticeship**, because that gives you the wage progression and credential in writing.

**ASE certifications** are the portable credential in this trade. The medium/heavy truck series (T-series) is what heavy-duty employers care about. Many employers pay for the tests and give raises for each certification earned. This is the thing to accumulate, because it belongs to you rather than to the employer.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** Reported apprentice diesel technician pay around **$20.76/hour average in Chicago**, with most in a **$19.57-$21.78** band; broader Illinois apprentice figures reported around **$27.39/hour**, which likely reflects more experienced apprentices or union shops. **All aggregated — verify locally**, and expect Walworth-area rates to differ from Chicago-market rates. Experienced diesel technicians with factory and ASE certifications earn substantially more, and dealership technicians often work flat-rate or incentive systems where productivity drives income.
- **Housing:** Your own.
- **Startup Costs:** **This is the real financial catch in this trade. Technicians are usually expected to own their own hand tools**, and a working set costs thousands of dollars accumulated over years. Some employers provide a tool allowance or a starter set; some do not. **Ask before accepting, and get the answer specifically.** Tool truck financing at high interest is a well-known trap for young technicians. Specialty and diagnostic tools are normally the employer's.
- **Benefits:** Dealerships and fleets typically offer health insurance, retirement, and paid time off. Municipal and school district fleet jobs may include Wisconsin Retirement System participation, which is a significant advantage.
- **Hidden Costs:** Tools, above all. Also boots, and the ongoing cost of replacing tools that break or wear out.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous. Diesel technician shortages are well documented nationally and this region has substantial trucking, agricultural equipment, and municipal fleet activity.

**Employers to target in the radius:** International, Freightliner, Peterbilt, Kenworth, and Volvo truck dealerships around Janesville, Beloit, Rockford, Racine, Kenosha, and Milwaukee; agricultural equipment dealers including John Deere and Case IH dealers serving Walworth and Rock counties; **school district and municipal fleet shops** (short commutes, good benefits, quieter environment); trucking company shops; waste hauling fleets; and Van Galder or other regional bus operators.

**Municipal, county, and school district fleet shops deserve particular attention** in a stay-local search. Short commute, predictable hours, WRS participation, and they train because they must.

## How to Get Started

1. **Verify the Lakeside International Trucks training claim directly.** Call their nearest location, ask whether they hire apprentices with no experience, and confirm what "100% of training costs" covers.
2. **Apply to every truck and agricultural equipment dealership within 45 minutes**, using their own careers pages. Look for "apprentice," "technician trainee," "lube technician," or "service technician entry level."
3. **Apply to municipal, county, and school district fleet shops.** Check Walworth County, Rock County, and local district employment pages. These are rarely advertised on aggregators.
4. **Ask the tool question before anything else: "Am I expected to buy my own tools, and is there a tool allowance?"** This is the difference between a job that costs you thousands and one that does not.
5. **Never finance tools from a tool truck at 20% interest as a first-year apprentice.** Buy used, buy gradually, buy mid-grade. This is the most common financial mistake young technicians make.
6. **Ask whether the employer sponsors a registered apprenticeship** and what the wage progression looks like.
7. **Ask who pays for ASE tests and factory training**, and whether certifications come with a raise.
8. **Ask about a CDL** — whether it is required, on what timeline, and who pays.
9. **Contact Gateway or Blackhawk Technical College** about diesel and automotive programs and which employers sponsor apprentices.
10. **Ask the Walworth County Job Center** about WIOA funding for transportation-sector training.

## The Hard Parts

**Tool costs are the single biggest trap in this trade.** A young technician can end up thousands of dollars in debt to a tool truck at high interest, effectively working to pay for the tools they need to work. This has damaged a lot of careers. Buy used, buy slowly, and refuse financing.

**Flat-rate pay systems can be brutal for beginners.** Under flat rate you are paid for the book time on a job, not the hours you actually take. A fast experienced technician earns well; a slow apprentice earns poorly and feels it. **Ask whether the shop is hourly or flat rate**, and be cautious about flat rate as a first job.

**The physical toll is real and cumulative.** Heavy lifting, awkward positions under vehicles, and cold concrete. Backs, knees, shoulders, and hands take damage over a career. Older technicians in this trade generally have something wrong with them.

**Cold.** Wisconsin winters in a partly heated shop, or outside on a roadside call.

**Dirty in a way that does not wash off easily.** Diesel fuel, oil, coolant, and brake dust. Your hands will look like a mechanic's hands.

**Diagnostics can be genuinely frustrating.** Intermittent electrical faults on a modern truck can consume days. Customers and service managers get impatient.

**Emergency and after-hours calls.** A broken-down truck is a business losing money, and shops respond accordingly.

**Shop culture varies widely.** Dealerships tend to be more structured; small independents depend entirely on the owner. Ask to see the shop and talk to a technician before accepting.

**The trade is changing.** Emissions systems, electronics, and eventually electric drivetrains mean continuous learning. That is an opportunity for someone who likes learning and a problem for someone who does not.

## Where This Can Lead

Strong, durable career with genuinely broad options. Freight moves regardless of the economy, and equipment always needs maintenance.

Progression: apprentice to technician to senior or master technician, then shop foreman, service advisor, service manager, and shop or fleet manager. Certifications drive pay directly, which makes the trajectory unusually transparent — each ASE and factory certification is a measurable raise.

**Specializations that pay more:** engine and drivetrain rebuild, electrical and electronic diagnostics (the highest-value specialty), emissions systems, hydraulics, and heavy equipment. Agricultural and construction equipment technicians are in similar demand.

Adjacent moves: field service technician for an equipment manufacturer, mobile repair, technical training, warranty and service engineering, parts and technical sales, and fleet management. Manufacturers actively recruit experienced dealership technicians.

Also viable: **your own shop.** Diesel repair is a trade where a skilled person with tools, a bay, and a reputation can work independently. Mobile truck repair in particular has a low capital requirement, and this region's trucking traffic supports it.

The skills also transfer sideways into [industrial maintenance](industrial-maintenance-apprentice.md), which shares hydraulics, electrical, and mechanical fundamentals.
