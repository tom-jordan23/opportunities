# Correctional Officer

> **Status:** Draft — pay and academy length VERIFIED 2026-08-26
> **Last Verified:** 2026-08-26 (pay, academy, add-ons verified against state sources)
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous. The Wisconsin Department of Corrections recruits year-round and is actively hiring statewide.

## Overview

This is the highest starting wage in this category, by a wide margin, with no credential required beyond a high school diploma.

**Verified:** the Wisconsin Legislative Fiscal Bureau records **minimum base pay of $33.66 per hour and maximum base pay of $39.86 per hour** for correctional officers as of June 30, 2024, and the Department of Corrections publishes the same starting figure as effective January 1, 2025. Requirements are age 18 or older, a high school diploma or equivalent, and no felony or domestic violence convictions. DOC's own careers page confirms officers **"complete a paid seven week academy which includes on the job training at a correctional institution."**

That combination is unusual enough to state plainly: over thirty dollars an hour, paid training, and a public pension, available to an eighteen-year-old with a diploma.

It is also, of everything documented in this category, the job most likely to be genuinely hard on a person. This document spends more space on the downsides than the upsides, and that is deliberate.

## What You'll Do

You supervise incarcerated people inside a secure facility.

The routine is structured and repetitive: unlock and lock, count, escort, search, observe, document. You conduct security checks and cell searches, monitor movement between areas, distribute meals and medication, and log everything. Most of a shift is watching and accounting for people.

The rest of the job is managing conflict. You de-escalate arguments, respond to fights, handle people in mental health crisis, and occasionally use force. You will be sworn at, tested, manipulated, and threatened. You also spend a lot of time talking with people, because officers who can talk to people have far fewer incidents than officers who cannot.

Shifts are typically eight or twelve hours, rotating, covering nights, weekends, and holidays. Facilities are staffed continuously and mandatory overtime is common.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** **18 or older.** High school diploma or equivalent. **No felony convictions and no domestic violence convictions.** United States citizenship or work authorization. Valid driver's license.
- **Skills Needed:** None required at hire. What matters is composure, consistency, and the ability to hold a boundary without escalating.
- **Physical Requirements:** Standing and walking for long shifts. You must be able to physically intervene. Expect a physical assessment and a medical evaluation.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time, rotating shifts. Mandatory overtime is normal, not exceptional.
- **Screening:** Background investigation, drug screen, and psychological evaluation.

## Training Provided

**Paid academy, then paid on-the-job training.** You are an employee earning wages from the first day of training, not a student.

**VERIFIED:** DOC states officers "complete a paid **seven week** academy which includes on the job training at a correctional institution." (The earlier six-week figure in circulation is wrong.) Curriculum covers security procedures, defensive tactics, use-of-force law and policy, emergency response, crisis intervention and de-escalation, report writing, and inmate management.

Training continues after the academy. Field training pairs you with experienced officers before you work a post independently, and annual in-service training is required.

**County jails train too, on a different standard.** County jail officers in Wisconsin follow a jail officer certification pathway — Madison College, among others, offers a Jail Officer Certificate. Some counties hire and then send you to certification. Walworth County and Rock County both operate jails, which means the shortest commute version of this job may be a county position rather than a state one.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay — VERIFIED:** **minimum base $33.66/hour, maximum base $39.86/hour** (Legislative Fiscal Bureau, as of June 30, 2024; DOC publishes $33.66 as effective January 1, 2025). At 40 hours the minimum is roughly $70,000 annually before overtime.
- **Add-on pay — VERIFIED:** the state funds several add-ons on top of base. A **$5/hour high-vacancy add-on** applies at institutions where the combined vacancy rate for correctional officer and sergeant positions exceeds 40%. There is a **$3/hour maximum-security add-on** (raised from $2 under 2023 Act 19, and extended to supervisors) and a **$1/hour medium-security add-on**. A separate **$5/hour add-on** has been funded for Waupun Correctional Institution. **Ask which add-ons apply at the specific institution you would be assigned to — it can change the rate by several dollars an hour.**
- **Shift differentials:** an older DOC document lists $0.45/hour for nights and $0.60/hour for weekends, with premium overtime rates not applying to differentials. **Those figures come from a stale document (see warning below) and need confirmation.**
- County jail pay is usually lower than state pay — confirm locally.
- **Housing:** Your own.
- **Startup Costs:** Effectively zero. Uniforms and equipment are provided. No tuition.
- **Benefits:** State employment includes health insurance, paid leave, and participation in the **Wisconsin Retirement System**. The pension is a significant part of the total value and is easy to undervalue at 20.
- **Hidden Costs:** No financial ones. The costs are physical, psychological, and relational.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous and aggressive. The Wisconsin DOC states it is hiring and seeking candidates for secure correctional facilities statewide. Corrections has persistent staffing shortages nationally.

**Timing warning:** an academy start date can fall about **five or six months after your initial application**. Apply early and plan for the wait. Do not quit an existing job on the strength of an application.

**Facilities and jails in or near the radius:** Wisconsin DOC operates institutions across the state — confirm which are within a 60-minute drive, as the nearest state institutions may be at the edge of or beyond the radius. Walworth County Jail (Elkhorn) and Rock County Jail (Janesville) are both well inside it. Illinois Department of Corrections and county jails in Winnebago and McHenry counties are additional options on the Illinois side.

## How to Get Started

1. **Read the Wisconsin DOC security careers page** at doc.wi.gov and check which institutions are hiring. **Ignore the "Understanding Correctional Officer Pay (New Hires)" PDF linked from that page — it is badly out of date (see warning below).** Email DOCCorrectionalOfficerRecruitment@wisconsin.gov for current figures.
2. **Map the commute before applying.** This matters more than usual — a rotating night shift plus a long drive is a serious combination. The nearest state institution may be farther than the county jail.
3. **Apply to Walworth County and Rock County jails as well.** Check governmentjobs.com/careers/walworthco and the Rock County human resources page. A jail officer job in Elkhorn may beat a state job an hour away, even at lower pay.
4. **Apply now if you are interested at all**, because the academy start is months out. Applying costs nothing and preserves the option.
5. **Be completely honest on the application.** Background investigations find things. Undisclosed history disqualifies people more reliably than disclosed history does.
6. **Get physically ready.** There is a physical assessment and defensive tactics training. Arriving in poor condition makes the academy much harder.
7. **Talk to someone who does the job before you accept.** This is the most important step in this document. Ask a current or former officer what it cost them. If you cannot find one, that itself is worth noticing.

## A Warning About DOC's Own Pay Document

DOC's careers page links a PDF titled **"Understanding Correctional Officer Pay (New Hires)"** that states a starting rate of **$16.99/hour**. **That document is obsolete.** Its own headings read "HIRE THROUGH JANUARY 3, 2021" and its progression table tops out at $23.85/hour after 15 years. It describes the pay structure that existed before **2023 Wisconsin Act 19**, which raised correctional officer minimum pay from an effective $24.29/hour to $33/hour — an increase of $8.71, about 36%.

Anyone researching this job online will find that $16.99 figure and may rule the career out on the strength of it. The current verified minimum is **$33.66/hour**. If a recruiter or document quotes you anything near $17, you are looking at stale information.

For context on why the raise happened: the same Legislative Fiscal Bureau paper records that the overall vacancy rate across affected institutions fell from **33.4% to 11.1%** after the increase. The state raised pay because it could not staff its prisons.

## The Hard Parts

This section is longer than the others because the risks here are larger than the others.

**The psychological cost is real and measurable.** Corrections officers experience elevated rates of PTSD, depression, substance abuse, divorce, and suicide compared with the general population. This is not a rumor or a deterrent — it is a documented occupational health pattern, and it is the single most important fact in this document. If you take this job, plan for your mental health actively rather than hoping to be fine.

**Violence is a genuine possibility.** Assaults on staff happen. Most days are routine; the exceptions are severe.

**You will witness things that stay with you.** Self-harm, overdoses, deaths, and long-term human suffering.

**Mandatory overtime and forced shifts.** Chronic short-staffing means being held over and having days off cancelled. This is the complaint that appears most often from working officers.

**Rotating shifts wreck sleep and relationships.** Nights, weekends, and holidays, indefinitely.

**The culture can be corrosive.** Some facilities have healthy professional cultures. Others have cynical ones that change how people talk and think about others. Which one you land in is largely luck.

**It follows you home.** Officers describe becoming guarded, suspicious, and short-tempered with family. This is the cost people most often name years later.

**The environment is bleak.** Concrete, fluorescent light, noise, and no natural daylight for a full shift.

**A specific warning about age.** An eighteen or nineteen-year-old supervising adults in a prison is being asked to carry authority that most people that age have never held. Some handle it well. For others, the money is not worth what the first few years take. Go in with your eyes open, and consider whether a county jail, dispatch, or a trade might give you similar pay with less cost.

## Where This Can Lead

Inside corrections: sergeant, lieutenant, captain, and administrative or program roles. Promotion is generally structured and merit-based, and the pension improves with rank and years.

Sideways into law enforcement: many officers move to police departments, sheriff's offices, probation and parole, or federal agencies including the Bureau of Prisons, ICE, and the U.S. Marshals Service. Corrections experience is directly relevant and often preferred.

Other directions: security management, court services, juvenile justice, and social services. Some officers use tuition benefits to finish a degree in criminal justice or social work while employed.

The pension is the underrated outcome. A Wisconsin Retirement System career started at 20 is worth a great deal by 50, and very few paths in this guide offer that.
