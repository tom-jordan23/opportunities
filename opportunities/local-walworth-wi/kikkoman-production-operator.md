Two things make this worth serious attention beyond the short drive. First, the wage is competitive for entry-level work with no credential requirement. Second, food manufacturing is a genuinely stable industry — people keep buying soy sauce through recessions.

What it is not: a fast path to a portable credential. This is a job that pays well from day one and teaches you industrial work. Whether it leads anywhere depends on what you do inside the company.

## What You'll Do

Food production operator work in a brewing and bottling facility.

Depending on the assigned area: operating and tending production equipment, monitoring fermentation and processing stages, running filling and packaging lines, performing quality checks, sanitizing equipment, palletizing finished product, and documenting everything for food safety compliance.

Food manufacturing is heavily regulated, which shapes the day. There are documented procedures for nearly everything, sanitation standards are strict, and record-keeping is constant. This is good discipline to learn and tedious to perform.

The rhythm is shift work on a line. When the line runs, you keep it running. When it stops, you find out why. Sanitation is a large part of food production and it is wet, warm, and unglamorous work.

**A note on soy sauce specifically:** it is brewed, which means fermentation, which means the plant has a distinctive smell. Most people stop noticing. Ask about it on a plant tour.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+ for most industrial shift work — confirm with the employer. **High school diploma or equivalent is required** per reported postings. Background check and drug screen are standard in food manufacturing.
- **Skills Needed:** None stated. Mechanical aptitude and reliability matter more than anything on a résumé.
- **Physical Requirements:** Standing for full shifts, lifting, repetitive motion, working around moving equipment, wet floors, and temperature variation. Food plants require hairnets, hearing protection, and steel-toe boots.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time. **2nd and 3rd shift are the openings** — evenings and overnights. Manufacturing overtime is common.

## Training Provided

On the job, at the employer's expense, from day one.

Food manufacturing training covers plant-specific equipment operation, Good Manufacturing Practices, food safety and HACCP principles, sanitation procedures, lockout/tagout and machine safety, and quality documentation. Larger food companies typically have structured onboarding because regulators require documented training.

**The credential question is the weak point of this opportunity.** Production operator training is largely plant-specific and does not automatically produce a portable certificate. What you gain is verifiable industrial work history, food safety knowledge, and equipment experience — all of which employers value, but none of which is a license you carry.

**How to fix that from inside.** Ask about three things: forklift certification (portable and useful everywhere), internal maintenance apprenticeship or maintenance technician training, and tuition assistance. Food plants need maintenance technicians badly, and moving from operator to maintenance is the single best career move available inside a facility like this. See [Industrial Maintenance Apprentice](industrial-maintenance-apprentice.md).

## The Money Reality

At 40 hours the top of that band is roughly $47,000 annually before overtime. **Still not employer-confirmed — verify in person.** Ask whether the quoted rate includes a shift premium or is the base, and which bonus applies to the specific opening.
- **Housing:** Your own — and this is the real financial advantage. Living at home with a ten-minute commute and a $21.81 wage is a genuinely strong savings position. The commute cost here is close to nothing, which is not true of most options in this category.
- **Startup Costs:** Minimal. Steel-toe boots, roughly $80-150. The plant supplies other required equipment. No tuition, no certification.
- **Benefits:** Listings state "Full Benefits" without detail. Ask about health insurance, retirement match and vesting, paid time off, and the hiring bonus payout schedule.
- **Hidden Costs:** The hiring bonus almost certainly has conditions — typically paid in installments contingent on staying a set number of months. **Ask exactly when it is paid and what happens if you leave.** Also ask whether there is any repayment obligation attached to it.

## How to Get Started

1. **Drive to the plant and apply in person.** N1365 Six Corners Rd, Walworth. In-person application is reported to be accepted, and for a local plant it is the strongest move — you become a face rather than a file.
2. **Ask for the current pay rate and shift differential in writing.** Confirm whether $21.81 is base or includes a shift premium.
3. **Ask about the hiring bonus terms specifically:** when it is paid, in how many installments, and what happens if you leave before it vests.
4. **Ask whether you would be a Kikkoman employee or a staffing agency employee.** This is the single most important question. Direct hire and temp placement look identical in a job posting and are completely different jobs. If it is through an agency, ask what share of temps convert to permanent and after how long.
5. **Also apply through the Walworth County Job Center in Elkhorn**, which is reported to accept Kikkoman applications. While there, ask whether Kikkoman or any other local employer has an active OJT contract.
6. **Ask about forklift certification and maintenance training pathways.** This is how you turn a job into a career, and asking signals ambition.
7. **Request a plant tour or ask what the shift environment is like** — temperature, noise, smell, pace. A good employer will tell you honestly.

## The Hard Parts

**Second and third shift is the cost of entry, and it is a real cost.** Working evenings or overnights at 20 changes your social life, your sleep, and your health. It is manageable for a few years and harder over a decade. Go in knowing that days are something you earn with seniority.

**Line work is repetitive.** The same task, thousands of times a shift. Some people find a rhythm in it. Others find it unbearable, and there is no way to know which you are until you try.

**Sanitation duty.** Wet, warm, chemical-heavy cleaning work is part of food production and often falls to newer employees.

**Physical wear.** Standing on concrete, lifting, and repetitive motion accumulate into back, knee, and shoulder problems over years.

**Limited portable credential.** Unless you deliberately pursue forklift certification, maintenance training, or tuition assistance, five years here leaves you with work history rather than a qualification. That is the risk with all single-employer production work, and it is avoidable only by acting on it.

**Small-town employer dynamics.** In a village the size of Walworth, your employer is also your neighbor. That cuts both ways — reputation matters more, and a bad exit is more visible.

**The fermentation smell.** Minor, but real, and worth confirming you can live with.

## Where This Can Lead

Inside the company: line lead, quality technician, sanitation lead, shift supervisor, and — the valuable one — **maintenance technician**. Food plants run continuously and need mechanics, electricians, and controls technicians. Moving from operator to maintenance typically involves an internal apprenticeship or employer-supported technical college coursework, and it roughly changes your earning trajectory. If you take this job, treat that transition as the goal.

Outside the company: this region is dense with food and industrial manufacturers — Kerry and Frito-Lay in Beloit, Nestlé in Burlington, Birds Eye and Seneca operations, plus many smaller plants. Food manufacturing experience with a clean record transfers directly, and quality and food safety experience opens roles at any processor.

Longer term, industrial maintenance, quality assurance, and food safety management are all reachable without a four-year degree, and all pay substantially more than line work.
