# Arborist / Tree Care Groundworker

> **Status:** Draft
> **Last Verified:** Not yet fact-checked — drafted 2026-08-26
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Continuous, with spring and storm-season surges. Tree care companies hire groundworkers year-round.

## Overview

Wisconsin has a **registered apprenticeship for Arborist.** That fact alone puts this on the list, because it means paid, structured training with a defined wage progression exists for a trade most people have never considered as a career.

The entry position is **groundworker** — the person on the ground supporting the climber. It requires nothing to start. The established career path is that **groundworkers progress to climber, aerial lift operator, or plant health care technician**, which is a clear internal ladder from a job that takes anyone willing to work.

The professional credential is the **ISA Certified Arborist**, which requires **three or more years of full-time practical work experience in arboriculture**, or a relevant degree. That structure suits this category well: you work, you accumulate the experience, and the certification follows.

The work is outdoors, physical, and genuinely skilled at the higher levels. Climbing arborists who can rig and remove a large tree near a house are specialists, and they are paid like it.

## What You'll Do

**As a groundworker**, you support everything happening above you. You drag brush, feed the chipper, run ropes and rigging from the ground, operate the chipper and sometimes a stump grinder, set up traffic control and drop zones, clean the site, and keep the climber supplied. You are also the climber's safety backup, which means paying attention constantly.

Feeding a chipper is the defining task and the most dangerous one. It is repetitive, heavy, and demands respect for the machine every single time.

**As you progress**, you learn climbing — ascending with rope and saddle, positioning, making cuts safely at height, and rigging pieces down in a controlled way near buildings and power lines. This is genuinely technical work involving physics, rope systems, and judgment.

**Plant health care** is the other direction: diagnosing tree disease and pest problems, soil management, and applying treatments. It requires a pesticide applicator license and suits people who are more interested in biology than climbing.

Work is seasonal in rhythm — spring and summer are busiest, winter brings dormant pruning and storm work. **Storm response is where the money and the long hours are.** After a windstorm, tree crews work extended days for weeks.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma helpful but often not required. **Valid driver's license** is essential; a CDL is often required or trained for, since crews run chip trucks.
- **Skills Needed:** None for groundworker. Willingness to work hard outdoors is the actual qualification. Comfort with heights matters if you want to climb.
- **Physical Requirements:** **Among the most demanding in this category.** Dragging heavy brush all day, lifting logs, working in heat and cold, and for climbers, substantial upper body strength and endurance. This is genuinely hard physical work.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time, seasonal in intensity. Long days during storm response. Some winter slowdown depending on the employer.

## Training Provided

**On the job, and through a registered apprenticeship if you find a sponsor.**

**Employer training** is the norm. Tree care companies hire groundworkers with no experience and teach chipper operation, rigging, chainsaw use, and safety procedures. Climbing is taught to those who show aptitude and interest, usually after a season on the ground.

**Wisconsin Registered Apprenticeship — Arborist.** Listed by DWD, this formalizes the training with paid related instruction and a wage progression. **Whether any employer within this radius sponsors it is unverified** and is the key question to ask.

**Certifications along the way:** chainsaw safety, aerial lift operation, electrical hazard awareness (critical for anyone working near power lines), first aid and aerial rescue, and pesticide applicator licensing for plant health care work. Employers typically fund these because they are required for the work.

**ISA Certified Arborist** is the credential that changes your standing in the industry. It requires **three or more years of full-time practical arboriculture experience** or a relevant degree, plus passing the exam. The Wisconsin Arborist Association and Illinois Arborist Association both run exam preparation and continuing education.

The certification requires continuing education units to maintain, which is normal for the industry.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** **Not established for this region and it needs verification.** Groundworker pay in the Midwest generally starts modestly — comparable to other entry-level outdoor labor. Climbers earn substantially more, and ISA Certified Arborists with climbing skill are well paid. Storm work generates significant overtime. **Get real local figures before relying on this path.**
- **Housing:** Your own. Tree care companies operate across a service area, so you may travel to job sites within an hour of the shop.
- **Startup Costs:** Low. Employers supply saws, ropes, and safety equipment. You buy boots — good ones, because you will stand in them all day — and work clothing. Climbers eventually acquire personal saddles and gear, which is a later expense.
- **Benefits:** Varies widely. Larger regional companies and municipal forestry departments offer benefits. Small independent tree services often do not. **Municipal and utility forestry positions are the better-benefit version of this work.**
- **Hidden Costs:** Minimal at entry. ISA exam and membership fees later, often employer-reimbursed.

## Hiring Pattern

Continuous, with clear seasonality. Spring hiring is heaviest, and storm events create sudden demand.

**Employers in the radius:** independent tree care companies across Walworth, Rock, Racine, and Kenosha counties; regional and national firms including Davey Tree and Bartlett; **utility line clearance contractors** working for Alliant, We Energies, and ComEd — a large and steady employer category that trains extensively; and **municipal forestry departments** in Lake Geneva, Elkhorn, Delavan, Whitewater, Janesville, and Beloit.

**Utility line clearance is worth specific attention.** It is year-round, well-organized, trains thoroughly because the electrical hazards demand it, and it connects to the utility world generally. It is also more dangerous and more regimented than general tree care.

**Municipal forestry** is the stable version — city employment, benefits, Wisconsin Retirement System participation, and no storm-chasing.

## How to Get Started

1. **Apply directly to local tree care companies as a groundworker.** Most hire with no experience. Call rather than applying online — these are small businesses.
2. **Apply to utility line clearance contractors** working for Alliant, We Energies, and ComEd. Search for "line clearance" or "utility forestry." They train and they hire year-round.
3. **Check municipal forestry openings** in Lake Geneva, Elkhorn, Delavan, Whitewater, Janesville, and Beloit — these are city jobs with benefits and a pension.
4. **Ask about the Wisconsin Arborist registered apprenticeship** at every employer, and call the DWD Bureau of Apprenticeship Standards at **(414) 216-8417** to ask which employers sponsor it.
5. **Ask who pays for certifications** — chainsaw safety, aerial lift, pesticide applicator, and eventually the ISA exam.
6. **Ask about the path from ground to climbing**, and how long it typically takes. An employer with no answer is an employer with no ladder.
7. **Join the Wisconsin Arborist Association** and attend a local event. Small industry, and people hire people they have met.
8. **Get in shape before you start.** The first two weeks of dragging brush eliminate people who are not physically ready.

## The Hard Parts

**This is one of the most dangerous jobs in the country.** Tree care has consistently high fatality and serious injury rates. Chainsaws, chippers, falling limbs, falls from height, and electrocution near power lines are all genuine, regular hazards. Chipper incidents in particular are catastrophic when they occur. **Take every safety procedure seriously every time, and be willing to walk away from an employer who does not.**

**Electrical hazard is the specific killer.** Trees near power lines kill arborists every year. If an employer is casual about line clearance distances, leave.

**The physical demand at entry is brutal.** Dragging brush for ten hours is exhausting in a way that surprises people. Many quit in the first two weeks.

**Company quality varies enormously.** Reputable firms have strong safety cultures, proper equipment, and training. Others cut corners badly. In a trade this dangerous, the difference is life-altering. **Ask about safety training, equipment inspection, and whether they have an aerial rescue plan.**

**Weather exposure is total.** Heat, cold, rain, and mud. Storm work happens in the worst conditions.

**Seasonality can mean reduced winter hours** at smaller companies. Utility line clearance and municipal forestry are steadier.

**Pay at entry is modest** relative to the physical risk and difficulty. The pay improves substantially with climbing skill and certification, but the first year is hard work for ordinary wages.

**Hearing and vibration damage** from saws and chippers accumulate. Wear the protection.

## Where This Can Lead

A genuine skilled trade with a clear ladder and real self-employment potential.

Progression: groundworker to climber to crew leader to foreman. **ISA Certified Arborist** after three years of experience changes your professional standing and pay. Further ISA credentials include Board Certified Master Arborist, Tree Worker Climber Specialist, and Utility Specialist.

**Specializations:** plant health care and diagnostics, utility line clearance supervision, consulting arboriculture (tree risk assessment and expert testimony, which pays well and is easier on the body), and municipal urban forestry.

**Municipal city forester** positions are stable, pensioned, and reachable with certification and experience — a good long-term destination for someone who wants to stay local.

**Self-employment is very realistic in this trade.** A certified arborist with a truck, a chipper, and a reputation can build a strong business. This region has abundant mature trees, lake properties, and storm damage. Many tree care businesses started exactly this way. The capital requirement is real but not prohibitive, and demand is steady.

Adjacent: landscaping and grounds management, nursery work, and utility vegetation management.
