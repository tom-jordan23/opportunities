# Water / Wastewater Operator (Municipal)

> **Status:** Draft — pay VERIFIED 2026-08-26
> **Last Verified:** 2026-08-26 (pay verified against a published municipal salary schedule)
> **Fact Checker:** Pending
> **Hiring Pattern:** Occasional but predictable. Small municipalities hire when someone retires, and the workforce is aging. Openings are rarely advertised widely.

## Overview

This is the most overlooked good job in the region.

Every city and village runs a drinking water system and a wastewater treatment plant. Both are legally required to be run by state-certified operators. Both are staffed by people who, in most cases, were hired with no relevant background and trained by the municipality that hired them.

The Wisconsin DNR runs the certification program. The entry-level tier is literally called **Operator-in-Training** — the state built a credential for people who are still learning. Municipalities routinely hire someone as a laborer or utility worker, pay for the study materials and exams, and grow them into a certified operator.

The reasons this is underrated: nobody markets it, the job title sounds unglamorous, and the work happens where the public never looks. The reasons it is good: stable public employment, a pension, essential-service job security, and a certification that is portable to every municipality in the state.

The workforce is also old. A significant share of operators nationally are near retirement, and small towns are worried about replacing them. That is leverage for a young person willing to learn.

## What You'll Do

The work splits between two plants, and small municipalities often have staff who cover both.

**Water side.** You operate and monitor the wells, pumps, and treatment equipment that produce drinking water. You take samples and run lab tests for chlorine residual, bacteria, and other parameters. You maintain pumps, valves, hydrants, and meters. You respond to main breaks, which happen at inconvenient hours in January.

**Wastewater side.** You operate the treatment plant that processes sewage — screening, clarifiers, aeration basins, digesters, disinfection. You monitor biological processes, which is the genuinely interesting part: a treatment plant is a managed ecosystem of microorganisms, and keeping it healthy is a skill. You run lab tests, adjust chemical dosing, manage solids handling, and troubleshoot when the process goes off.

Both jobs involve preventive maintenance, meticulous regulatory record-keeping, and on-call rotation. There is real mechanical and electrical work — pumps, motors, blowers, controls.

Be plain about one thing: wastewater treatment smells, and you will work around sewage. Most operators stop noticing within weeks. Some never do.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. Valid driver's license — a CDL is often required or expected within a set period. Background check and drug screen for municipal employment.
- **Skills Needed:** None at hire for entry-level utility worker roles. Mechanical aptitude helps a lot. You need enough comfort with basic chemistry and math to pass the exams, and enough discipline to keep accurate records.
- **Physical Requirements:** Outdoor work in all weather. Confined space entry. Lifting, climbing ladders, working in and around water and machinery. On-call callouts at night and on weekends.
- **Time Commitment:** Full-time. Regular daytime hours plus on-call rotation. Emergencies do not respect the schedule.

## Training Provided

This is the part that makes it belong in this category.

**Wisconsin DNR Operator Certification.** For wastewater, the entry-level **Operator-in-Training** tier requires passing the **Basic General Wastewater Exam** plus a **Basic Subclass Exam**. Advancement to full operator requires documented experience — one year based on full-time employment at a treatment plant, or **1,000 hours of cumulative experience over a minimum of twelve months** for part-time operators. Municipal waterworks has a parallel certification structure.

Certifications renew every three years and require continuing education credits. Operators-in-training and basic wastewater treatment plant operators may apply up to three hours of supervisor or management training per three-year renewal period.

**Who pays.** Municipalities typically cover exam fees, study materials, and continuing education, and give you paid time to attend training. Confirm this with the specific employer, but it is the normal arrangement — the municipality needs certified staff to stay legal, so your certification is their problem as much as yours.

**Where the classes are.** The DNR maintains a training calendar and a list of pre-approved online providers. Wisconsin technical colleges — including Moraine Park and others in the system — run water and wastewater operator certification courses. Blackhawk Technical College and Gateway Technical College serve this region.

The Wisconsin Wastewater Operators' Association is an active professional body and a good place to meet people already doing the job.

| Water Laborer I / Wastewater Specialist I | J | **$25.98** | $30.53 | $35.07 |
| **Water Operator / Wastewater Operator** | K | **$27.94** | $32.82 | $37.71 |
| Lead Operator | M | $32.30 | $37.95 | $43.60 |
| Water Superintendent / Wastewater Superintendent | O | $37.34 | $43.88 | $50.42 |

  Annualized, a certified Water or Wastewater Operator at Whitewater runs **$58,107 at minimum to $78,444 at maximum**. A Superintendent runs $77,677 to $104,865.

  Two things worth noting. First, the entry rungs — Water Laborer and Wastewater Specialist — are **already paid $22.47 to $25.98 at minimum**, which is competitive with most other entry points in this category. Second, the ladder from laborer to operator to lead to superintendent is fully visible in a single public document, with a dollar figure attached to every rung. Very few careers show you the whole path that plainly.

  Whitewater is one city. Lake Geneva, Elkhorn, Delavan, and Walworth publish their own schedules and will differ. But the shape is now established rather than guessed.
- **Housing:** Your own.
- **Startup Costs:** Effectively zero for municipal hires. Work boots. The municipality typically pays exam and course fees.
- **Benefits:** Municipal employment in Wisconsin generally includes health insurance, paid leave, and participation in the **Wisconsin Retirement System**. On-call pay and overtime are usually part of the package.
- **Hidden Costs:** On-call duty constrains your life during rotation weeks. Continuing education takes time, usually paid.

## Hiring Pattern

Occasional, driven by retirements rather than growth. But there are many employers — every municipality in the radius runs both systems.

**Critically: these jobs are rarely advertised on job boards.** They are posted on the village or city website, sometimes on a physical bulletin board at the municipal building, and often filled by someone who already asked. Aggregators will not find them for you.

**Municipalities inside the radius** include Walworth, Fontana, Lake Geneva, Williams Bay, Elkhorn, Delavan, Darien, Sharon, Genoa City, East Troy, Whitewater, Burlington, Janesville, Beloit, plus Walworth County itself and the Illinois-side villages of Richmond, Harvard, and Genoa City's neighbors. That is a lot of separate employers, each with a small staff and an aging workforce.

## How to Get Started

1. **Read the DNR operator certification pages** for both waterworks and wastewater so you understand the tier structure before you talk to anyone.
2. **Make a list of every municipality within 30 minutes** and check each one's website employment page directly. Do not rely on Indeed. Check them every couple of weeks.
3. **Call the plants directly and ask to speak with the operator or public works director.** Say you are interested in becoming an operator, ask what they look for, and ask whether they expect any retirements. This single phone call is more effective than fifty online applications, because these are small crews who hire people they have met.
4. **Ask specifically: "Would you hire someone with no experience and train them toward certification?"** In this field the answer is frequently yes, and almost nobody asks.
5. **Apply for any public works or utility laborer opening**, even if it is not an operator title. That is the standard on-ramp.
6. **Ask whether they will pay for the Operator-in-Training exams.** Usually yes.
7. **Get your CDL if you can** — it is often required or preferred, and the Walworth County Job Center may be able to fund it. See [School Bus Driver](school-bus-driver-cdl.md) for another route to a paid CDL.
8. **Contact Blackhawk or Gateway Technical College** about water/wastewater certification courses, and ask whether WIOA funding applies.
9. **Join the Wisconsin Wastewater Operators' Association** and attend a regional meeting. Operators know who is hiring before it is posted.

## The Hard Parts

**On-call is the real cost.** When you are on rotation, a main break or a plant alarm at 2 a.m. is your problem. Winter water main breaks mean standing in a hole in freezing mud at night.

**The smell.** Wastewater treatment is not subtle. Most people adapt. Some do not.

**Confined spaces and genuine hazards.** Wet wells, digesters, and vaults carry risks of toxic gas, oxygen deficiency, and drowning. The safety procedures exist because people have died. Take them seriously every time, including the thousandth time.

**Regulatory paperwork is relentless.** Discharge monitoring reports, sampling logs, compliance records. Errors have legal consequences for the municipality.

**Small crews mean no anonymity.** In a two or three-person utility, you cannot hide from a coworker you do not get along with, and there is no other department to transfer to.

**The exams require real study.** Chemistry, biology, hydraulics, math, and regulation. People fail them. Studying is genuinely necessary.

**Openings are unpredictable.** You may need to wait, take a laborer job first, or commute farther than you would like to get in.

**Nobody will ever thank you.** The public notices water only when it fails.

## Where This Can Lead

The certification ladder is defined by the state and each rung raises your pay. Operator-in-Training leads to basic operator, then advanced subclasses, then chief operator or plant superintendent. A superintendent in a mid-sized Wisconsin city is a well-paid municipal management position, and the path there does not require a degree.

Portability is excellent. Wisconsin certification works at any municipality in the state, and the underlying skills transfer to industrial water treatment — food processing plants, breweries, and manufacturers all run pretreatment systems and pay well for people who understand them. Note that this radius contains many food processors, which makes that a real local option.

Adjacent moves: DNR or EPA regulatory work, engineering consulting firms that need operations experience, equipment and chemical vendors, and industrial maintenance.

Job security is close to absolute. Water and sewer service does not get outsourced, offshored, or automated away.
