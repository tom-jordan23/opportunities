# CNC Machinist / Tool & Die Apprentice

## Overview

Rockford, Illinois is a precision machining town. It has been one for over a century, and it supplies aerospace, defense, and industrial customers who need parts made to very tight tolerances. That cluster is roughly 50 minutes from Walworth, and it is one of the densest concentrations of skilled machining employers in the Midwest.

The trade has two related entry points. **CNC machine operator** is the accessible one — you run computer-controlled machines, load parts, and inspect output. **Tool and die maker or precision machinist** is the skilled apprenticeship, a multi-year program producing a craftsman who can make the tooling that makes the parts.

Reported details worth attention: CNC Set-Up Mills Operator positions in Rockford with a **$2,500 sign-on bonus**, and a **three-year paid schooling program** where apprentices attend classes at the Technology & Manufacturing Association while working, covering tool and die manufacturing, mold making, CNC programming, and metal fabrication, with **journeyman wages reaching $30 or more per hour** after graduation.

Sign-on bonuses for entry-level machine operators mean employers cannot find enough people. That is your leverage.

## What You'll Do

The two roles are genuinely different, and it is worth understanding which one you are applying for.

**CNC operator.** You run machines that are already programmed and set up. You load raw material, start the cycle, unload finished parts, measure them with calipers and micrometers, and watch for tool wear or drift. You keep the machine fed and the parts within tolerance. It is attentive, repetitive work, and it is how most people enter the trade.

**CNC set-up / machinist.** You prepare the machine for a job — installing tooling, setting work offsets, loading and proving out the program, making first-article parts and adjusting until they measure correctly. This requires real understanding of machining, materials, and metrology, and it pays considerably more than operating.

**Tool and die maker.** You make the dies, molds, fixtures, and gauges that production uses. This is the top of the craft, involves both CNC and manual machining, and requires reading complex drawings and holding tolerances measured in ten-thousandths of an inch.

Across all three: precision matters absolutely. A part that is two thousandths oversize is scrap, and in aerospace work scrap is expensive and traceable. The satisfaction of the trade is making something physical, exactly right, from a drawing.

The environment is a machine shop — noise, coolant mist, metal chips, oil smell, and concrete floors. Most shops run multiple shifts.

## Requirements

- **Age/Eligibility:** 18+. High school diploma or equivalent. Aerospace and defense work may require U.S. citizenship or permanent residency due to **ITAR export control rules** — a real and commonly overlooked gate in Rockford specifically. Background check and drug screen.
- **Skills Needed:** For operator roles, none required. **Basic math is genuinely necessary** — you will work with decimals, fractions, and tolerances constantly, and shop math is a real screening subject. Blueprint reading is taught. Mechanical aptitude and patience for precision matter more than speed.
- **Physical Requirements:** Standing for full shifts, lifting material and fixtures, repetitive motion. Good close vision for measurement work. Less physically punishing than construction, harder on feet and back than office work.
- **Time Commitment:** Operator roles start immediately. The reported apprenticeship path is **three years** of combined paid work and classroom instruction.

## Training Provided

**Multiple routes, and the paid ones are the point.**

**Employer on-the-job training for operator roles.** Many shops hire untrained operators and teach machine operation, measurement, and quality inspection internally. This is the fastest entry and requires nothing up front.

**The TMA apprenticeship route.** A reported **three-year paid schooling program** in which apprentices attend classes at the **Technology & Manufacturing Association** in Schaumburg, Illinois while employed, covering tool and die manufacturing, mold making, CNC programming, and metal fabrication. Reported outcome: **journeyman wages of $30+ per hour.** Note the location — Schaumburg is a significant drive from Walworth, so confirm class frequency and format before assuming it is workable.

**The local trade association.** The **Rock River Valley Tooling and Machining Association** in Rockford, **(815) 985-8582**, is the regional body for tool and die and precision machining apprenticeships. Call them — trade associations know which member shops are taking apprentices, and that information is not on any job board.

**Rock Valley College** in Rockford runs apprenticeship programs, and Wisconsin-side options include Gateway and Blackhawk Technical College for machine tool coursework.

Typical apprenticeship structure is three to four years combining on-the-job training with classroom instruction, requiring only a high school diploma, basic math, and an interest in machining to enter.

## The Money Reality

- **Pay:** Aggregated Illinois listings show machinist apprentice rates in a roughly **$16-$25/hour** range. CNC Set-Up Mills Operator positions in Rockford were reported with a **$2,500 sign-on bonus**, indicating competitive entry pay. The reported apprenticeship outcome is **journeyman wages of $30+/hour**. Experienced CNC programmers and tool and die makers earn more. **All figures are aggregated and need employer verification.**
- **Housing:** Your own. Rockford is roughly 50 minutes from Walworth — a real commute, about 90 miles round trip. Belvidere and Belvidere-area shops are slightly closer. **Factor the fuel cost honestly**; at that distance it meaningfully affects a $18/hour job and matters much less at $30.
- **Startup Costs:** Low to moderate. Steel-toe boots and safety glasses. **Machinists commonly build their own measuring tool set** — calipers, micrometers, indicators — which can run several hundred dollars over time. Some shops supply them; ask.
- **Benefits:** Employer-dependent. Established shops and larger manufacturers generally offer health insurance and retirement. Small job shops vary widely.
- **Hidden Costs:** The commute is the main one. Personal tooling. Apprenticeship classes in Schaumburg, if that route applies, add travel.

## Hiring Pattern

Active and continuous. Sign-on bonuses in entry-level operator postings are a straightforward signal that shops are short of people.

**The cluster:** Rockford and Belvidere hold a large number of machining and tooling shops, from small job shops to large aerospace suppliers. Beloit and Janesville have machining employers as well and are considerably closer to Walworth. Snap-on in Kenosha and Elkhorn does precision manufacturing.

**Start with the closer shops.** Beloit and Janesville are 30-40 minutes; Rockford is 50. If a Beloit shop will train you, take that over an identical job in Rockford.

## How to Get Started

1. **Call the Rock River Valley Tooling and Machining Association at (815) 985-8582.** Ask which member shops are taking apprentices or training operators. This is the fastest way to find openings that are never advertised.
2. **Apply to CNC operator openings even with no experience.** Look for "operator," "machine operator," or "production machinist" titles. Set-up and programming titles require experience; operator titles often do not.
3. **Search machining employers in Beloit and Janesville first**, before Rockford, purely on commute economics.
4. **Ask every shop the apprenticeship question:** "Do you sponsor a tool and die or machinist apprenticeship, and would you consider an apprentice with no experience?"
5. **Check ITAR eligibility if applying to aerospace suppliers.** Rockford has many, and citizenship or permanent residency requirements will eliminate some applicants regardless of aptitude. Know before you invest time.
6. **Brush up shop math now** — decimals, fractions, converting between them, and reading a micrometer. Free resources exist. Shops test this, and it is the most common screening filter.
7. **Ask about the TMA program** and confirm what attending classes in Schaumburg actually requires.
8. **Ask Rock Valley College** about their apprenticeship programs and employer connections.
9. **Ask the Walworth County Job Center** whether WIOA funds machine tool training — manufacturing is a named priority sector.

## The Hard Parts

**The commute to Rockford is a genuine drag.** Fifty minutes each way, ninety miles round trip, on winter roads, for a job that may start near $18/hour. Do the fuel math before accepting. Prioritize Beloit and Janesville shops.

**Operator work can be monotonous.** Load, cycle, unload, measure, repeat. Some shops run one part for weeks. The interesting work is in set-up and programming, and getting there takes time.

**Precision is unforgiving and public.** Scrapping an expensive part is a visible mistake, and in aerospace it comes with paperwork. Early on you will scrap something and feel terrible about it.

**Shop culture varies enormously.** Some shops are professional and invest in people. Others are old-school, gruff, and hard on new hires. Small job shops in particular are entirely dependent on the owner's temperament. Try to talk to current employees before accepting.

**Real physical hazards.** Rotating machinery, sharp chips, coolant on skin, and hearing damage. Machining injuries tend to be severe when they happen.

**Coolant and mist exposure.** Long-term skin and respiratory exposure is a genuine occupational health topic in this trade.

**ITAR requirements can disqualify you** from a large share of Rockford's best-paying work regardless of skill.

**The apprenticeship requires an employer, and that is the bottleneck.** As with all registered apprenticeships, you cannot enroll yourself.

**Manufacturing is cyclical.** Aerospace and industrial demand fluctuate, and machine shops lay off in downturns. Job shops are more exposed than large manufacturers.

## Where This Can Lead

This is a craft with a long ladder and real portability. Operator to set-up machinist to CNC programmer to tool and die maker, and each step is a substantial pay increase.

**NIMS credentials** are the portable proof of skill in this trade and are worth pursuing whether or not an employer requires them.

Higher-value destinations: **CNC programmer** and **CAM programmer** (moving from running machines to telling them what to do, which pays well and is easier on the body), **quality inspector and CMM programmer** (metrology is a specialized, well-paid niche), **tool and die maker** (the top of the craft), and **manufacturing engineering technician**.

Adjacent moves: applications engineer for machine tool builders, technical sales for tooling suppliers, and field service. Machine tool distributors actively recruit experienced machinists.

Also genuinely viable: **your own shop.** Machining is one of the few remaining trades where a skilled person with a used machine in a rented bay can build a business. It takes capital and years, but the path exists and people in this region have done it.
